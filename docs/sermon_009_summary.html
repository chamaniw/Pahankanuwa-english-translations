Sermon 009 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #9 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #9

Primary Theme: The subtle and inescapable nature of kamma and its retributions (vipāka), personal responsibility without a permanent soul (anattā), the necessity of constant self-reflection (paccavekkhaṇā), and supramundane liberation through the stilling of all preparations (sabba-saṅkhāra-samatha).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Mahākāla, a devout sotāpanna (Stream-enterer) lay disciple in Sāvatthī who regularly observed the eight Uposatha precepts and spent nights at the Jetavana monastery listening to Dhamma.

The Interaction: Early one morning, after spending the night listening to Dhamma at Jetavana, Mahākāla went to the monastery pond to wash his face.

The Disaster / Turning Point: A fleeing thief, pursued by angry homeowners, dropped a bundle of stolen goods right at Mahākāla’s feet to frame him. The pursuing crowd mistook the innocent lay disciple for a thief in disguise, beat him mercilessly, and killed him on the spot.

The Result / Resolution: When novice monks reported this seemingly undeserved tragic death to the Buddha, the Blessed One revealed that Mahākāla's death was entirely fitting given his past kamma. In a past life as a royal border guard, he had lusted after a traveler's beautiful wife, falsely framed the husband by hiding a stolen gem in his carriage, and had him executed. As a result, the guard suffered in the Avīci hell and was subsequently beaten to death in 100 lifetimes. The Buddha declared that one is one's own executioner through past evil deeds and uttered Dhammapada Verse 161.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Self-Responsibility Without a Permanent Soul (Anattā & Kamma)

The Dhammapada verse (Attanāva kataṃ pāpaṃ...) emphasizes that evil is done by oneself and born of oneself, crushing the foolish person just as a diamond cuts a rock-born gem. Skeptics mistakenly argue that if there is no permanent self (anattā), one cannot be held responsible for kamma. Ven. Nanananda clarifies that intention (cetanā) driven by ignorance (avijjā) and craving (taṇhā) creates the conceit of "I" and "mine." The uninterrupted continuous mind-stream (citta-santāna) carries this karmic momentum across lives, ensuring retribution returns to the doer without needing a permanent soul.

2. Correspondence of Action and Result (Kamma-sarikkhatā)

There is often a striking parallelism between an unwholesome act and its retribution. Just as the guard framed an innocent man using a hidden gem leading to his execution, Mahākāla's past kamma inclined the thief's mind to drop the stolen goods at his feet. Ven. Nanananda compares this returning property of kamma to an indigenous boomerang that returns to the thrower's hand.

3. Daily Reflection via the Ṭhāna Sutta (Abhiṇha-Paccavekkhaṇā)

To safeguard oneself against unwholesome conduct, everyone—lay or ordained—should constantly reflect on the fivefold formula from the Ṭhāna Sutta:

Kammassakomhi ("I am the owner of my kamma")

Kammadāyādo ("I am the heir to my kamma")

Kammayoni ("Kamma is the matrix/womb from which I am born")

Kammabandhu ("Kamma is my kinsman")

Kammapaṭisaraṇo ("Kamma is my refuge")

4. Acting as an Enemy to Oneself (Amitteneva Attanā)

Citing Dhammapada verse 66, the sermon emphasizes that foolish people move about acting as their own worst enemies (amitteneva attanā) by committing evil acts that produce bitter fruits (kaṭukapphala). In ultimate terms, the proximate cause of one's suffering or death is not external enemies, but one's own past unwholesome deeds.

5. The Tangled Ball of Saṁsāric Kamma and Negative *Anumodanā*

Saṁsāra becomes an entangled ball of thread when beings react to karmic retributions with hatred (dosa), generating further evil kamma. Furthermore, delusion leads people to participate in unwholesome deeds through negative rejoicing (anumodanā)—such as taking pleasure in an enemy's downfall or approving of corrupt acts—thereby accumulating unwholesome kamma mentally.

6. The Cosmic Struggle: Kusala-chanda vs. *Chanda-rāga*

The Dhamma recognizes two eternal universal factions: the wholesome/white side (devas) and the unwholesome/black side (asuras). Beings align with either side through their intentions. One must abandon destructive passion (chanda-rāga) and cultivate kusala-chanda—the wholesome desire and spiritual urgency for the Dhamma that impels one toward Nibbāna.

7. Virtue (Sīla) as Mental Tameness (Hīlākama)

Sīla represents bodily and verbal tameness (hīlākama), analogous to training a wild elephant or horse. Abstinence (virati) from unwholesome actions requires active mental effort and wisdom. Developing sīla leads to the happiness of blamelessness (anavajja-sukha) and freedom from remorse (avippaṭisāra), protecting the mind at the critical moment of death.

8. Insight Reflection (Paccavekkhaṇā) and Self-Examination

Just as one uses a mirror to examine physical appearance (as taught in the advice to Ven. Rāhula), one must use the mirror of wisdom (paccavekkhaṇā) to examine whether bodily, verbal, and mental acts bring harm to oneself, others, or both. Reviewing one's virtue (sīlāvajjanā) brings clarity and disciplines the mind.

9. Supramundane Liberation (Lokuttara-Vimutti) and Stilling of Fabrications (Sabba-Saṅkhāra-Samatha)

Ordinary meritorious acts prolong life in saṁsāra, but to escape the entanglement of kamma completely, one must still the momentum of mental fabrications (saṅkhāra). This is accomplished through the four stages of path-knowledge (magga-ñāṇa):

Stream-entry (Sotāpatti): Destroys lower realm kamma and limits rebirth to seven lifetimes.

Once-returning (Sakadāgāmitā): Reduces rebirth to a single return to the human realm.

Non-returning (Anāgāmitā): Severs lower fetters, leading to birth in the Pure Abodes (Suddhāvāsa).

Arahatship: Stills all karmic forces except present-life retributions (diṭṭhadhammavedanīya), realizing Nibbāna (sabba-saṅkhāra-samatha).

10. Cultivating Right Livelihood (Sammā-Ājīva) and Good Friendship (Kalyāṇa-Mittatā)

In an era marked by rapid material advancement and spiritual decline, practitioners must establish a disciplined daily routine, maintain Right Livelihood (sammā-ājīva), and practice vipassanā (insight meditation) to observe the momentary impermanence of the aggregates. Associated with good friends (kalyāṇa-mitta) is vital, as spiritual friendship encompasses the entirety of the holy life (sākalamevidaṃ brahmacariyaṃ).
