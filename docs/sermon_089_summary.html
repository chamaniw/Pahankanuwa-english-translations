Sermon 089 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #89 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #89

Primary Theme: The essential power, supreme value, and balancing of faith (saddhā) as the foundational seed (saddhā-bīja) driving the entire noble path—from spiritual urgency (saṃvega) and gradual practice to the maturing of wisdom (paññā) and the ultimate calming of all mental preparations (saṅkhārūpasamaṃ sukhaṃ).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Young Brahmin Vakkali (later Ven. Vakkali Thera) of the city of Sāvatthī.

The Interaction: Enchanted by the magnificent physical form of the Buddha while He was walking for alms, Vakkali entered the dispensation solely to gaze continuously at Him. The Buddha patiently endured this until Vakkali’s intellectual capacity developed, at which point He admonished him: "What is the use of gazing at this foul body? He who sees the Dhamma, sees me."

The Disaster / Turning Point: Upon entering the rainy season (vassa) at Rājagaha, the Buddha purposefully aroused spiritual urgency (saṃvega) in Ven. Vakkali by driving him away with the command, "Apehi, Vakkali" ("Depart, Vakkali!"). Overwhelmed by unbearable grief at the prospect of three months without seeing the Teacher, Vakkali climbed Vulture's Peak (Gijjhakaṭa) intending to commit suicide by jumping off the cliff.

The Result / Resolution: As Vakkali prepared to jump, the Buddha uttered Dhammapada verse 381, manifested His form through psychic power, and called him back ("Ehi"). Filled with intense rapture (pāmojja) and faith, Vakkali leaped into the air toward the Buddha. Supported by the Buddha's psychic power and his own past perfections (pāramī), Vakkali deeply realized the calming of preparations (saṅkhāra) while mid-air and attained Arahantship together with the analytical knowledges (paṭisambhidā). The Buddha subsequently declared him foremost among monks liberated through faith (saddhādhimiutta).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Faith as the Foundational Seed (Saddhā-bīja):

In the context of spiritual practice as agriculture, faith (saddhā) is the seed-paddy. Without clarity and devotion toward the Teacher, the necessary energy (viriya) to walk the path cannot arise.

2. Balancing the Spiritual Faculties (Indriya-samatta):

Liberation requires developing and balancing the five spiritual faculties (indriya): faith (saddhā), energy (viriya), mindfulness (sati), concentration (samādhi), and wisdom (paññā). Excessive faith without wisdom must be brought into equilibrium by the Teacher, just as the Buddha balanced Ven. Soṇa's energy or Ven. Vakkali's faith.

3. True Seeing of the Buddha through Dhamma (Dhamma-dassana):

Gazing at the Buddha's physical body (pūti-kāya) is insufficient. True seeing occurs when one sees the Dhamma ("Yo dhammaṃ passati so maṃ passati"). However, the living Buddha's physical form, postures, and conduct act as a living Dhamma book that calms and stills defilements rather than increasing them.

4. The Simile of the Elephant’s Trunk (Saddhā-hattho):

Faith is described as the trunk of the Dhamma-bodied elephant ("Saddhā-hattho mahānāgo"). Like an elephant’s trunk, faith is powerful enough to uproot trees and roll rocks (overcoming major obstacles), yet delicate enough to pick up a needle (directing faith precisely toward subtle wisdom).

5. Crossing the Flood by Faith (Saddhāya taratī oghaṃ):

As taught in the Āḷavaka Sutta, one crosses the rapid current of saṃsāric suffering (ogha) through faith. Faith provides the maximum commitment and determination of "death or crossing," enabling the practitioner to leap into the current rather than hesitating on the bank.

6. The Wise Cowherd and the Ford (Cūḷagopālaka Sutta):

The Buddha is the wise cowherd who knows the ford (panthota) to cross the river of saṃsāra safely. He guides disciples across systematically: Arahants lead, followed by Non-returners (anāgāmī), Once-returners (sakadāgāmī), Stream-enterers (sotāpanna), and lastly Dhamma-followers (dhammānusārī) and Faith-followers (saddhānusārī), represented by newborn calves crossing via the mother's call.

7. The Interconnected Chain of Spiritual Faculties:

The five spiritual faculties operate sequentially: faith (saddhā) generates energy (viriya); energy brings forth mindfulness (sati); sustained mindfulness becomes concentration (samādhi); and concentration gives birth to true, non-intellectualized wisdom (paññā).

8. Faith as Noble Wealth (Saddhā-dhana):

Faith is the chief among the noble wealths (ariya-dhana). Taking refuge (saraṇāgamana) deposits the Triple Gem into one's heart as an unstealable personal possession (māmaka). True refuge alone is sufficient to make one a lay disciple (upāsaka) or establish a novice in monkhood (sāmaṇera).

9. The Fourfold Declaration of a Faithful Disciple (Catuppada-veyyākaraṇa):

A genuine disciple possesses four traits:

The attitude: "The Blessed One is the Teacher, I am the disciple; the Blessed One knows, I do not know."

Viewing the dispensation as fertile and nourishing (ruhamaniyaṃ/ojavantaṃ).

Applying fourfold unyielding energy (caturaṅga-samannāgata-viriya): "Let only skin, sinews, and bones remain... I shall not relax energy."

Reaching one of two guaranteed fruits: Non-returning (anāgāmī) or Arahantship.

10. Process of Gradual Training (Anupubba-sikkhā, Kiriyā, Paṭipadā):

The path proceeds step-by-step: born of faith, one approaches the Teacher (saddhā-jāto upasaṅkamati), sits close (payirupāsana), listens, retains the Dhamma, examines its meaning, develops agreement with the teachings (dhamma-nijjhānakkhanti), generates zeal (chanda), exerts effort (viriya), balances the faculties (tulanā), and attains Nibbāna.

11. Recollection of the Buddha (Buddhānussati) and Taming Methods:

Recollecting the nine virtues of the Buddha (Iti pi so bhagavā...) acts like a water-clearing gem (udaka-prasādaka-māṇikya), temporarily freeing the mind from lust, hatred, and delusion (rāga, dosa, moha). As a charioteer of men to be tamed (purisadhamma-sārathī), the Buddha tames gentle (saṇhena), harsh (pharusena), or combined means—repressing (niggayha) and supporting (paggayha) so that only those with core heartwood (sāra) remain.

12. Nibbāna as the Stilling of Formations (Saṅkhārūpasama):

Preparations (saṅkhāra) are mental fabrications constructed within one's own mind. Ven. Vakkali experienced extreme swings between deep grief (when driven away) and intense joy (when invited back). By recognizing these constructs as a conflict arising within his own mind and letting go through faith transformed into wisdom, he realized Nibbāna—the complete calming and stilling of all preparations (saṅkhārūpasamaṃ sukhaṃ).
