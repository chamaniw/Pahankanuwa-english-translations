Sermon 131 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #131 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #131

Primary Theme: The inescapable law and supremacy of karma (kamma-niyāma / kamma-bala) over psychic power (iddhi-bala), illustrated through the martyrdom and Parinibbāna of Ven. Mahā Moggallāna; the grave consequences of corrupting the mind toward noble ones; and the three-stage practical path (sīla, samādhi, paññā) leading to ultimate extinguishment (nivīma / Nibbāna).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The Great Arahant Mahā Moggallāna (the Chief Disciple foremost in psychic power); in his past life, a devoted son manipulated by his wife to murder his blind parents; along with 500 Nigaṇṭha sectarians, 500 hired bandits, and King Ajātasattu.

The Interaction: Envious of the immense gain and honor accrued to the Buddha’s Dispensation due to Ven. Moggallāna’s psychic visits to the heavenly and hell realms, 500 Nigaṇṭha ascetics raised 1,000 gold coins (kahāpaṇas) to hire 500 murderous bandits to assassinate the Elder at Kālasilā.

The Disaster / Turning Point:

For two months, Ven. Moggallāna evaded the surrounding bandits using psychic powers—first slipping through a keyhole, then breaking through the roof's peak.

In the third month, perceiving that a grave past-life karma had caught up with him (having beaten his blind parents to death in a forest after being deceived by his wife), he ceased escaping. The bandits captured him, beat his body until his bones were crushed to the size of broken rice grains, and discarded him in a bush.

The Result / Resolution:

Using jhāna-veṭhuma (the "meditative wrap"), Ven. Moggallāna bound his crushed body together through meditative concentration (jhāna), flew through the air to pay final homage to the Buddha, delivered a sermon upon request, and returned to Kālasilā to enter final Parinibbāna.

King Ajātasattu’s spies caught the boasting bandits in a tavern, leading to the arrest of both the 500 bandits and the 500 Nigaṇṭhas. By royal decree, they were buried waist-deep in the palace yard, burned, and shredded with iron harrows.

In the Dhamma Hall, the Buddha uttered Dhammapada verses 137–140, explaining that while Moggallāna’s death was fitting for his past residual karma, his attackers suffered immediate public destruction and hellish rebirth for harboring malice against an uncorrupted noble one.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Supremacy of Kamma-Bala over Iddhi-Bala:

Psychic power (iddhi-bala), though magnificent, cannot override or subvert the ripening of past intentional actions (kamma-bala). Even a Chief Disciple foremost in psychic powers must undergo the physical exhaustion of residual unwholesome karma before final Parinibbāna.

2. The Ten Consequences of Harming Noble Ones (Dasannamaññataraṃ Ṭhānaṃ):

Based on Dhammapada 137–140, whoever inflicts violence or harbors hatred toward harmless, uncorrupted noble ones (adaṇḍesu appaduṭṭhesu) rapidly incurs one of ten severe retributions:

Severe, excruciating pain (vedanaṃ pharusaṃ)

Bodily maiming or destruction (sarīrassa ca bhedanaṃ)

Grave, incurable illness (garukaṃ vāpi ābādhaṃ)

Madness or mental instability (cittakkhepaṃ)

Persecution by royal authority (rājato vā upassaggaṃ)

Terrible, baseless slander (abbhakkhānaṃ dāruṇaṃ)

Ruin or loss of relatives (parikkhayañca ñātīnaṃ)

Complete destruction of wealth (bhogānañca pabhaṅguraṃ)

Destruction of dwellings by fire (aggi ḍahati pāvako)

Rebirth in hell upon the breakup of the body (kāyassa bhedā nirayaṃ)

3. Intention (Cetanā) and Consciousness (Viññāṇa) as a Karmic Matrix:

Karma is generated by intention (cetanāhaṃ bhikkhave kammaṃ vadāmi). Karmic seeds deposited in Viññāṇa interact with the physical elements (mahābhūta: earth, water, fire, air) inside and outside the individual, operating with computational precision across countless aeons until fully exhausted.

4. Entanglement in the Karmic Web (Kammacakra):

Samsara functions as a chaotic web of karmic entanglements. Beings with weak or uncultivated minds (such as the 500 bandits, the Nigaṇṭhas, and King Ajātasattu) become unwitting instruments used to exhaust the past karmic debts of others, thereby generating fresh, devastating unwholesome karma for themselves.

5. Re-definition of Vāsanā (Habitual Tendencies):

Ven. Nanananda clarifies that in Dhamma terminology, vāsanā does not mean "luck" or "fortune" (as used in modern Sinhala). It refers to the subtle residual traces, smells, or habitual tendencies left behind by past defilements—analogous to the lingering odor of fish or incense on a wrapping paper after the contents are removed. While the Buddha abandoned all defilements along with their residual tendencies (savāsanā sakala-kilesa pahīna), Arahants may retain harmless habitual traces (vāsanā), though these no longer produce future rebirth (bhava).

6. The Arahant’s Mind and the Sewing Machine Analogy:

The mind of an Arahant is likened to a sewing machine operating without an "under-thread" (yaṭi nūla). Because the underlying dormant tendencies (anusaya) have been eradicated through wisdom, no karmic "stitch" or knot can be formed to bind them to future existence, even when experiencing diṭṭhadhammavedanīya (karma to be felt in this very life).

7. Equanimity (Upekkhā) through the Realization of Non-Self (Anattā):

Ven. Moggallāna experienced his horrific death without harbor of hatred toward his killers because he recognized them merely as external conditions exhausting his past karma. Realizing that the body consists only of the four impersonal elements (mahābhūta) free from an "I" or "mine" (anattā) provides the ultimate mental strength of equanimity (upekkhā).

8. Analytical Breakdown of the Ratana Sutta Stanza:

The sermon unpacks Khīṇaṃ purāṇaṃ navaṃ natthi sambhavaṃ...:

Khīṇaṃ purāṇaṃ: Exhaustion of old past-life karma that sustained present physical existence.

Navaṃ natthi sambhavaṃ: Non-accumulation of new volitional karma.

Virattacittā āyatike bhavasmiṃ: Mind entirely detached from desire for future becoming (bhava-taṇhā).

Khīṇabījā: The seed of consciousness (viññāṇa-bīja) deprived of the moisture of craving (taṇhā), resulting in total extinguishment (nibbanti dhīrā yathāyaṃ padīpo) like a lamp out of oil.

9. Visible Symbols of Samsaric Torment:

The extreme, self-destructive suffering of hells and lower realms is reflected in observable natural phenomena: winged termites (meru) emerging en masse only to lose their wings and perish, or moths (palaṅgāṭiyan) blindly flying directly into a burning flame despite seeing others burn.

10. The Three Progressive Levels of Extinguishment (Nivīma):

Morality Level (Sīla-nivīma): Establishing internal immunity against unwholesome actions by undertaking training rules (e.g., nicca-sīla, ājīva-aṭṭhamaka-sīla) as a firm mental determination (adhiṭṭhāna) and active self-reflection (āvarjanā).

Concentration Level (Samādhi-nivīma): Suppressing the five mental hindrances (nīvaraṇa) using wholesome meditation subjects (kammaṭṭhāna—such as mettā, buddhānussati, asubha, or maraṇānussati), rendering the mind malleable and tranquil.

Wisdom Level (Paññā-nivīma): Penetrating the three characteristics—Impermanence (Anicca), Suffering (Dukkha), and Non-self (Anattā)—to sever mental clinging (upādāna) to the five aggregates (pañcakkhandha), extinguishing the fire of existence (bhava-ggi) permanently across the four stages of Awakening (Sotāpatti, Sakadāgāmi, Anāgāmi, Arahatta).
