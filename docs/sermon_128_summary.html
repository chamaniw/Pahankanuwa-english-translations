Sermon 128 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #128 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #128 (Pahankaṇuwa Sinhala Audio Dhamma Sermon No.65)

Primary Theme: Deconstructing the four perversions (vipallāsa)—specifically the false perception of beauty (subha-saññā) and selfhood in physical form (rūpa)—through insight into impermanence (anicca), foulness (asubha), element contemplation (dhātu-kammaṭṭhāna), and not-self (anattā) to bring about disenchantment (nibbidā), the cessation of existence (bhava-nirodha), and Nibbāna.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Princess Janapadakalyāṇī Rūpanandā, the betrothed of Prince Nanda (the Buddha’s younger half-brother). Known as 'Rūpanandā' due to her extraordinary bodily beauty.

The Interaction: After Prince Nanda, Prince Siddhattha, Prince Rāhula, and Mahāpajāpatī Gotamī were all ordained, Rūpanandā joined the Order of Bhikkhunīs, questioning the point of remaining at home alone. Reluctant to listen to the Buddha because he constantly pointed out the defects of form (rūpa), impermanence (anicca), suffering (dukkha), not-self (anattā), and foulness (asubha), she feared he would disparage her beauty. Eventually, hearing widespread praise of the Buddha, she decided to attend a sermon in disguise among the other nuns at Jetavana monastery.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Sensing her mind, the Buddha created a psychic vision (visible only to him and Rūpanandā) of a breathtaking 16-year-old maiden fanning him. Rūpanandā felt her pride humbled ("Am I not like a crow next to a swan?"). The Buddha then rapidly aged the created form in a fast-moving sequence: transforming her into a 20-year-old, then an old hag with broken teeth and grey hair relying on a walking staff, then showing her collapsing in severe illness wallowing in urine and excrement, then dying, and finally showing her corpse swelling and oozing pus and worms from nine apertures. Seeing this complete, instantaneous destruction of form filled Rūpanandā with intense urgency, fear of existence (bhava), disenchantment (nibbidā), and an acute understanding of impermanence (anicca) and its disadvantages (ādīnava).

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha uttered verses instructing her to see the diseased, putrid body as equal internally and externally (yathā idaṃ tathā etaṃ...), and to view the four elements as empty (dhātuyo suññato passa). Hearing this, she attained Stream-entry (sotāpatti-phala). The Buddha then uttered Dhammapada verse 150 ("Aṭṭhinaṃ nagaraṃ kataṃ..."), revealing the body as a city built of bones plastered with flesh and blood, carrying decay, death, pride, and detraction. Upon contemplating this verse, Rūpanandā attained Arahatship.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Four Perversions (Vipallāsa-dhamma): The world attempts to secure existence (bhava) by maintaining four upside-down views: regarding the impermanent (anicca) as permanent, the unsatisfactory (dukkha) as pleasant, not-self (anattā) as self, and the foul (asubha) as beautiful (subha).

2. The Skillful Means of the Buddha (Upāya-kosalla / Purisadamma-sārathī): Demonstrating his quality as the incomparable charioteer of men to be tamed, the Buddha used form itself to extract the "thorn" of pride in form. By accelerating eighty or ninety years of physical decay (vipariṇāma) into a few moments, he cleared the path for the insight cognitive process (vipassanā citta-vīthi).

3. Perception of Compactness (Ghana-saññā) vs. Identity: Form (rūpa) is taken by the world as proof of identity ("This is me"), whereas the Buddha reveals it as proof of change (aññathābhāva). The perception of compactness blinds people to the ongoing continuity of preparations (saṅkhāra), causing them to grasp at isolated stages of life.

4. The Body as a City of Bones (Aṭṭhinaṃ Nagaraṃ Kataṃ): The body is structured like a house—pillars of bones tied with sinews and plastered with flesh and blood. Embedded within it from the very moment of birth (jāti) are decay and death (jarāmaraṇa), conceit ("I am" / asmi-māna), and detraction (makkha). Concealing old age through artificial means is a delusion born of ignorance.

5. Element Meditation (Dhātu-kammaṭṭhāna) and Emptiness: The internal body consists merely of the four elements: earth (pathavī), water (āpo), fire (tejo), and wind (vāyo). Looking at elements as empty (dhātuyo suññato passa) means viewing them devoid of self-substance (attā) and identical to external elements.

6. Equanimity (Upekkhā) and Neutralizing Consciousness (Viññāṇa): Discriminative consciousness creates the artificial boundary between "internal" and "external." Contemplating internal and external elements as identical ("As is this, so is that") brings the mind to equanimity (upekkhā), dissolving this division and rendering the seed of consciousness hollow so it cannot germinate another birth.

7. The Mechanism of Existence (Paṭiccasamuppāda): The process of becoming is defined by the formula: Kammaṃ khettaṃ, viññāṇaṃ bījaṃ, taṇhā sneho (Karma is the field, consciousness is the seed, and craving is the moisture). Grasping (upādāna) acts as the roots. Nibbāna is achieved by drying up the moisture of craving (taṇhākkhaya).

8. Radical Attention (Yoniso Manasikāra) and Insight into Name-and-Form (Nāmarūpa-dhamma): Insight (vipassanā) requires turning attention inward (yoniso manasikāra) to observe that the mind grasping an object breaks up concurrently with the object itself. Realizing that both object and subject dissolve moment by moment eliminates the concept of a self (attā), revealing only a stream of preparations (saṅkhāra).

9. The Necessity of Disenchantment (Nibbidā): True vision of reality (yathābhūta-ñāṇadassana) necessarily produces disenchantment (nibbidā). One cannot see the truth while continuing to indulge in the perception of beauty (subha-saññā) or harboring desire for existence (bhava-chanda).

10. The Entire Five Aggregates are Suffering (Pañcupādānakkhandhā Dukkhā): Worldly attempts to end suffering while preserving existence (bhava) are futile. The Buddha declared that the entirety of the five aggregates of grasping—form (rūpa), feeling (vedanā), perception (saññā), preparations (saṅkhāra), and consciousness (viññāṇa)—is dukkha.

11. The Sharpness of Insight Wisdom (Vipassanā-paññā): Like a surgeon using a knife to cure a patient, the butcher simile in the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta illustrates how the practitioner uses the sharp scalpel of insight wisdom to dissect the body into elements (dhātu-manasikāra) to eradicate the notion of a living entity or self.

12. The Essence of the Dispensation (Sāsana): The unique core of the Buddha’s teaching is encapsulated in three words of eight syllables: Aniccaṃ, dukkhaṃ, anattā. Spiritual progress does not depend on academic degrees or theoretical knowledge, but on directly developing virtue (sīla), concentration (samādhi), and wisdom (paññā) to penetrate the Noble Eightfold Path.
