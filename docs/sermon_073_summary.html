Sermon 073 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #73 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #73 (Audio Sermon No. 110)

Primary Theme: Transcending the dualistic traps of the dear (piya) and undear (appiya) through wise attention (yoniso manasikāra), dismantling saṁsāric knots (gantha) tied by craving (taṇhā), and establishing equanimity (upekkhā) through systematic perceptual training (saññā-bhāvanā).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: A young beloved son from a wealthy family in Sāvatthī and his parents.

The Interaction: After listening to an appreciative sermon (anumodanā dāna) at home, the son desires to ordain. His parents forbid him out of intense maternal and paternal affection, taking turns guarding him.

The Disaster / Turning Point: The son tricks his mother (claiming he needs to use the toilet while she guards the door) and flees to ordain. Devastated, the father follows him into the order, and the mother subsequently ordains as a bhikkhunī. Instead of practicing for Nibbāna, all three convert the monastic life into a surrogate household domain, continuously meeting and conversing, unable to endure separation due to household love (gehasita pema).

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha admonishes them with Dhammapada verses 209–211 on the danger of piya and appiya. Unable to discipline themselves to the Buddha's word, all three disrobe and return home—failing to benefit from renunciation and later experiencing deep regret (pihayattānuyoginaṁ) upon seeing diligent monks attain Arahantship.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Impediment of Relatives and Lay Household Connections (Ñāti-palibodha & Kula-palibodha)

While relatives aid in lay life, saṁsāric affection (gehasita pema) becomes a severe obstacle (palibodha) in monastic life. The spiritual journey to Nibbāna must be traveled alone; bringing lay attachments into the dispensation converts the sacred domain back into a worldly household.

2. Unwise Attention vs. Wise Attention (Ayoniso Manasikāra vs. Yoniso Manasikāra)

Ayoge yuñjamattānaṁ refers to applying oneself to improper reflection (ayoniso manasikāra). True spiritual application (yoga) consists of looking inward to the origin (yoniso) where defilements arise, rather than looking outward. Misplacing attention leads to abandoning true spiritual good (atthaṁ hitvā) and grasping dear things (piyaggāhī).

3. The Trap of Dualistic Attachment: Dear (Piya) and Undear (Appiya)

Separation from the dear causes suffering (piyānaṁ adassanaṁ dukkhaṁ), while contact with the undear causes suffering (appiyānañca dassanaṁ). Liberation is found only when all distinction between dear and undear vanishes: "Ganthā tesaṁ na vijjanti, yesaṁ natthi piyāppiyaṁ" (For whom there is no dear or undear, no knots exist).

4. The Exemplary Equanimity of Venerable Saṅgāmajī

In contrast to the family of three, Arahant Saṅgāmajī demonstrated total equanimity (upekkhā) when his former wife brought their child to him while he meditated. Because he neither rejoiced upon her arrival nor grieved upon her departure (āyantiṁ nābhinandati, pakkamantiṁ na socati), the Buddha praised him as a true Brahmin.

5. Craving (Taṇhā) as the Seamstress and the Formation of Knots (Gantha)

Craving acts as a seamstress (sibbani), tying knots (gantha) through grasping (upādāna) onto things perceived as dear (piyarūpaṁ sātarūpaṁ). Knots are tied not only through lust (rāga) for the dear, but also through hatred (dvesa) toward the undear (exemplified by Serivāṇija’s vow of revenge in the Serivāṇija Jātaka).

6. Fivefold Perceptual Discipline (Saññā-Bhāvanā) for Neutralizing Mind States

The Buddha prescribed specific mental exercises to neutralize defilements:

Perception of the repulsive in the unrepulsive (appaṭikūle paṭikūlasaññī)—using asubha-saññā (foulness) and anicca-saññā (impermanence) to subdue lust (rāga).

Perception of the unrepulsive in the repulsive (paṭikūle appaṭikūlasaññī)—using mettā (loving-kindness) and dhātu-saññā (elements) to eradicate hatred (dvesa).

Perception of the repulsive in both; perception of the unrepulsive in both.

Transcending both to dwell with equanimity, mindful, and clearly comprehending (upekkhako vihareyya sato sampajāno).

7. Frequent Contemplation on Inevitable Separation (Abhiṇha-Paccavekkhaṇa)

As taught in the Abhiṇha-paccavekkhitabba Sutta, everyone must frequently reflect: "Sabbehi me piyehi manāpehi nānābhāvo vinābhāvo" ("I must undergo change and separation from all that is dear and pleasing"). This contemplation counteracts intense desire-and-lust (chandarāga) that drives unwholesome physical, verbal, and mental misconduct.

8. Critique of Worldly "Positive Thinking"

Ven. Nanananda critiques modern optimistic/positive thinking that covers up the reality of suffering (dukkha) and foulness (asubha). While the world seeks gratification (assāda), the Buddha directs attention to its grave danger (ādīnava). Ignoring bodily filth and impermanence fuels unrestrained desires, suicides, and crime.

9. The Primary Requirement of Satipaṭṭhāna: *Vineyya Loke Abhijjhādomanassaṁ*

To practice the Four Foundations of Mindfulness (Satipaṭṭhāna), one must first suspend extreme greed (abhijjhā) and displeasure (domanassa). Seeing things as they truly are (yathābhūta) requires a neutral mind withdrawn from the polarization of rāga and dvesa.

10. Deconstructing Self via Element Contemplation (Dhātu-Manasikāra)

The conflict between self and world is ultimately a battle between the internal grasped body (upādinna) and external ungrasped world (anupādinna). By contemplating both internal (ajjhatta) and external (bahiddhā) forms simply as the four primary elements (catudhātu: earth, water, fire, wind), the conceit "I am" (asmi-māna) is eradicated.

11. Dismantling Compactness (Ghana-Saññā) through Insight (Vipassanā)

Worldly delusion relies on the perception of compactness (ghana-saññā). Through vipassanā, one observes that what seems like a solid self or continuous breath is merely a rapid continuity (santati) of arisings and passings-away—a heap of momentary ripples with nothing worthy of being taken as "mine."
