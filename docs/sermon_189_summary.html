Sermon 189 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #189 Summary

Sermon #189 Summary

1. Overview

- Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #189 (Paticca Samuppada Sermon 7)

- Primary Theme: The deconstruction of conceptual imagining (maññanā) and self-conceit (attamāna) through the radical re-evaluation of linguistic usage and mind-objects (dhammā), culminating in the non-grasping of all phenomena—including Nibbāna itself.

---

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

- The Protagonist: Venerable Ānanda Thera (supplemented by the narrative of Venerable Khemaka Thera).

- The Interaction: On the eve of the First Buddhist Council, Venerable Mahā Kassapa and the 499 Arahants reminded Venerable Ānanda of his obligation to attain Arahanthood before the formal Dhamma and Vinaya recitals began.

- The Disaster / Turning Point: Driven by the desire to qualify for the Council, Ven. Ānanda spent the greater part of the night in intense walking meditation (kāyagatāsati). By framing Nibbāna as a target or "certificate" to be achieved, he unknowingly generated self-conceit, anxiety, and spiritual restlessness (dhammuddhacca), blocking his liberation. Exhausted in the last watch of the night, he decided to lie down. At the precise interim moment—a "posture-junction" where his feet were raised off the floor but his head had not yet touched the pillow—he recognized that he had made a "thing" (dhamma) out of Nibbāna.

- Result / Resolution: In that brief interval of letting go, Ven. Ānanda relinquished all mental preparations (saṅkhārā) and conceptual imaginings (maññanā). His mind was completely released from all influxes (āsavā), and he attained Arahanthood instantaneously between the postures of sitting and lying down.

---

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Falsity of Self-Conceit (*Anattani Attamāniṁ / Maññanā*)

Worldlings are bound to Saṁsāra because they project a self where no self exists (anattani attamāniṁ). This conceiving or imagining (maññanā) treats name-and-form (nāmarūpa) as real ("This is the truth"). However, whatever one conceives in a certain way inevitably turns otherwise (yena yena hi maññati tato taṁ hoti aññathā), revealing its inherent impermanence and falsity (mosadhamma).

2. The Ninefold Imagining & The Subtle Bondage of Māra (*Asmīti Maññitam*)

Existence in all three realms (Sensuous, Form, and Formless) is governed by imagining. The Buddha highlights a ninefold scheme of conceiving starting from the basic conceit "(I) am" (asmīti), extending to notions of future existence, non-existence, percipience, and non-percipience. The Buddha diagnoses all conceptual imagining as a disease (rogo), an abscess (gaṇḍo), and a barb (sallaṁ).

3. The Root Pattern of All Mind-Objects (*Mūlapariyāya Sutta*)

The Mūlapariyāya Sutta outlines the fundamental linguistic and psychological pattern by which phenomena present themselves to the mind. It representative list of 24 dhammā (ranging from the four primaries to Nibbāna) demonstrates how different levels of noble disciples interact with mental concepts.

4. Tripartite Analytical Framework of Mind-Attitudes

- The Worldling (*Puthujjana*): Perceives an object (sañjānāti), imagines the object (maññati), imagines in it, from it, thinks "it is mine" (meti maññati), and delights in it (abhinandati) because it is uncomprehended (apariññātaṁ).

- The Learner (*Sekha*): Understands the object through higher knowledge (abhijānāti). Because full comprehension is not yet complete, the learner trains with the prohibitive injunction: "Do not imagine..." (mā maññi).

- The Arahant / Buddha: Possesses higher knowledge and does not imagine (na maññati), nor delight in any concept, having fully comprehended it (pariññātaṁ).

5. Critique of Linguistic Delusion & The Grasping of Nibbāna

Language creates fictitious entities (dhammā) out of process-experiences. Commentators often stumble on the instruction not to delight in Nibbāna (nibbānaṁ na maññati / nābhinandati), mistaking it for non-Buddhist concepts of Nibbāna. Ven. Nanananda emphasizes that the Buddha attacked the delusion of linguistic usage as a whole: treating "Nibbāna" as a concrete entity or goal to be possessed (meti) perpetuates the ego-construct and blocks liberation.

6. The Role of Attention: Radical vs. Non-Radical (*Yoniso vs. Ayoniso-Manasikāra*)

Attention (manasikāra)—one of the five constituents of nāma—is the culprit behind Saṁsāra when operating as non-radical attention (ayoniso-manasikāra), projecting mind-objects outward like a mirage. Radical attention (yoniso-manasikāra) turns attention back to its source/origin (yoni), penetrating the mind-made nature (manomayā) of all phenomena (manopubbaṅgamā dhammā).

7. Restlessness Caused by Spiritual Striving (*Dhammuddhacca*)

Spiritual effort coupled with subtle grasping generates dhammuddhacca (restlessness brought on by attachment to Dhamma concepts). Over-exertion creates an artificial barrier to Nibbāna; true awakening occurs when all mental preparations (saṅkhārā) and subtle expectations drop away.

8. The Four Modes of Practice (*Yuganaddha Sutta*)

Venerable Ānanda identifies four avenues to Arahanthood:

- Insight preceded by calm (samathapubbaṅgamaṁ vipassanaṁ).

- Calm preceded by insight (vipassanāpubbaṅgamaṁ samathaṁ).

- Calm and insight yoked together in tandem (yuganaddhaṁ).

- A mind settled and concentrated after being overcome by Dhamma-restlessness (dhammuddhacca-viggahita-mānasaṁ), reflecting Ven. Ānanda’s own breakthrough experience.

9. Nibbāna as Unfalsifying and Beyond Delight (*Amosadhamma / Avedayita Sukha*)

Nibbāna is the unfalsifying truth (amosadhamma). Delighting (nandi) is the root of suffering (nandi dukkhassa mūlaṁ). Nibbāna is not a state of sensory gratification, but a bliss free from feeling (avedayita sukha), characterized by total equanimity beyond both joy and grief.

10. The Subtlety of Residual Conceit (*Asmi-Māna* & The Khemaka Sutta)

The transition from Non-Returnership (Anāgāmi) to Arahanthood requires the total eradication of the subtle residual conceit "(I) am" (asmi-māna). As illustrated in the Khemaka Sutta, even when a practitioner no longer identifies any aggregate as "self," a faint trace of "(I) am" lingers—like the scent of a lotus that cannot be localized to any single petal, or the residual odour of soap in a freshly washed cloth—which is only worn away by relentless contemplation of impermanence (udayabbaya).

11. The Instrumentality of the Dhamma (*Kullūpama Sutta*)

The Dhamma is compared to a raft: it is meant for crossing over the stream of existence, not for holding onto (nittharanatthāya no gahaṇatthāya). At the peak of insight, even good mind-objects (dhammā) must be relinquished, let alone unwholesome ones (adhammā).

12. The Middle Path of Pure Process (*Dhammāva Dhamme Abhisandenti*)

The Buddha’s Middle Path avoids absolute assertions of existence or non-existence. Spiritual practice is simply a causal sequence where "mere phenomena flow into other phenomena, mere phenomena fulfill other phenomena for the purpose of passing from the near shore to the farther shore" (dhammāva dhamme abhisandenti...), completely void of an underlying, permanent agent ("I").
