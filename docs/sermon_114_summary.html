Sermon 114 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #114 Summary

Here is a structured summary of Pahankanuwa Sermon #114 based on the text provided:

---

Sermon #114 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #114 (Sinhala Audio Sermon No. 167)

Primary Theme: The ancient ideal and practice of the sage (muni-paṭipadā), the radical necessity of seclusion (viveka) and non-association (asanthava), detachment from worldly fame and gains (yasa/mīḷha-sukha), and the urgent cultivation of wise attention (yoniso manasikāra) toward the destruction of birth (jāti-khaya).

---

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Ascetic Asita (the former royal priest of King Suddhodana) and his nephew, the youth Nālaka.

The Interaction: While dwelling in daytime concentration (divāvihāra) in the Tāvatiṃsa heaven, Ascetic Asita witnesses the deities celebrating hysterically. They inform him that the Bodhisatta has been born in Lumbini for the welfare of the world and will roll the Wheel of Dhamma at Isipatana. Asita descends to King Suddhodana’s palace, examines the newborn prince's bodily marks, and confirms his destiny as the Supreme Buddha.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Using his foresight, Asita realizes he will die before the Bodhisatta attains Buddhahood and preaches the Dhamma. Overcome by grief at missing this rare opportunity, he sheds tears. Reassuring the anxious king that no harm will befall the prince, Asita hurries to his nephew Nālaka, commanding him to renounce the world immediately and seek out the Buddha as soon as the Dhamma is proclaimed.

The Result / Resolution: One week after the preaching of the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, Ascetic Nālaka approaches the Buddha and requests the instruction on the sage's practice (moneyya-paṭipadā). The Buddha delivers the detailed code of the sage’s conduct and mental training, leading to complete spiritual fulfillment and providing a timeless template for those seeking liberation from Saṃsāra.

---

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Virtue of the Sage’s Practice (*Moneyya-Paṭipadā*):

The Buddha describes moneyya-paṭipadā as an exceptionally difficult and supreme practice. It demands radical sense-restraint, tranquility, humility, and maintaining an equal mind when encountering either insult or homage upon entering society for alms.

2. Moderation in Food and Physical Restraint:

The sage observes strict moderation in eating (ūnodaro mitāhāro appicchassa alolupo), leaving space for four to five mouthfuls of water, eating without greed, and avoiding dropping hints or expressing discontent with whatever alms-food is received.

3. The Quality of Unshakable Equanimity (*Tādī*):

The term tādī ("such" or "unshakable") signifies perfect equanimity (upekkhā). Whether receiving abundant alms or returning empty-handed, the sage remains unaffected—like a person who approaches a fruitless tree and turns back (rukkhaṃva upanivattati) without anger or blame.

4. The Razor’s Edge Simile (*Khuradhārūpamo Bhava*):

The Buddha urges the practitioner to be like a razor's edge. Just as one must be meticulously careful when licking honey smeared on a sharp razor so as not to cut the tongue (lippataṃ), a practitioner must consume food completely freed from the subtle craving for taste (rasa-taṇhā).

5. True Sagacity through Solitude and Silence (*Ekattaṃ Moneyyaṃ*):

The term muni (sage) implies both physical solitude (ekattaṃ) and silence coupled with wisdom (mona/mauna). True silence is not merely abstaining from speech, but internal seclusion characterized by the quietude of a mind freed from unwholesome applied thoughts (vitakka).

6. The Metaphor of Water and Noiseless Depth:

Drawing contrast through natural similes, the sermon highlights that shallow streams and small waterfalls flow noisily (saṇantā yanti kussobbhā), whereas deep oceans move silently (tuṇhī yāti mahodadhi). Deficit causes noise (yadūnakaṃ taṃ saṇati), just as a fool is noisy like a half-filled pot (aḍḍhakumbhūpamo bālo), while the wise sage remains calm like a full pool (rahado pūro va paṇḍito).

7. Homelessness and Non-Association (*Muni Sutta* Analysis):

Citing the Muni Sutta (Santhavāto bhayaṃ jātaṃ, niketā jāyate rajo...), Ven. Nanananda Thero emphasizes that fear and danger arise from social associations (santhava), and the dust of defilements arises from household life (niketa). The sage’s vision is defined by homelessness (aniketa) and non-attachment (asanthava).

8. Uprooting the Moisture of Craving (*Taṇhā-Sineha*):

Spiritual progress requires uprooting the root causes of birth (jāti) and refusing to supply the "moisture" of craving (taṇhā-sineha) to newly arising mental formations (saṅkhāra). An Arahant processes thoughts without feeding them with craving, thereby terminating the process of becoming (bhava).

9. The Transcendent Bliss of Seclusion vs. "Excrement-Pleasure" (*Mīḷhasukhaṃ*):

In the Icchānaṅgala Sutta, the Buddha explicitly rejects fame, retinues, and gains (yasa). He contrasts spiritual forms of happiness—the bliss of renunciation (nekkhamma-sukha), seclusion (paviveka-sukha), appeasement (upasama-sukha), and awakening (sambodhi-sukha)—with the gross pleasure derived from worldly gain and honor, which he starkly labels as "excrement-pleasure" (mīḷhasukhaṃ).

10. Supreme Goal Demands Supreme Practice (*Aggeneva Aggassa Patti Hoti*):

Liberation cannot be bought or achieved through weak effort (hīnena); the supreme state (agga) can only be reached through supreme practice (aggeneva). True adherence to the Dispensation means prioritizing practice (paṭipatti) over mere theoretical learning (pariyatti).

11. Brevity of Life and Spiritual Urgency (*Saṃvega*):

Human life is fleeting, likened to a dewdrop on a blade of grass or a line drawn on water. Given the extreme rarity of acquiring a human birth free from the eight unfortunate conditions (aṭṭha-akkhaṇa), practitioners must generate urgent spiritual anxiety (saṃvega) to practice the Dhamma while the opportunity exists.

12. Wise Reflection (*Yoniso Manasikāra*) vs. Distortions (*Vipallāsa*):

To overcome the cognitive distortions (vipallāsa) that view the impermanent as permanent, the painful as pleasant, the foul as beautiful, and non-self as self, one must continuously apply yoniso manasikāra by contemplating all conditioned phenomena through the lens of the Three Marks (tilakkhaṇa) and the Four Noble Truths.
