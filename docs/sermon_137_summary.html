Sermon 137 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #137 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #137 (Sinhala Audio Sermon No. 29)

Primary Theme: The risky, unstable "somersaults" of samsāra (samsāric wandering across high and low realms); the eradication of the underlying latent tendency of craving (taṇhā-anusaya) as the absolute prerequisite for ending continuous suffering (dukkha); and the vital necessity of applying insight meditation (vipassanā-bhāvanā) and wise attention (yoniso manasikāra) to internalize the Dhamma rather than treating reincarnation as mere intellectual curiosity.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: A young sow (piglet) seen by the Buddha in Rājagaha, who was previously a hen during the time of Kakusandha Buddha, later a royal princess, a jhāna-attaining ascetic born in the Brahma realm, subsequently reborn through several animal and human lives (in Burma, Benares, Suppāraka, Kāvīra, Anurādhapura), and finally born as Sumanā, daughter of the householder Sumana and wife of King Dutugemunu’s minister Lakuṇṭaka Atimbara in Sri Lanka.

The Interaction:

1. In Rājagaha: The Buddha smiles upon seeing a young sow while entering Rājagaha for alms. Ven. Ānanda asks the reason for the smile. The Buddha reveals her long past lineage—from a hen listening to meditation chanting, to a princess, to a Brahma deity, down to her current animal state as a sow.

2. In Sri Lanka (Final Life): An Arahant monk possessing supernormal powers (abhiññā) visits minister Lakuṇṭaka Atimbara's home for alms, recognizes Sumanā, and remarks to fellow monks: "Look, that young sow from the Buddha's time has now become the wife of the prime minister!" Sumanā overhears or learns of this remark.

The Disaster / Turning Point:

Past Life Crisis: As a hen listening to a practitioner chanting vipassanā kammaṭṭhāna, she was violently killed by a hawk that severed her neck. However, the wholesome potential (kusala-satti) of hearing the Dhamma propelled her into human and Brahma births. Yet, because the underlying latent craving (taṇhā-anusaya) was not destroyed, she eventually fell back down to an animal birth as a sow.

Final Life Awakening: Hearing the monk's statement triggers the recollection of past lives (jātissara-ñāṇa) in Sumanā. Seeing the terrifying, repetitive somersaults of samsāra, a powerful sense of spiritual urgency (saṁvega) arises in her. She obtains permission from her husband to renounce lay life and ordain in the Order of Nuns (Bhikkhunī Sāsana).

The Result / Resolution:

After her ordination, listening to the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta at Tissamāhāvihāra leads her to attain Stream-entry (sotāpatti-phala).

Returning to Mokkanta village, while listening to a monk preach the Āsivīsūpama Sutta, she attains full Arahantship.

Prior to her final passing away (parinibbāna), she recounts her complete past life history to the fourfold assembly as a testament to samsāric urgency, encouraging all to strive with diligence before entering parinibbāna.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Latent Craving as the Taproot of Suffering (Taṇhā-Anusaya & Dukkha)

Analysis: Citing Dhammapada verse 338, the Thero emphasizes the simile of the tree: just as a tree grows back if its taproot remains undamaged, suffering (dukkha) continually recurs as long as the latent underlying tendency of craving (taṇhā-anusaya) is not eradicated. Most people merely prune the branches of suffering without cutting the root.

2. The "Somersaults" of Samsāric Existence (Samsārik-Āvarta / Bhava)

Analysis: Samsāric journeying is not a stable progression; it is filled with risky, unpredictable somersaults. Attaining high states—such as royal birth or even the Brahma realms through jhāna—offers no permanent security. Without noble attainment, one can plunge back down to the lowest animal existences (tiracchāna-yoni) or hells (niraya).

3. Internalization of Samsāric Lessons (Yoniso Manasikāra vs. Curiosity)

Analysis: Hearing reincarnation stories or seeing suffering in others must not end in mere intellectual curiosity or entertainment. The practitioner must internalize (ehipassiko / attani Upaneyyako) such stories, thinking: "Could my life have passed in this manner too?" This reflection generates genuine spiritual urgency (saṁvega).

4. The Fruitfulness of Attentive Hearing (Dhammasavaṇa-ānisaṁsa)

Analysis: Even an animal (the hen) that listened to vipassanā chanting without conceptual comprehension experienced composure and faith (saddhā). This created a wholesome karmic potential (kusala-satti) that later conditioned her to develop the perception of a worm-infested corpse (pulavaka-saññā) upon seeing worms in a toilet.

5. Wisdom (Paññā) as the Tool to Sever the Creeper of Craving (Taṇhā-Latā)

Analysis: Desires sprout and spread like invasive vines (latā). The Buddha calls on practitioners to sever this root with the weapon of insight-wisdom (vipassanā-paññā). Insight meditation is the unique process capable of digging down to cut the latency layer (anusaya).

6. The Illusion of Motion: Samsāra as Spinning in Place (Samsaraṇa)

Analysis: From one angle, samsāra seems like a vast linear journey, but functionally it is samsaraṇa—spinning around a single circular loop, like a child riding a merry-go-round. Regardless of whether one becomes a king, deity, or animal, life is a repetitive cycle of birth (jāti), aging (jarā), sickness, and death (maraṇa).

7. The Concealment of Suffering through Postures and Habits

Analysis: The inherent painfulness (dukkha-lakkhana) of existence is routinely masked by changing postures (sitting, turning, standing) and satisfying basic biological drives (eating when hungry). Civilizational comforts and personal conceit (māna) cover up the underlying friction of the body and mind, preventing ordinary people from seeing dukkha.

8. Understanding the Aggregates as a Heap of Fabrications (Saṅkhāra-Puñja)

Analysis: What we perceive as "I" or "my life" is merely a mass of five aggregates (pañcakkhandha): form, feeling, perception, volitional preparations (saṅkhāra), and consciousness. These fabrications are like continuously running mental paintings. Recognizing that there is no true self or solid state within these changing fabrications brings about the cessation of becoming (bhava-nirodha).

9. The Mechanism of Re-becoming (Bhava-Netti and Upādāna)

Analysis: At the moment of death, unfulfilled expectations leave life feeling like a "blank," prompting craving (taṇhā) to grasp (upādāna) a new birth. Craving acts as the conduit of becoming (bhava-netti), pulling the consciousness into new forms of birth according to kamma, where the illusion of "I" and "mine" is immediately re-established.

10. Radical Attention (Yoniso Manasikāra) vs. Perverse Perceptions (Vipallāsa)

Analysis: The world operates under distorted perceptions: permanence (nicca-saññā), beauty (subha-saññā), and self (atta-saññā). The practitioner uses any object—whether a decaying corpse, a toilet, or an old garment—to cultivate their opposites: impermanence (anicca), foulness (asubha), suffering (dukkha), and non-self (anattā).

11. Extracting the Taste of Liberation (Vimutti-Rasa) over Curiosity

Analysis: Every teaching of the Buddha contains the taste of liberation (vimutti-rasa). Modern interest in reincarnation research often focuses on publicity, titles, and curiosity, which only expands becoming (bhava). Dhamma research must be directed toward the Noble Quest (ariya-pariyesana) to achieve true freedom.

12. The True Vitality of the Buddha Sāsana

Analysis: The Buddha's Dispensation (Sāsana) does not consist of external buildings, decorations, or state holidays. It lives exclusively through the internal practice of virtue (sīla), concentration (samādhi), and wisdom (paññā) within the hearts of practitioners. The culmination of learning (pariyatti) and practice (paṭipatti) is direct realization (paṭivedha) of Nibbāna.
