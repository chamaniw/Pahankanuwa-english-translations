Sermon 018 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #18 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #18

Primary Theme: Deconstructing the cognitive illusion of existence through extreme sense-restraint (indriya-saṃvara-sīla) and bare awareness (diṭṭhe diṭṭhamattaṃ), demonstrating how penetrating the true nature of thought and consciousness leads directly to immediate liberation (Nibbāna).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Bāhiya Dārucīriya, an ascetic who wore garments made of tree bark (valkalā) after losing his clothes in a shipwreck at Suppāraka. Deluded by public veneration into believing he was an Arahant, he was redirected by a deity who was a past relative.

The Interaction: Upon learning from the deity that the fully Enlightened Buddha resided at Jetavana in Sāvatthi, Bāhiya traveled a vast distance across India with intense urgency. Finding the Buddha walking for alms in Sāvatthi, Bāhiya threw himself at the Buddha's feet and entreated Him three times for a Dhamma teaching, despite initial refusals due to the alms-round.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Acknowledging the extreme unpredictability of life—that either he or the Buddha could die at any moment—Bāhiya desperately begged for even a brief summary of the Dhamma. Recognizing his mature spiritual faculties, the Buddha delivered the concise instruction on bare sensory experience.

The Result / Resolution: Bāhiya comprehended the hint instantly and attained Arahantship on the spot. Shortly after, he was gored to death by a cow due to past kamma and attained final Nibbāna (pariNibbāna). The Buddha declared him foremost among disciples who possess swift direct knowledge (khippābhiññā), attributing this capacity to his past life during Kassapa Buddha's era, when he meditated unto death on a mountain cliff.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Restraint of the Sense Faculties (Indriya-Saṃvara-Sīla):

The Buddha presents the deepest level of sense-restraint as the direct path to liberation: training the mind so that in the seen there is only the seen (diṭṭhe diṭṭhamattaṃ), in the heard only the heard (sute sutamattaṃ), in the sensed only the sensed (mute mutamattaṃ), and in the cognized only the cognized (viññāte viññātamattaṃ).

2. Deconstruction of Sensory "Things" (Diṭṭha, Suta, Muta, Viññāta):

Ordinary worldlings solidify sensory impressions into permanent "things" or entities. Ven. Nanananda illustrates that visual forms are like a rainbow or a mirage, sounds are constructed rhythms striking the eardrum, physical/tactual sensations (muta) deceive through conditioned memory (e.g., comparing honey to sugar), and consciousness (viññāṇa) merely categorizes and divides raw input into dualities like pleasant and painful.

3. Dissolution of Ego-Identification (Na Tena):

By remaining at the limit of bare sensory experience without prolifically expanding upon it through craving (taṇhā), conceit (māna), or views (diṭṭhi), one does not construct an identity or define oneself "by it" (na tena).

4. Prevention of Mental Entanglement (Na Tattha):

When a person does not measure or identify themselves "by" sensory objects (na tena), they do not slip inside or become trapped "therein" (na tattha). They refrain from residing inside the sights, sounds, sensations, or thoughts they experience.

5. Transcending Spatial and Relational Delusions (Na Idha, Na Huraṃ, Na Ubhayamantare):

When one is not "therein," the fundamental dualistic concepts of existence collapse: one is neither "here" (this self / this world), nor "there" (an external world / the next world), nor "in between the two" (na ubhayamantare). This absolute transcendence of spatial and conceptual boundaries constitutes the true end of suffering (esevanto dukkhassa).

6. The Buddha’s Skillful Means (Upāya-Kosalla) in Delaying Instruction:

The Buddha twice postponed Bāhiya's request to teach while on alms-round. This served a dual purpose: it allowed Bāhiya’s physical fatigue and hyper-excitement to subside, while simultaneously sharpening his spiritual urgency (saṃvega) to receive a concise, deep hint that he would absorb with total concentration.

7. Past Life-Sacrificing Effort as the Cause for Swift Knowledge (Khippābhiññā):

Bāhiya's immediate realization was rooted in past practice during the declining era of Kassapa Buddha. Seven monks climbed a cliff, destroyed the ladder, and committed to meditate until realization or death. The intense morality (sīla) and commitment of the five who died as worldlings generated the power for them to become swift-realizers (khippābhiññā) under Gotama Buddha.

8. Thought (Citta / Saṅkhāra) as the Bond of Māra:

True escape from saṃsāra is not achieved by analyzing external objects scientifically, but by penetrating the nature of thought itself. Thought is both the instrument of delusion and the medium through which liberation is understood; breaking the bond of Māra means unmasking how the mind constructs reality.

9. The Simile of the Deer Chasing the Mirage:

Unenlightened beings spend saṃsāra running between "here" (present sensory indulgence/meritorious deeds for future rewards) and "there" (future existences), much like a deer running back and forth after a shimmering mirage of water, remaining perpetually unsatisfied and exhausted.

10. Penetrative Wisdom (Paññā) vs. Academic Knowledge (Ñāṇa):

Liberation cannot be attained through accumulated facts, degrees, or worldly knowledge, which often weigh down the individual. Wisdom is compared to a diamond gem (vajra-māṇikya) that cuts through all conceptual entanglements, enabling the experiential vision of things as they really are (yathābhūta-ñāṇadassana).

11. Saṃsāric Existence as a Delusory Dream (Supina):

All past experiences are like last night's dream, future aspirations are like tonight's unformed dream, and present active engagement is merely a day-dream. Consciousness (viññāṇa) acts as a magician (māyā), presenting non-existent solid entities as real.

12. Nibbāna as Cessation (Nibbuti) vs. Worldly Existence (Pavatti):

Worldly religions and philosophies focus on perpetuating existence (pavatti). The Buddha uniquely teaches cessation (navatma)—the complete cooling down and stopping of saṃsāric proliferation, bringing an end to the cycle of birth and death.
