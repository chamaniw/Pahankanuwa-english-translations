Sermon 092 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #92 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #92

Primary Theme: Defining true prosperity through Righteous Conduct (Dhamma-cariyā) and the Seven Noble Treasures (Sapta Ariya-Dhana), illustrating how inner spiritual wealth supersedes unrighteous worldly gain (adhamma-samiddhi) and leads to ultimate liberation (Nibbāna).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The lay disciple (upāsaka) Dhammika of Sāvatthi, his wife (purāṇa-dutiyikā), and their son.

The Interaction: Dhammika lives a virtuous lay life according to the Dhamma. Experiencing weariness (nibbidā) toward household existence, he informs his wife of his desire to renounce the world. She requests that he postpone his ordination until their unborn child is born and can walk.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Recognizing that household obligations are unending and that waiting for permission would indefinitely delay his spiritual liberation, Dhammika departs to ordain without his wife's final consent.

The Result / Resolution: Dhammika meditates diligently and attains Arahatship. Returning home as a monk, he preaches the Dhamma to his son, who ordains and becomes an Arahat. Seeing both her husband and son fully liberated, the wife also ordains and attains Arahatship. The Buddha uses this occasion to utter Dhammapada 84, declaring that a true wise person (paṇḍita) does not desire wealth, children, or kingdoms through unrighteous means (adhamma).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Righteous Conduct and the Middle Path (Dhamma-cariyā & Sama-cariyā):

True progress is rooted in loving the Dhamma (dhammakāmo bhavaṃ hoti), whereas hatred of the Dhamma leads to downfall (dhammadessī parābhavo). Wholesome action (kusala) is defined by the far-sighted principle taught to Ven. Rāhula: actions that do not lead to the downfall of oneself, others, or both.

2. The Five Precepts (Pañca-sīla) as the Foundation of Lay Duty (Gahaṭṭha-vatta):

The minimal standard for lay life requires avoiding killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, lying, and intoxicants. Intoxicants are categorized as an apuññāyatana—a source of generating demerit (an "evil-manufacturing institution") that causes delusion, madness (bālatantant), and downfall in both worlds.

3. Abstinence from Wrong Livelihood (Micchā-ājīva):

Lay disciples must abstain from five unrighteous trades: weapons (sattha), living beings/slaves (satta), meat (maṃsa), intoxicants (majja), and poison (visa). Pursuing prosperity through evil means triggers the provocation of internal and external physical elements (cattāro mahābhūtā), resulting in sickness, natural disasters, and Samsaric ruin.

4. Analysis of the Three Types of Persons (Andha, Ekacakkhu, Dvicakkhu):

Andha (The Blind): Lacks both economic skill and spiritual knowledge (kusala/akusala).

Ekacakkhu (The One-Eyed): Possesses economic expertise to acquire wealth (even through unrighteous, competitive means) but remains spiritually blind to moral cause and effect.

Dvicakkhu (The Two-Eyed): Acquire wealth righteously through hard work and sweat, while simultaneously cultivating moral discernment, generosity, and spiritual progress.

5. Simile of Wealth Earned Righteously:

Wealth accumulated righteously through honest effort is compared to honey gathered by bees or an anthill built grain by grain—it is sweet, stable, and imperishable, growing gradually without exploiting others.

6. Dual Welfare in Lay Life (Dīghajānu Sutta):

Progress in the present world requires persistent effort (uṭṭhāna-sampadā), protection of wealth (ārakkha-sampadā), good friendship (kalyāṇamittatā), and balanced livelihood (sama-jīvitā). Progress for the future world requires faith (saddhā-sampadā), virtue (sīla-sampadā), generosity (cāga-sampadā), and wisdom (paññā-sampadā).

7. The World-Protecting Qualities (Loka-pālaka-dhamma):

Moral shame (hiri)—the mind's instinctual shrinking away from evil—and moral dread (ottappa)—fear of the consequences of sin—act as the true internal guardians of society, far more effective than external military or police control.

8. True Relinquishment (Cāga) vs. External Giving (Dāna):

Cāga is the internal removal of the stain of stinginess (macchera-mala) and the mental strength to let go of attachment (taṇhā). While physical giving (dāna) can occur while holding on internally, true cāga provides psychological immunity during sudden worldly loss and prepares the mind to abandon attachment to the five aggregates.

9. The Seven Noble Treasures (Sapta Ariya-Dhana):

The ultimate wealth consists of Faith (saddhā), Virtue (sīla), Moral Shame (hiri), Moral Dread (ottappa), Learning (suta), Generosity (cāga), and Wisdom (paññā). Unlike material riches—which are vulnerable to fire, floods, kings, thieves, and unloved heirs—these noble treasures are imperishable, unstealable (ajeyyo), and internal.

10. Insight Wisdom into Arising and Passing Away (Udayabbayagāminī Paññā):

The highest treasure is wisdom (narānaṃ ratanaṃ), which purifies the mind by contemplating the impermanence of the five aggregates of grasping (pañcupādānakkhandha): seeing form as a lump of foam (rūpa), feeling as a bubble (vedanā), perception as a mirage (saññā), preparations as a banana tree trunk (saṅkhāra), and consciousness as an illusion (viññāṇa).

11. The Ghee Jar Simile and Universal Loneliness:

A person who has developed the mind through virtue and wisdom is like a jar of ghee submerged in water: when the physical jar (body) breaks and sinks to the bottom, the ghee (trained mind) naturally rises upward. Realizing that family members are merely temporary companions meeting at a resting-hall (ambalama) and that one owns only one's kamma (kammasako'mhi), a wise person naturally inclines toward renunciation (nekkhamma) and Nibbāna.
