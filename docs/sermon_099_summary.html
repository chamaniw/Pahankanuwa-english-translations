Sermon 099 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #99 Summary

Sermon #99 Summary

1. Overview

- Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #99

- Primary Theme: The spiritual efficacy of paying homage to elders and chanting Paritta to enhance lifespan (āyu), beauty (vaṇṇa), happiness (sukha), and strength (bala), contrasted with the ultimate necessity of developing wisdom (paññā) and walking the Noble Eightfold Path to achieve release from Saṃsāra.

---

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

- The Protagonist: The child Dīghāyu (later known as Prince Āyuvaḍḍhana), his parents (two former brahmins who had practiced ascetic holy life for forty-eight years before one returned to lay life to preserve his lineage), and an old ascetic friend.

- The Interaction: The parents bring their young child to pay respects to their former ascetic friend. When the parents bow, the ascetic says, "May you live long!" ("Dīghāyu hotu"), but when the child bows, the ascetic remains completely silent.

- The Disaster / Turning Point: The ascetic reveals that the child faces a life-threatening danger and will die in seven days at the hands of a yakkha named Avaruddhaka, who had obtained a boon from King Vessavaṇa. Admitting he has no cure, the ascetic directs the parents to seek help from the Ascetic Gotama (the Buddha).

- The Result / Resolution: The Buddha instructs the parents to erect a pavilion in front of their home and seat the child in the center surrounded by monks chanting Paritta continuously for seven days. On the seventh day, the Buddha and a vast assembly of powerful deities arrive, forcing the yakkha to retreat far into the background. The fatal hour passes, and the Buddha reveals the child will now live for one hundred and twenty years. As an adult, Prince Āyuvaḍḍhana leads five hundred lay followers in Dhamma practice, prompting the Buddha to utter Dhammapada stanza 109.

---

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Four Desired Qualities (*Āyu, Vaṇṇa, Sukha, Bala*):

According to Dhammapada 109 (Abhivādanasīlissa...), those who are habituated to bowing down (abhivādanasīla) and constantly honoring elders (vuḍḍhāpacāyi) increase four blessings: lifespan (āyu), beauty (vaṇṇa), happiness (sukha), and strength (bala).

2. The Mechanism and Power of *Paritta* (*Paritrāṇa*):

Paritta (or pirit) means protection from all sides. It derives its cosmic protective power from an assertion of truth (saccakiriya) regarding the genuine virtues of the Triple Gem (saccena suvatthi hotu), which dispels invisible non-human afflictions like yakkhas.

3. Monastic Order of Seniority vs. Lay Order:

Unlike lay society, which often measures seniority by age, wealth, or status, the Dispensation (Sāsana) determines seniority strictly by time elapsed since higher ordination (upasampadā) measured in rains retreats (vassa). This allows even a mother to bow to her newly ordained seven-year-old child.

4. Proper Ministering to Parents by Monastics:

When an ordained child receives homage from his mother, he bestows blessings rather than bowing back. The supreme way a monastic ministers to parents is by helping them grow in faith (saddhā), virtue (sīla), generosity (cāga), and wisdom (paññā).

5. The *Tittira Jātaka* and the *Tittiriya Brahmacariya*:

Spurred by the group-of-six monks (chabbaggiya) selfishly seizing lodgings at Jetavana and leaving Ven. Sāriputta without a room, the Buddha relates how a partridge, a monkey, and an elephant determined their relative ages by their memories of a banyan tree. Recognizing the partridge as the eldest, the larger animals honored his counsel and kept the five precepts (pañcasīla), establishing the holy life of the partridge (Tittiriya Brahmacariya), which leads to rebirth in happy realms (sugati).

6. Brahmin Life Stages (*Āśrama*) vs. Buddhist Renunciation (*Nekkhamma*):

Brahminism divides life into four stages (āśrama) to balance sensual family lineage (praveṇiya) with sage practice. The Buddha rejects this "having it both ways" approach (illustrated by the story of Sudinna Kalandakaputta), viewing the world as a hospital where renunciation (nekkhamma) should be undertaken immediately upon realization, refuting accusations of clan destruction (kulupacchedāya).

7. Wisdom (*Paññā*) as the Supreme Gem:

While lifespan, beauty, happiness, and strength support physical existence, wisdom is the supreme gem for human beings (paññā narānaṃ ratanaṃ). Mere Paritta listening or respect cannot substitute for developing personal insight to escape Saṃsāra.

8. Futility of World-Expansion (*Lokavaḍḍhana*):

Prolonging existence without Dhamma is merely expanding the world (lokavaḍḍhana), which translates to expanding the cemetery. The Buddha noted that the skeletons left behind by a single human during one eon (kappa) would form a heap larger than Mount Vepulla.

9. Contemplation of the Fathom-Long Carcass:

The origin, cessation, and path to the cessation of the world are found within this fathom-long carcass endowed with perception and mind. Liberation requires practicing the four establishments of mindfulness (cattu-satipaṭṭhāna)—contemplating body (kāyānupassanā), feeling (vedanānupassanā), mind (cittānupassanā), and mind-objects (dhammānupassanā) internally and externally (bahiddhā).

10. The Noble Eightfold Path as the Only True Expressway:

Modern technological advancements ("divine eyes and ears") foster self-indulgence and risk reducing society to a beastly state based on wealth and status. The true shortcut to ending saṃsāric suffering (saṃsāra-dukkha) is exclusively the Middle Path / Noble Eightfold Path (eso maggo natthañño, dassanassa visuddhiyā).
