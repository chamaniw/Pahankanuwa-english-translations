Sermon 021 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #21 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #21

Primary Theme: A doctrinal breakdown of Dhammapada verse 204 (Ārogā paramā lābhā...), transitioning from practical, worldly values—health through moderation in eating, contentment as true wealth, and trust as genuine kinship—to the ultimate supramundane realization: Nibbāna as the unfelt happiness (avedayita-sukha) attained through the stilling of all fabrications (sabbasankhārasamatha).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: King Pasenadi of Kosala, his nephew Youth Sudassana, and the Buddha.

The Interaction: King Pasenadi, who habitually consumed a massive quantity of food (boiled rice from a droṇa measure of raw rice per meal), arrived to see the Buddha immediately after breakfast, overwhelmed by a severe food-coma, rolling side to side and nodding off in lethargy.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Recognizing the King's self-inflicted suffering, the Buddha sarcastically likened him to a pig fattened on waste for slaughter (mahāvarāho va nivāpapuṭṭho). Because the King was too drowsy to memorize the verse on moderation in eating (bhojane mattaññutā), his nephew Sudassana memorized it under the Buddha's instruction. Sudassana recited the stanza at every meal just as the King was about to put the final morsel of rice into his mouth, prompting the King to discard it and incrementally reduce his meal size day by day.

The Result / Resolution: The King reduced his intake from a droṇa to a single nāḷi, regaining health, physical lightness, and strength. Rejoyced, he reported to the Buddha his restored health, his peace settlement with his nephew King Ajātasattu (giving his daughter Vajirā in marriage along with Kāsi village as dowry), the recovery of a lost palace gem, and his marriage to Sakyan princess Vāsabhakhattiyā. The Buddha used these four worldly occasions to utter Dhammapada v. 204, elevating the King's temporal happiness to the highest spiritual truths.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Moderation in Eating (Bhojane Mattaññutā) & Medical Science:

The sermon aligns Dhamma with medical principles: hita-bhuk (eating wholesome/suitable food, sappāya) and mita-bhuk (eating in moderation, bhojane mattaññutā). Over-eating causes undigested food to accelerate decay, multiply diseases, and shorten life. Eating in moderation reduces feelings (vedanā), keeps mind and body alert, and preserves the lifespan (saṇikaṃ jīrati āyupālayaṃ).

2. Reflection on Food as Medicine (Āhāra-Paccavekkhaṇā):

Monastics and practitioners are instructed to consume food solely for the maintenance of the body, preventing the old pain (vedanā) of hunger without giving rise to new pain from over-eating. Food must be viewed not for entertainment or beautification, but as medicine for the self-groaning disease of hunger, as oil applied to a cart axle, or through the urgency-inducing (saṃvega) allegory of parents eating their child's flesh (putta-maṃsa-upamā) merely to cross the desert of saṃsāra.

3. Health as the Supreme Gain (Ārogā Paramā Lābhā):

Physical health is the foundational blessing without which no worldly wealth or status can be enjoyed. Unrestrained indulgence converts food from medicine into a toxic poison, leading to disease born of present-life karma and forcing wealthy individuals to eventually subsist on mere scraps.

4. Contentment as Supreme Wealth (Santuṭṭhi Paramaṃ Dhanaṃ):

Regardless of financial possessions, a miserly, envious person who lacks contentment is mentally destitute. True wealth is living contentedly within one's means. This principle is illustrated by King Kosala abandoning the plunder of King Ajātasattu’s army after recognizing the karmic cycle: "The slayer gets a slayer; the conqueror gets a conqueror; the plunderer gets plundered."

5. Trust as the Ultimate Kinship (Vissāsaparamaṃ Ñātī):

Kinship is not defined merely by blood relationships, which can still lead to war and distrust (as between Pasenadi and Ajātasattu), but by mutual trust (vissāsa). A trusted friend is a true relative, whereas relations lacking trust foster suspicion and envy.

6. The Universality of Suffering in Feeling (Yaṃ kiñci vedayitaṃ taṃ dukkhasmiṃ):

The Buddha declared that whatever is felt is included in suffering (dukkha). While worldlings only seek pleasant feeling (sukha-vedanā) to escape painful feeling (dukkha-vedanā), they fail to perceive the subtle neutral feeling (adukkhamasukha-vedanā), which is like unseeable deer tracks on a solid rock. Pleasant feeling is suffering because of its inevitable change (vipariṇāma-dukkha), painful feeling is directly suffering (dukkha-dukkha), and neutral feeling is suffering due to conditions (saṅkhāra-dukkha).

7. The Seesaw Simile of Saṃsāric Existence:

Saṃsāric existence is an endless oscillation between pleasure and pain, like children swinging on a seesaw. Swinging between these two extremes never yields lasting peace because pleasant feelings are impermanent (anicca).

8. The Three Subtle Levels of Fabrications (Saṅkhāra):

Bodily Formations (Kāya-Saṅkhāra): In-breaths and out-breaths (assāsa-passāsa).

Verbal Formations (Vacī-Saṅkhāra): Initial thought-conception (vitakka) and discursive thinking (vicāra), which become evident as acute suffering during restless, sleepless nights.

Mental Formations (Citta-Saṅkhāra): Perception (saññā) and feeling (vedanā), which represent the most subtle layers of conditioned fabrications.

9. Limits of Non-Buddhist Absorptions (Jhāna) & The Sphere of Neither-Perception-Nor-Non-Perception:

Ascetics like Uddaka Rāmaputta reached the summit of meditative absorptions—the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception (nevasaññānāsaññāyatana). However, because perception was neither fully present nor absent, it remained a state of perturbation and hesitation at the center of the seesaw, failing to yield final liberation from saṃsāra.

10. The Unbroken Pivot: Conceit "I Am" (Asmimāna):

The central pivot that keeps the seesaw of existence (bhava) swinging is the subtle conceit "I am" (asmimāna), which persists up to the stage of the Non-Returner (Anāgāmī). While coarse self-view (sakkāya-diṭṭhi) takes form or feeling as self, asmimāna is the underlying root of existence. Removing this central pivot halts the swinging and effects the cessation of becoming (bhava-nirodha).

11. Nibbāna as Unfelt Happiness (Avedayita-Sukha):

Addressing potential critics who question how happiness can exist without feeling, the Buddha explained that he does not define happiness solely in terms of pleasant feeling (vedayita-sukha). True supramundane happiness is the happiness of stilling (upasama-sukha)—an unfelt happiness (avedayita-sukha) achieved in the cessation of perception and feeling (saññā-vedayita-nirodha), completely transcending all conditioned fabrications (sabbasankhārasamatha).

12. Deconstruction of the Five Aggregates (Pañcakkhandha):

Through wise attention (yoniso manasikāra), a meditating practitioner breaks down the illusion of a solid self by seeing the five aggregates (rūpa, vedanā, saññā, saṅkhāra, viññāṇa) as mere temporary "heaps" devoid of an enduring core, much like the layered trunk of a wild banana tree. Discerning their constant passing-away invokes disenchantment, leading to liberation through letting go.
