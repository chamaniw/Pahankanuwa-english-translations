Sermon 193 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #193 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #193 (Patičča Samuppāda Sermon 11: The Law of Dependent Arising – The Noble Truth of Cessation / Poṭṭhapāda Sutta Analysis)

Primary Theme: The Law of Dependent Arising (paṭiccasamuppāda) applied to the Noble Truth of Cessation (nirodhasacca). Ven. Katukurunde Nanananda Thero demonstrates how the gradual cessation of higher perceptions (anupubbābhisaññānirodha) occurs not by postulating a self (attā), but by understanding the causal nature of perception (saññā), relinquishing volitional preparations (saṅkhārā / cetanā), and recognizing worldly linguistic designations (lokasamaññā) without tenacious grasping.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The wandering ascetic Poṭṭhapāda, his large retinue of wandering ascetics at the Mallikārāma monastery in Sāvatthi, and later Citta Hatthisāriputta (the son of an elephant trainer).

The Interaction: While on his forenoon alms round, the Buddha visits Mallikārāma. Poṭṭhapāda welcomes him respectfully and reports a recent heated debate among ascetics of various sects regarding how the cessation of higher levels of perception (abhisaññānirodha) comes about.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Contemporary non-Buddhist yogins were trapped in a dilemma: they recognized perception as a disease, boil, and dart (saññā rogo saññā gaṇḍo saññā sallaṁ), yet feared that complete absence of perception meant total delusion (asaññā sammoho). Consequently, they oscillated endlessly at the level of Neither-Perception-nor-Non-Perception (nevasaññānāsaññāyatana). Poṭṭhapāda brings this dilemma to the Buddha, asking whether perception arises randomly without cause, whether it is identical to or distinct from a self (attā), and later poses the ten unrecorded/unexplained points (avyākata).

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha refutes all non-causal and soul-based theories, revealing that perceptions arise and cease conditionally through training (sikkhā). He details how abandoning volitional intention (cetanā) at the peak of perception (saññagga) leads to the attainment of cessation (nirodhaṁ phusati). Poṭṭhapāda takes refuge as a lay disciple; Citta Hatthisāriputta enters the Order and rapidly attains full Arahanthood.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Superiority of Cessation Over All Realms of Existence:

Citing verses from the Itivuttaka (Tika Nipāta), the Buddha emphasizes that realms of Form (rūpa) and Formless realms (arūpa) remain bound to repeated existence (punabbhavaṁ) if cessation is not understood. Only by realizing the assetless Deathless Element (amataṁ dhātuṁ nirūpadhiṁ) and relinquishing all assets (upadhipaṭinissagga) does one become a "dispeller of death" (maccuhāyino).

2. The Causal Principle of Perception (Sahetu-Sappaccaya):

The Buddha refutes the notion that perception arises or vanishes spontaneously without cause. He establishes the fundamental refrain: "Through training some perceptions arise, through training some perceptions cease" (Sikkhā ekā saññā uppajjanti, sikkhā ekā saññā nirujjhanti), proving that mental states are strictly conditionally arisen (paṭiccasamuppanna).

3. Gradual Cessation of Higher Perceptions (Anupubbābhisaññānirodha):

The path in the Buddha’s dispensation follows a gradual training, action, and practice (anupubbasikkhā, anupubbakiriyā, anupubbapaṭipadā). Progression through the four jhānas and the formless attainments occurs by systematically perceiving the grossness of lower factors, abandoning them, and replacing them with subtler perceptions of seclusion, concentration, equanimity, and spatial/cognitive infinity.

4. Breakthrough via the Abandonment of Volitional Preparations (Saṅkhārā):

Pre-Buddhistic yogins were stuck in an ethereal "see-saw" because they clung to volitional preparations (saṅkhārā / cetanā). At the peak of perception (saññagga, identified here with the Sphere of Nothingness, Ākiñcaññāyatana), the disciple in this dispensation makes a radical volitional decision: "To go on intending is bad for me... What if I were not to intend and specially prepare?" (yannūnāhaṁ na ceva ceteyyaṁ nābhisaṁkhareyyaṁ). Ceasing cetanā causes all perceptions to fall away, directly touching cessation (nirodhaṁ phusati).

5. Plurality and Singularity of the "Peak of Perception" (Saññagga):

When asked if there is one peak of perception or many, the Buddha affirms both. Reaching cessation and penetrating full understanding (yāvatā saññāsamāpatti tāvatā aññāpaṭivedho) is possible from any jhānic baseline (except the state of neither-perception-nor-non-perception) by turning away from cetanā and abhisaṅkhārā.

6. Epistemic Order: Perception Precedes Knowledge:

The Buddha clarifies that perception arises first, and knowledge (ñāṇa) arises subsequently (saññā kho... paṭhamaṁ uppajjati, pacchā ñāṇaṁ). Supported by mindfulness and full awareness (sati-sampajañña), one becomes "percipient of oneself" (sakasaññī), knowing perception dynamically as a conditioned phenomenon rather than misconstruing it as a metaphysical self.

7. Refutation of Soul-Theories (Attāvāda) via Counter-Questioning:

Poṭṭhapāda proposes three definitions of self: gross physical (oḷārika), mind-made/jhānic (manomaya), and formless/percipient (arūpa). The Buddha uses counter-questioning (paṭipucchāvyākaraṇīya pañha) to show that because perceptions are constantly undergoing "otherwiseness" (aññā va saññā uppajjanti, aññā va saññā nirujjhanti), perception can never be equated with an enduring self.

8. Exposure of Baseless Assertions (Appāṭihīraka):

The Buddha illustrates the absurdity of speculative views (such as claiming the self is purely happy after death without direct experience) through two similes:

The Beauty Queen Simile: Professing love for the most beautiful woman in the land without knowing her name, clan, height, complexion, or village.

The Mansion Staircase Simile: Building a staircase at a crossroads for a mansion whose location, height, and orientation are completely unknown.

9. De-reification of Self into "Acquisitions of Self" (Attapaṭilābha):

The Buddha redefines static metaphysical concepts of "self" into functional "acquisitions of self" (attapaṭilābhā: gross, mind-made, and formless). These are merely experiential states generated through specific training (sikkhā) and are intended to be abandoned entirely to fulfill wisdom here and now.

10. Linguistic Usage vs. Tenacious Grasping (Lokasamaññā & Time Confusion):

Drawing from the Niruttipatha Sutta, Ven. Nanananda highlights how personality-view (sakkāyadiṭṭhi) stems from confusing the three periods of time (past, present, future). The Buddha uses the Milk-to-Ghee Simile (milk → curd → butter → ghee → cream of ghee) to show that a present state cannot be designated by its past or future manifestations. Concepts are mere worldly appellations, expressions, usages, and designations (lokasamaññā, lokaniruttiyo, lokavohārā, lokapaññattiyo) which the Enlightened One utilizes without tenacious grasping (aparāmasaṁ).

11. The Raft and Creeper Similes for Meditative Release:

Meditative serenity (samatha) and insight (vipassanā) function like a hanging vine used to swing across a wide river. To reach the safety of the opposite bank (Nibbāna), one must eventually dare to let go of the vine (saṅkhārā). Emerging from the cessation of perception and feeling (saññāvedayitanirodha), the mind naturally awakens to the signless (animitta), unestablished (appaṇihita), and void (suññata) nature of reality, securing full Arahanthood.
