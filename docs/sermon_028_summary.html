Sermon 028 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #28 Summary

Sermon #28 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #28

Primary Theme: The non-deterministic law of karma (kamma-vipāka), the immediate and dramatic recoil of evil committed against the innocent, and the ultimate neutralization of karmic forces through the spiritual training of virtue (sīla), concentration (samādhi), and wisdom (paññā).

---

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The hunter Koka, who hunts with a pack of dogs, and an innocent, Vinaya-observant Bhikkhu walking on his alms round (piṇḍapāta).

The Interaction: Meeting the Bhikkhu in the morning, Koka irrationally deems the sight of a monk an "unlucky wretch" (anitta-dassana). After roaming the forest without catching any game, he meets the Bhikkhu again on his return path. Filled with ill-will (vyāpāda), Koka blames the monk for his bad luck and resolves to have him killed by his hunting dogs.

The Disaster / Turning Point: The monk climbs a tree to the height of a man—adhering strictly to Vinaya rules regarding tree climbing. Koka surrounds the tree and shoots arrows into the soles of the monk’s feet. Overcome by intense physical pain (vedanā), the monk’s outer robe involuntarily slips off his shoulder and falls straight over Koka, completely covering his head. Mistaking the robe-covered hunter for the fallen monk, Koka's own dogs attack and devour their master, leaving only his bones.

The Result / Resolution: The monk, troubled by disciplinary remorse (kukkucca) over whether his falling robe compromised his virtue (sīla) or caused the man's death, presents the case to the Buddha. The Buddha confirms the monk's pure morality, explains that Koka reaped the exact fruit of harboring hatred against an innocent person—recounting a past life where Koka was a physician killed by a snake he tricked children into catching—and utters Dhammapada Verse 125. Upon hearing the stanza, the Bhikkhu penetrates the Dhamma and attains Arahantship.

---

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Recoil of Evil against the Innocent (*Yo appaduṭṭhassa narassa dussati*)

Committing an act of ill-will against an innocent, defilement-free person (anaṅgaṇa) inevitably recoils upon the perpetrator. Just as fine dust thrown against the wind (sukhumo rajo paṭivātaṃva khitto) flies back into the thrower's face, karmic retribution (kamma-vipāka) strikes back at the wrongdoer in a dramatic, exact manner.

2. Rejection of Absolute Determinism (*A-niyati-vāda*)

The Buddha explicitly rejects strict determinism—the view that one must experience the fruit of every past action in the exact same manner. If karma were strictly deterministic, living the holy life (brahmacariya) and attaining the cessation of suffering (dukkha-nirodha) would be impossible, as beings would be trapped paying back endless past deeds.

3. The Lump of Salt Simile (*Loṇaphala Sutta* Principle)

The magnitude of karmic results depends on the mental development of the individual. A minor evil act committed by an undeveloped, narrow-minded person lacking virtue, concentration, and wisdom (appātumo) leads straight to hell—like a lump of salt dissolved in a tiny cup of water making it undrinkable. The exact same deed committed by a person of great moral and mental stature (mahātma) yields only minor, temporary results in this life (diṭṭhadhammavedanīya)—like a lump of salt dissolved in the vast River Ganges.

4. Threefold Ripening of Karma (*Kamma-Vipāka*)

Karma matures over time through three primary modes:

Diṭṭhadhammavedanīya (to be experienced in this present lifetime),

Upapajjavedanīya (to be experienced in the immediate next birth), and

Aparāpariyavedanīya (to be experienced in subsequent future existences).

5. Complex Near-Death Dynamics (*Maraṇāsanna-Citta*)

Apparent anomalies—such as bad people taking rebirth in heavenly worlds (sugati) or good people falling into states of deprivation (apāya)—occur due to prior/subsequent karmas or the alignment of the mind with right view (sammā-diṭṭhi) or wrong view (micchā-diṭṭhi) at the crucial near-death moment (maraṇāsanna). However, unwholesome karma remains stored and must be experienced eventually.

6. Fourfold Functional Breakdown of Karma

The text delineates four functional operations of karma:

Janaka-kamma: Productive karma that yields rebirth/reconnection (paṭisandhi).

Upatthambhaka-kamma: Supportive karma that strengthens existing pleasant or painful results.

Upapīḷaka-kamma: Obstructive/weakening karma that counteracts or suppresses other karmic forces.

Upaghātaka-kamma: Destructive karma that completely cuts off the force of a productive karma, often causing untimely death (akāla-maraṇa).

7. Intention as Karma (*Cetanā*) and Self-Illusion (*Sakkāya-Diṭṭhi*)

Karma is fundamentally volition/intention (cetanāhaṃ bhikkhave kammaṃ vadāmi). Past karmic forces (kammāvega) constantly emerge into consciousness (viññāṇa). When a worldling (puthujjana) governed by self-illusion (sakkāya-diṭṭhi) reacts to these emerging forces with "I" and "mine", new karma is accumulated, entangling the "ball of thread" of existence (saṃsāra).

8. Refutation of Jain Self-Mortification (*Nigaṇṭha-Vāda*)

The Buddha refutes the Jain view that past karma can be exhausted through physical torture and bodily asceticism. Attempting to burn away karma through physical pain is futile because one can never quantify or calculate how much karma remains. True escape is achieved not by bodily suffering, but by cutting the root of karma through mental training.

9. Neutralization of Karma via the Noble Eightfold Path

The wheel of karma (kamma-cakka) can only be broken by the Wheel of Dhamma (Dhamma-cakka). Through virtue (sīla), one restrains physical and verbal acts (virati); through concentration (samādhi), one suppresses incoming defilements; and through wisdom (paññā), one contemplates the impermanence (anicca), suffering (dukkha), and non-self (anattā) of all preparations (saṅkhāra), maintaining equanimity (upekkhā) and eradicating the conceit "I am" (asmi-māna).

10. The Universal Scope of the Five Precepts (*Pañca-Sīla*)

The training rules are not merely social conventions but reflect profound natural laws. Violating precepts results in both self-harm (atta-hiṃsā) and harm to others (para-hiṃsā). Observing sīla protects the individual's continuum across saṃsāra, enabling one to reach the stages of Awakening (Sotāpatti, Sakadāgāmī, Anāgāmī, Arahatta) and attain the deathless Great Nibbāna.
