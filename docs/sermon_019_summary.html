Sermon 019 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #19 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #19

Primary Theme: The operation of kamma and rebirth, the inexorable nature of saṃsāric consequences, and the conquest of the wheel of kamma (kamma-vaṭṭa) through the supramundane Middle Way—specifically "neither-dark-nor-bright kamma."

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Arahant Tissa (a family-associate monk, kulūpaga), a royal jeweler, his devoted wife, and a pet crane.

The Interaction: For twelve years, Arahant Tissa regularly visited the home of the jeweler for alms. The jeweler and his wife treated the Thera with deep devotion, regarding him as they would their own parents.

The Disaster / Turning Point: King Kosala sent a priceless gemstone to be cleaned. The jeweler, who was cutting meat, took the gem with blood-stained hands and went to wash up. Attracted by the smell of blood, the household's pet crane swallowed the gem in front of the Thera. Discovering the gem missing, the jeweler suspected the monk. Refusing to listen to his wife's pleas, he tortured the Arahant by tightening a wooden tourniquet around his head until blood poured from his sensory orifices. When the crane came to drink the blood, the enraged jeweler kicked and killed it.

The Result / Resolution: Seeing that the bird was dead, the Thera disclosed that the crane had swallowed the gem, having previously kept silent to save the bird's life. Upon cutting open the bird and finding the gem, the jeweler was struck with profound remorse (saṃvega) and begged for forgiveness. The Thera attributed the ordeal to a "saṃsāric flaw" (saṃsārika-dosa), forgave him, but recognized the danger (ādīnava) of household intimacy. He immediately undertook the strict almsround practice (dhutaṅga). Due to physical trauma, the Arahant soon attained PariNibbāna. Rebirth destinations: the Arahant attained complete extinction (pariNibbāna); the wife went to a heavenly realm (sagga); the jeweler fell into hell (niraya); and the crane was reborn in the womb (gabbha) of the jeweler's wife.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Inviolability of the Law of Kamma (kamma-niyāma)

Kamma is a powerful natural law that governs saṃsāric existence; not even Buddhas or Arahants can bypass the physical fruition (vipāka) of past deeds. The Arahant Tissa had to experience severe physical trauma due to the alignment of past kamma, illustrating that saṃsāric beings are true heirs to their actions (kammadāyādā).

2. Saṃsāric Flaws (saṃsārika-dosa) and the Perils of Monastic Intimacy (kulūpaga-ādīnava)

The Thera categorized the tragedy not as personal malice, but as a structural flaw inherent in saṃsāra (saṃsārika-dosa). Furthermore, he recognized the fault of being a family-associate monk (kulūpaga). While laypeople value intimacy, for monastics, household familiarity presents grave dangers, prompting the Thera to resolve upon strict dhutaṅga (wandering house-to-house for alms).

3. The Strategic Importance of Intention (cetanā) in the Present Moment

Citing the Buddha's declaration ("Cetanāhaṃ bhikkhave kammaṃ vadāmi"), Ven. Nanananda highlights that kamma operates at the precise moment of intention. While past fruit (vipāka) cannot be avoided, maintaining a wholesome stance and mindful determination in the present moment prevents one from yielding to saṃsāric habits or being dragged into the evil kamma of others.

4. The Practical Necessity of the Threefold Training (tivisikkhā)

Virtue (sīla): Provides a firm ethical foundation and enables "abstinence upon the arrival of the occasion" (sampatta-virati), guarding the mind against unwholesome impulses.

Concentration (samādhi): Calms mental hindrances (nīvaraṇa), producing a focused, equanimous mind capable of making wise choices under pressure.

Wisdom (paññā): Insight wisdom (vipassanā-paññā) grounded in path-knowledge (magga-ñāṇa) provides the ultimate equanimity (upekkhā) needed to break the cycle of kamma.

5. Practical Analogy of Driving a Vehicle

Ven. Nanananda uses a modern simile to clarify the practical application of the threefold training: driving on the road requires abiding by traffic rules (sīla), driving with focused awareness rather than a scattered mind (samādhi), and making split-second decisions in emergencies (paññā).

6. The Fourfold Classification of Kamma (catur-kamma)

Dark kamma with dark results (kaṇha-kamma): Afflictive actions leading to afflictive rebirths like hell (niraya), exemplified by the five heinous crimes (ānantarika-kamma).

Bright kamma with bright results (sukka-kamma): The ten wholesome courses of action leading to pleasant existences (e.g., Subhakiṇha Brahma realm).

Dark-and-bright kamma with mixed results (kaṇha-sukka): A mixture of wholesome and unwholesome deeds yielding mixed human or heavenly existences.

Neither-dark-nor-bright kamma (akaṇha-asukka): Intention directed toward the destruction of all kamma—equated with the Noble Eightfold Path (Ariya Aṭṭhaṅgika Magga) and the Seven Factors of Enlightenment (satta bojjhaṅga).

7. The Middle Way: Crossing the Flood Without Halting or Straining (ogha-taraṇa)

Referencing the Ogha-taraṇa Sutta, the Buddha crossed the four floods (ogha: sensuality, becoming, views, ignorance) "without halting and without straining." Halting represents passive indulgence in sensual pleasures (kāmasukhallikānuyoga); straining represents extreme, self-mortifying ascetical struggles (attakilamathānuyoga). The Middle Way requires developing wholesome states (puññā/kusala) as a raft without clinging to them as an ultimate self.

8. Two Levels of Right View (sammā-diṭṭhi)

Mundane Right View (kammassakatā sammā-diṭṭhi): Understanding ownership of kamma, distinguishing merit from demerit, and taking personal responsibility for actions.

Supramundane Right View (lokuttara-sammā-diṭṭhi): Direct penetration of the Four Noble Truths, seeing existence as purely suffering (dukkha) devoid of a soul or "I".

9. Origination (samudaya) vs. Cessation (nirodha) Orientations

Unenlightened worldlings (puthujjana) live facing the side of origination (samudaya), constantly striving to renew the perception of permanence (nicca-saññā) and selfhood. The noble disciple (ariya-sāvaka) stands facing the side of cessation (nirodha), actively reducing craving and ignorance.

10. The Mechanics of Saṃsāric Accumulation (saṅkhāra, anusaya, āsava, vaṭṭa)

Kamma is rooted in ignorance (avijjāpaccayā saṅkhārā). Repetitive actions construct saṃsāric habits (kamma-vaṭṭa), depositing underlying tendencies (anusaya) at the bottom of the mental continuum and generating taints (āsava) and floods (ogha). These forces can only be appeased (saṅkhāra-upasama) through wisdom.

11. Gradual Purification via the Seven Relay Chariots (satta-visuddhi)

Using the classic simile of King Pasenadi's seven relay chariots, the sermon outlines the step-by-step purification of the mind—from purification of virtue (sīla-visuddhi) to purification of mind (citta-visuddhi) and purification of view (diṭṭhi-visuddhi)—culminating in the simultaneous alignment of all eight path factors in a single supramundane moment.
