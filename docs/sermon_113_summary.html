Sermon 113 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #113 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #113

Primary Theme: Overcoming the mental rust of stinginess (macchariya) through the practice of generosity (cāga), and penetrating the root illusion of ownership ("I" and "mine") via direct insight into non-self (anattā).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Merchant Ānanda of Sāvatthi (an immensely wealthy miser holding forty crores of wealth) and his son, Mūlasiri.

The Interaction: Fortnightly, Merchant Ānanda gathered his relatives and preached a "sermon of stinginess" to his son Mūlasiri, instructing him never to give away wealth but to relentlessly accumulate it, drawing similes from the gradual depletion of eye-collyrium and the gradual building of anthills by termites or honeycombs by bees.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Ānanda died without revealing the location of five hidden treasures and was reborn in the womb of an outcaste woman in a nearby village. His conception brought immediate, severe economic hardship to the five hundred families of the village. The villagers isolated his family and exiled the mother. When born, the child possessed a hideously deformed body resembling a hungry ghost (peta). Years later, sent out by his mother to beg with a potsherd, he instinctively wandered into his former mansion. Unrecognized, he was beaten by guards and thrown onto a rubbish heap.

The Result / Resolution: Passing by on almsround, the Buddha recognized the outcaste boy as former Merchant Ānanda and summoned Mūlasiri. Upon the Buddha's instruction, the deformed child revealed the exact locations of the five hidden treasures, convincing Mūlasiri of his past-life identity. The Buddha then preached Dhammapada verse 62 to the gathered crowd, generating deep spiritual urgency (saṃvega) and guiding many to the realization of the paths and fruits of liberation (magga-phala).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Stinginess as Mental Rust and Poverty of Mind: Unlimited craving (taṇhā) and stinginess (macchariya) act as mental rust (mala). Richness is a state of mind (citta), not a measure of physical possessions; despite his forty crores, Merchant Ānanda lived and died in extreme spiritual poverty.

2. Kamma as the Sculptor of Mind and Body: In accordance with the teaching "Kammassakā sattā...", volition (cetanā) shapes one's future state of existence (bhava). Ānanda's distorted, miserly stream of thoughts directly molded a deformed, ghost-like physical body in his next birth.

3. Past-Life Memory Stored in Consciousness: Past-life memory (jātissara-ñāṇa) remains stored in the continuity of consciousness (viññāṇa). The child navigated his former mansion and recalled hidden treasures naturally via these stored mental tracks rather than through supernormal meditative absorptions (abhiññā).

4. Dangers of Discouraging Generosity: Advising others against giving constitutes wrong guidance and wrong view (micchā-diṭṭhi). The Buddha compared a miser's unused wealth to a demon-haunted pond (rakkhasa-pukkharaṇī)—clean water that no one can drink—which brings downfall to lower realms.

5. Attributes of the Worthy Person (Sappurisa): A noble disciple (sotāpanna) possesses noble wealth (ariya-dhana) characterized by generosity (cāga). They dwell at home with a mind free from the stain of stinginess (vigatamalamaccherena cetasā), open-handed (payatapāṇi), delighting in relinquishment (vossaggarato), accessible to beggars (yācayogo), and committed to sharing (dānasaṃvibhāgarato).

6. The Threefold Mind in Giving (Ti-cetanā): For an act of giving to yield maximum spiritual fruit, purity must be maintained across three stages: joy before giving (pubbeva dānā sumano), clarity and peace during giving (dadaṃ cittaṃ pasādayaṃ), and satisfaction without regret after giving (datvā attamano hoti). Regret defiles the merit (kusala).

7. Adorning and Equipping the Mind (Cittālaṅkāraṃ Cittaparikkhāraṃ): The highest purpose of giving is not material return, but adorning the mind for concentration (samatha) through the recollection of generosity (cāgānussati) and equipping the mind for insight (vipassanā) by loosening the bonds of craving (taṇhā).

8. The Four Bases of Benevolence (Saṅgaha-vatthu): Generosity (dāna), kindly speech (peyyavajja), useful conduct (atthacariyā), and impartiality (samānattatā) act as social pillars that support society just as a linchpin holds together a moving chariot.

9. Viewing the World as a Resting Hall (Ambalama): Wise practitioners treat lands, houses, and worldly environments as a temporary transit hall (ambalama). Understanding life's transient nature prevents attachment and encourages continuous sharing of resources and labor.

10. Shattering "I" and "Mine" through Non-Self (Anattā): The line "Attā hi attano natthi" ("Even oneself does not belong to oneself") cuts off the foundation of craving (taṇhā) and ignorance (avijjā). Because the five aggregates (pañcakkhandha—rūpa, vedanā, saññā, saṅkhāra, viññāṇa) cannot be kept under absolute control and lead to affliction, they are non-self (anattā).

11. The Body as an Open Bag: The thirty-two parts of the body (dvattiṃsākāra) undergo a constant process of arising and passing away (udayabbaya), comparable to a bag open at both ends. Worldly life operates under this dual law of income and expenditure, filling and emptying.

12. Cultivating Extinction (Nibbāna) in the Present: Giving provides a moment-to-moment abandonment of defilement through substitution of opposites (tadaṅga-pahāna). Systematically relinquishing greed (lobha), hatred (dosa), and delusion (moha) brings about the calming of all fabrications (sabbasaṅkhārasamatha) and the cessation of existence (bhava-nirodha).
