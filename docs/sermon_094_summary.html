Pahankanuwa Sermon #94 Summary

Sermon #94 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #94 (Sinhala Audio Sermon No. 122)

Primary Theme: The ruinous nature of knowledge acquired without moral wisdom (bāla vs. paṇḍita), the inexorable law of karma (kamma-niyāma), and the application of the pragmatic "Unquestionable Teaching" (Apaṇṇaka Dhamma) to establish right view (sammā-diṭṭhi) and attain the cessation of existence (bhava-nirodha).

---

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Saṭṭhikūṭa Peta (a hungry ghost three gavu in size) and his past-life persona: an overly ambitious pupil who learned the art of pebble-slinging from a crippled master (piḷā).

The Interaction: A cripple earned great wealth (Sabbaṭṭhaka reward and four villages) from the King by using his pebble-slinging skills to shoot goat dung into the mouth of an excessively talkative royal chaplain (purohita). Impressed by this feat, a young man served the cripple diligently to learn the craft.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Upon graduating, the pupil sought to test his skill. Misinterpreting his master’s legal warning to avoid fines by only targeting someone with "no father or mother," the foolish pupil shot a pebble straight through the head of the Paccekabuddha Sunetta as He entered the city for alms.

The Result / Resolution: The Paccekabuddha suffered intense pain (vedanā) and entered final Nibbāna (parinibbāna). Enraged townspeople beat the perpetrator to death on the spot. After suffering in hell (niraya) for aeons, he was reborn as a giant hungry ghost (peta) whose head is repeatedly crushed by 60,000 glowing iron hammers. Ven. Moggallāna witnessed this peta while descending Vulture’s Peak, and the Buddha confirmed the vision, taking the event as the nidāna to recite Dhammapada verse 72.

---

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Knowledge Without Wisdom Leads to Ruin (*Yāvadeva anatthāya, ñattaṃ bālassa jāyati*)

Academic degrees, intellectual cleverness, or physical skills acquired by a spiritually ignorant person (bāla) serve only for self-destruction (anatthāya). Unchecked by moral vision, such knowledge severs the "head" of true wisdom and destroys one's wholesome roots (sukkaṃsaṃ).

2. The Inescapable Machine of Karma (*Kamma-niyāma* / *Kamma-yantraya*)

Karma is an automatic, universally operating law (kamma-niyāma) from which even Buddhas and Arahants cannot grant exemption. As stated by the Blessed One (Nāhaṃ... katānaṃ upacitānaṃ kammānaṃ abyantīkiriyaṃ vadāmi), accumulated deeds do not dissipate without yielding their results; the momentum of karma regenerates the physical form of hell-beings and petas to endure prolonged retribution.

3. Refutation of Heretical Nihilism and Fatalism

The sermon highlights the severe dangers of ancient false views (micchā-diṭṭhi), such as:

Fatalism (Niyati-vāda / Saṃsāra-suddhi): Promoted by Makkhali Gosāla (compared to a net catching fish), claiming purification happens automatically through transmigration like a unrolling ball of thread, denying individual effort and cause-and-effect.

Non-action (Akiriya-vāda): Taught by Pūraṇa Kassapa, asserting that no merit or demerit accrues from any action, no matter how good or heinous.

4. The Limits and Dangers of Pure Logic (*Tarka*)

Intellectual logic is a double-edged sword: an argument can be internally consistent yet entirely contrary to ultimate reality. Relying exclusively on speculative logic to reject spiritual truths or the words of enlightened beings is an exercise in self-deception.

5. The Unquestionable Teaching (*Apaṇṇaka Dhamma*)

Originating from the Apaṇṇaka Sutta (and exemplified in the Apaṇṇaka Jātaka), this principle offers a pragmatic framework for decision-making in the absence of direct spiritual verification (paccakkha). By weighing the severe drawbacks (ādīnava) of wrong view against the spiritual benefits (ānisaṃsa) of right view, a wise person (viññū-purisa) adopts the stance that guarantees peace and safety in either eventuality.

6. Graduated Application of *Apaṇṇaka Dhamma* to Higher Realms

The sermon shows how the apaṇṇaka method elevates one's view progressively:

Mundane Sphere: Choosing right view over nihilism avoids hellish destinations (duggati).

Fine-Material (Rūpa) vs. Formless (Arūpa): Recognizing that attachment to form leads to conflict, one strives for formless attainments (samāpatti).

Formless Realms vs. Cessation (Bhava-Nirodha): Realizing that even formless states harbor subtle grasping (upādāna), the wise practitioner chooses the path leading beyond all existence—Nibbāna.

7. Mundane Right View (*Kammassakatā Sammā-Diṭṭhi*) as an Indispensable Ladder

Mundane right view—accepting the fruits of almsgiving, moral cause-and-effect, and the reality of this world and the next—is characterized as sāsavā puññabhāgiyā upadhivepakkā (tainted, siding with merit, ripening in favorable existence). Prematurely discarding this mundane right view under the guise of higher non-attachment is like throwing away a ladder before climbing it.

8. Supramundane Right View (*Lokuttara Sammā-Diṭṭhi*)

Distinct from mundane right view, supramundane right view is ariyā anāsavā lokuttarā maggaṅgā (noble, taintless, supramundane, a path factor). It belongs directly to the Noble Eightfold Path and focuses on the direct penetration of the Four Noble Truths to completely eradicate existence (bhava).

9. Mental Environmental Pollution (*Mānasika Parisara Dūṣaṇaya*)

While modern society worries about physical and atmospheric pollution caused by technological misuse, a far more devastating crisis is mental environmental pollution. Books, ideologies, and materialist philosophies that promote wrong view (micchā-diṭṭhi) corrupt public consciousness, leading countless beings into states of woe.

10. The Functional Harmony of Faith (*Saddhā*) and the Faculties (*Indriya*)

Far from being blind belief, genuine faith (saddhā) established through reasoned apaṇṇaka reflection serves as the catalyst for the remaining spiritual faculties: energy (viriya), mindfulness (sati), concentration (samādhi), and ultimately liberation through wisdom (paññā).
