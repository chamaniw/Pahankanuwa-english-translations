Sermon 144 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #144 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #144 (Pahankaṇuwa Sinhala Audio Dhamma Sermon No. 96)

Primary Theme: The essential contrast between mere scholastic learning (pariyatti / gantha-dhura) and direct meditative realization (vipassanā-dhura), emphasizing that liberation consists of practical training (dhammānudhamma-paṭipatti) and non-clinging (anupādāna) rather than intellectual mastery.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Two monks who were close friends, ordained together in Sāvatthi in their old age. One chose the duty of study (gantha-dhura), while the other chose the duty of insight (vipassanā-dhura).

The Interaction: The insight-practicing monk retired to the forest, meditated diligently, and attained Arahantship with the four analytical knowledges (paṭisambhidā). Meanwhile, the studying monk became a renowned scholar and taught a large group of five hundred disciples. Whenever the Arahant monk's disciples visited Sāvatthi, they conveyed their teacher's humble respects to his scholar friend.

The Disaster / Turning Point: A feeling of jealousy and conceit (māna) arose in the scholar monk. Believing his friend was unlearned and incapable of reciting even a four-line verse, he resolved to embarrass and question him on scriptural matters upon his next visit. Perceiving that harassing an Arahant would cause the scholar monk to fall into hell, the Buddha compassionately intervened by visiting their hut.

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha questioned both monks. While the scholar monk successfully answered questions regarding mundane absorptions (jhānas), he became speechless when asked about the supramundane paths (magga-phala). The Arahant monk answered all path-related questions flawlessly, earning the Buddha’s resounding praise ("Sādhu"), which vibrated up to the Brahma world. The Buddha then uttered Dhammapada verses 19–20, clarifying that the scholar is like a cowherd counting another's cattle, whereas the practicing Arahant is like the owner who enjoys the five products of the cow.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Study Duty (Gantha-dhura) vs. Insight Duty (Vipassanā-dhura) and the Cowherd Simile

Mere intellectual acquisition, memorization, and teaching of the Buddha-word without personal realization is compared to a cowherd (gopa) who merely counts others' cows without tasting their milk or butter. Direct spiritual experience through practice makes one an owner who partakes in the true benefits of monkhood (sāmaññassa bhāgī).

2. The Principle of Non-Grasping (Anupādiyāno idha vā huraṃ vā)

The core nature of an Arahant is complete freedom from grasping (upādāna) "here or hereafter" (this world or the next, internal or external sense-bases, present or future five aggregates). True Dhamma fruit transcends conceptual clinging.

3. The Simile of the Raft (Kullūpama Sutta)

The Dhamma is taught for the purpose of crossing over (nittaraṇatthāya), not for holding onto or carrying on one's shoulders (no gahanaṭṭhāya). The near shore represents self-illusion (sakkāya-diṭṭhi), the far shore represents Nibbāna, the great body of water represents the four floods (ogha—sensual desire, becoming, views, ignorance), and the raft represents the Noble Eightfold Path.

4. The Simile of the Snake (Alagaddūpama Sutta)

Learning the Dhamma incorrectly for the sake of debates, theories, and looking down on others is like grabbing a venomous snake by its tail or body; it turns back and inflicts fatal harm. Scriptural knowledge must be studied solely for practical examination and implementation.

5. Definition of a True "Dweller in the Dhamma" (Dhammavihārī)

A monk who spends his day purely studying (pariyatti-bahulo), teaching (desanā-bahulo), reciting (sajjhāya-bahulo), or intellectually reflecting (vitakka-bahulo) is not a true dweller in the Dhamma. Only one who devotes himself to seclusion, concentration (samādhi), and insight meditation is designated by the Buddha as a dhammavihārī.

6. The Thirty-Seven Aids to Enlightenment (Sattatisa Bodhipakkhiyā Dhammā)

At the end of His ministry, the Buddha summarized His entire 45 years of teaching not as theoretical doctrines, but as practical groups of training—anchored by the Four Foundations of Mindfulness (satipaṭṭhāna), practiced in quiet retreats ("empty huts", "roots of trees").

7. Radical Attention (Yoniso Manasikāra) and the Five Aggregates (Pañcupādānakkhandha)

Realization occurs within the "fathom-long body" through radical attention, as illustrated by Ven. Udāyī. By observing the arising and passing away of form (rūpa), feeling (vedanā), perception (saññā), mental preparations (saṅkhāra), and consciousness (viññāṇa), one directly sees impermanence (anicca), suffering (dukkha), and non-self (anattā).

8. The Five Hindrances (Nīvaraṇa) and the Five Water Similes (Saṅgārava Sutta)

The Buddha explains to the Brahmin Saṅgārava why knowledge is sometimes forgotten or unclear using five similes of a water bowl:

Kāmacchanda (Sensual desire) = Water mixed with colorful dyes.

Byāpāda (Ill-will) = Water boiling and bubbling over a fire.

Thīna-middha (Sloth and torpor) = Water covered over with water-moss.

Uddhacca-kukkucca (Restlessness and worry) = Water stirred up and rippling in the wind.

Vicikicchā (Doubt) = Muddy, turbid water kept in the dark.

A mind overcome by these hindrances cannot see its own good, others' good, or understand deep Dhamma.

9. The Seven Factors of Enlightenment (Bojjhaṅga)

To clear the hindrances and bring the Noble Eightfold Path to maturity, one must systematically cultivate: mindfulness (sati), investigation of states (dhamma-vicaya), energy (viriya), rapture (pīti), tranquility (passaddhi), concentration (samādhi), and equanimity (upekkhā).

10. Systematic Cultivation of Satipaṭṭhāna & *Sampajañña*

Practice begins with mindfulness of breathing (ānāpānasati), posture awareness (iriyāpatha), clear comprehension of subtle physical movements (sampajañña), element analysis (dhātu-manasikāra—compared to a butcher dividing a cow), and cemetery contemplations (asubha).

Contemplation must be practiced internally (ajjhatta), externally (bahiddhā), and both internally and externally (ajjhatta-bahiddhā) to break subjective attachment.

11. The Fetters of the Sense-Bases (Saḷāyatana) and the Two Oxen Simile

Ven. Sāriputta explains to Ven. Mahākoṭṭhita that the eye is not a fetter to forms, nor are forms a fetter to the eye. Like a black ox and a white ox bound together by a single rope, the true fetter is the middle link: desire-and-lust (chanda-rāga). Liberation requires removing this internal desire, not destroying external objects.

12. The Principle of Gradual Training (Anupubba-sikkhā)

Spiritual awakening cannot be attained by abrupt jumping; it follows a process of gradual training, gradual action, and gradual practice (anupubba-sikkhā, anupubba-kiriyā, anupubba-paṭipadā), rooted in virtue (sīla), developed through concentration (samādhi), and perfected through liberating wisdom (paññā).
