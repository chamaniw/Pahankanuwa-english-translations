Sermon 085 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #85 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #85 (Sinhala Audio Sermon No. 182)

Primary Theme: Overcoming craving for food (rasa-taṇhā), understanding the insidious danger of gains, honors, and fame (lābha-sakkāra), and recognizing the absolute superiority of the "wonder of instruction" (anusāsanī-pāṭihāriya) over psychic displays (iddhi-pāṭihāriya).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Ven. Piṇḍola Bhāradvāja, a Great Arahant declared by the Buddha to be foremost (etadagga) among monks who utter the lion’s roar (sīhanāda).

The Interaction: Born to the chief Brahmin priest of King Udeni in Kosambī, Bhāradvāja was highly educated in the Vedas but suffered from an excessive craving (taṇhā) for food, earning him the nickname "Piṇḍola" (the alms-seeker). Inspired by the gains of the Sangha in Rājagaha, he ordained. Through the Buddha’s guidance and rigorous adherence to ascetic practices (dhutaṅga), he eradicated his greed, developed deep insight (vipassanā), and attained Arahantship with supernormal knowledges (abhiññā). He fearlessly declared his attainment to the Sangha, offering to resolve any doubts regarding the path and fruit (magga-phala).

The Disaster / Turning Point:

1. The Miserly Brahmin: Out of compassion, Ven. Piṇḍola preached on giving (dāna-kathā) to a miserly friend from his lay life. The Brahmin mistook his sermon for an attempt to extort food and wealth. To show his pure motives, Ven. Piṇḍola rejected a personal meal offer, redirected it to the Sangha, and uttered verses declaring that his monastic life was free from wrong livelihood (micchā-ājīva) and that food was not "close to his heart."

2. The Sandalwood Bowl Incident: A financier (seṭṭhi) in Rājagaha suspended a high-value sandalwood bowl from bamboo poles, challenging any genuine Arahant or possessor of supernormal powers (iddhi) to fly up and retrieve it. After the six famous non-Buddhist teachers failed, Ven. Piṇḍola Bhāradvāja (encouraged by Ven. Mahā Moggallāna) rose into the air, flew three times around Rājagaha, and claimed the bowl amidst public clamor.

The Result / Resolution: When the Buddha learned of the public uproar, He severely censured Ven. Piṇḍola Bhāradvāja for performing a psychic display (iddhi-pāṭihāriya) to laypeople for the sake of a "miserable wooden bowl." The Buddha ordered the bowl broken into pieces to be used as eye-ointment, prohibited wooden bowls altogether, and made displaying psychic powers to laypeople an offense of wrongdoing (dukkaṭa). This incident vividly demonstrated Ven. Piṇḍola's own verse: that gains, honors, and praise (sakkāra) constitute a sticky swamp (paṅka) and a subtle, hard-to-extract arrow (salla).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Lion's Roar (Sīhanāda) and Fearless Declaration:

The Dhamma is compared to a lion's roar because it fearlessly silences false teachings. Ven. Piṇḍola Bhāradvāja earned the foremost position (etadagga) in uttering the lion's roar due to his past aspiration (dating back to Padumuttara Buddha) and his fearless invitation to anyone doubting the supramundane paths and fruits (magga-phala).

2. Right Livelihood (Sammā-Ājīva) vs. Wrong Livelihood (Micchā-Ājīva / Anaya):

A genuine renunciant does not practice Dhamma to gain material requisites through illegitimate means (anaya / micchā-ājīva). Monastic life must remain free from subtle forms of extortion or preaching driven by personal gain.

3. Danger of the Craving for Taste (Rasa-Taṇhā):

Citing the Theragāthā, Ven. Nanananda points out: "rasataṇhāya gadhito hadayaṃ nāvabujjhati" (one bound by the craving for taste does not awaken the heart). Greed for taste blinds the mind, preventing it from awakening to Nibbāna or delighting in wisdom (ñāṇe na ramati).

4. Moderation in Eating (Bhojane Mattaññutā) and Practical Similes:

Food should be consumed with strict reflection, using three classic canonical similes:

Medicinal Ointment: Applied to a sore body wound solely to heal it.

Axle Grease: Applied to a chariot wheel merely to facilitate the journey (the carriage of the five aggregates, pañcakkhandha, toward Nibbāna).

Licking Honey from a Razor’s Edge (Khuradhārūpama): Eating taste-bound food with extreme care to protect the mind/tongue from being cut by sensual attachment.

5. The Son’s Flesh Simile (Putta-Maṃsūpama):

To highlight the spiritual urgency (saṃvega) regarding physical food (kabaḷīkārāhāra), the Buddha used the harrowing story of parents trapped in a desert who eat their only son's flesh—weeping and beating their breasts—solely to survive the crossing. Ven. Nanananda criticizes later commentary additions that tried to "soften" the story, emphasizing that the Buddha used this stark image to show that physical food must be taken without playfulness, intoxication, vanity, or lust, but solely to cross the desert of saṃsāra.

6. Mastering Food Leads to Non-Returning (Anāgāmī):

Conquering the craving for taste (rasa-taṇhā) entails abandoning attachment to the five cords of sensual pleasure (pañca-kāmaguṇa). Fully understanding physical food eradicates the residual sensual lust (kāmarāga) required to be reborn in the sense-realm, making one a Non-Returner (anāgāmī).

7. The Body as a Mere Accumulation (Samussaya):

The physical form (rūpakkhandha) is described as samussaya (a mere heap/piling up) or odanakummāsūpacaya (accumulated by rice and breadcrumbs). Seeing the body as an unstable, food-dependent aggregate frees the mind from identifying with it.

8. Gains, Honors, and Fame (Lābha-Sakkāra-Silōka) as a Swamp and Arrow:

Lay homage and gifts are characterized as a swamp (paṅka) in which ascetics sink, and a subtle arrow (sukhumaṃ sallaṃ) that pierces undetected and is hard to extract. Unworthy, weak-minded individuals (kāpurisa) cannot abandon honor. The Buddha compared sakkāra to the fruit of a banana tree, bamboo, or reed, or the pregnancy of a she-mule—something that ultimately destroys the recipient.

9. Obstacle Even to Arahants' Seclusion:

In conversation with Ven. Ānanda, the Buddha revealed that sakkāra is dangerous even to Arahants. While it cannot destroy their unshakable liberation (akuppā cetovimutti), it robs them of their quiet seclusion and pleasant abidings in concentration in this very life (diṭṭhadhamma-sukhavihāra).

10. The Three Wonders (Pāṭihāriya) and the Disadvantage of Psychic Feats:

The Buddha identified three types of wonders in the Kevaḍḍha Sutta:

Iddhi-pāṭihāriya (Wonder of Supernormal Powers)

Ādesanā-pāṭihāriya (Wonder of Mind-Reading)

Anusāsanī-pāṭihāriya (Wonder of Instruction)

The Buddha expressed "disgust and shame" (aṭṭīyati harāyati) toward iddhi and ādesanā displays because skeptics can dismiss them as mere magic, charms, or sciences (e.g., the Gandhārī or Maṇikā charms).

11. The Sixfold Structure of the Wonder of Instruction (Anusāsanī-Pāṭihāriya):

The true miracle of Buddhism lies in moral and mental transformation, encapsulated in six concise directives:

Evaṃ vitakketha (Think in this way)

Mā evaṃ vitakkayittha (Do not think in that way)

Evaṃ manasikarotha (Attend to things in this way)

Mā evaṃ manasikarotha (Do not attend to things in that way)

Idaṃ pajahatha (Abandon this)

Idaṃ upasampajja viharatha (Attain and dwell in this)

12. The Dhamma as Experiential and Immediate (Ehipassika & Sandiṭṭhika):

Unlike sectarian teachings that are ill-expounded (durakkhāta) or require belief in unseen promises (samparāyika - "go and see after death"), the True Dhamma (svākkhāta) is sandiṭṭhika (directly visible here and now), akālika (timeless/immediate), and ehipassika ("come and see"). When greed, hatred, and delusion are reduced, immediate peace is directly realized within oneself (paccattaṃ veditabbo viññūhi).
