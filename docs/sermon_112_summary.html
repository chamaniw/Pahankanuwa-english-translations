Sermon 112 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #112 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #112

Primary Theme: The unquenchable nature of craving (taṇhā) and sensual pleasures (kāma), the hidden suffering of household life, and the realization of Nibbāna as the complete destruction of craving (taṇhakkhaya).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: A young monk who left the household life to study the Vinaya and monastic discipline under a distant teacher; his father and younger brother.

The Interaction: As the father lay dying, he left one hundred kahāpaṇas (gold coins) with his younger son to be given to the ordained son to purchase a bowl and robes. When the monk returned to Sāvatthi, his brother wept and presented him with the money.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Although the monk initially rejected the money, doubt and disaffection (arati) took root in his mind. He began to think he could disrobe, use the hundred kahāpaṇas as capital for a lay enterprise (like farming), and live comfortably without begging for alms. Consequently, he neglected his studies, meditation, and routines, becoming wan and physically pale.

The Result / Resolution: Escorted by fellow monks to the Buddha, the Buddha employed a skill-in-means (upāya-kosalla): He had the monk gather a hundred pebbles to calculate lay expenses (50 for basic food/sustenance, 24 for oxen, plus costs for a plough, spade, sickle, etc.). The 100 pebbles proved grossly insufficient even for basic capital. The Buddha then recounted the Mandhātā Jātaka—showing that even King Mandhātā, who ruled thirty-six heavenly realms and could make a rain of seven treasures fall, died unsatisfied. Upon hearing Dhammapada stanza 186, the monk saw the fruitlessness of sensual desire and recognized the trouble of household existence.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Multiplication of Scarcity through Craving (Taṇhā and Dukkha)

Worldly people falsely believe that eliminating poverty or acquiring wealth brings true happiness. However, as long as craving (taṇhā) is present, lack and scarcity only expand. Craving functions like a growing vine—the more one receives, the more the demand for "yet more" grows, rendering complete satisfaction impossible through material gains.

2. Taṇhakkhaya (Destruction of Craving) as the Core Definition of Nibbāna

In both His first sermon (Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta) and Dhammapada verse 186, the Buddha identifies Nibbāna as taṇhakkhaya (the exhaustion or extinguishment of thirst). True disciples of the Buddha (sammāsambuddhasāvaka) do not delight even in divine or heavenly sensual bliss (dibbesu kāmesu); their sole delight lies in the eradication of craving.

3. Monastic "Death" vs. Skillful Means (Upāya-Kosalla)

In Noble parlance (ariya-vohāra), disrobing and returning to the lay life is categorized as "death," and money is likened to a dangerous "serpent." Through the practical demonstration of calculating household living costs using pebbles, the Buddha revealed the immense struggle to balance income and expenditure, restoring the monk's commitment to the Holy Life.

4. The Seven Similes for the Disadvantages of Sensual Pleasures (Kāmādīnava)

Drawing from the Potaliya Sutta, the Buddha presents seven striking similes to illustrate that sensual gratification (assāda) is minor, while the disadvantages (ādīnava) and suffering (dukkha) are vast:

Simile of the Meatless Bone (aṭṭhikaṅkala): Like a hungry dog gnawing a bare bone smeared with a trace of blood, which cuts its own mouth and leaves its hunger unappeased.

Simile of the Piece of Meat (maṃsapesi): Like a bird carrying meat while a flock of vultures attacks it; failing to let go results in death or severe injury (reflecting intense social competition).

Simile of the Grass Torch (tiṇukā): Like carrying a fiery grass torch against the wind, which inevitably burns the hand of the person who holds it.

Simile of the Pit of Glowing Embers (aṅgārakāsu): Like a man dragged against his will by two strong men into a pit of embers (representing being dragged by one's own uncontrolled thoughts/desires).

Simile of Borrowed Goods (yācitaka): Like someone borrowing luxury clothes and vehicles to show off at a festival, only for the true owners to reclaim them, leaving the borrower empty and disgraced.

Simile of a Dream (supinakūpama): Like waking up from a dream of beautiful gardens only to find nothing there; sensual enjoyments are transient daydreams.

Simile of the Tree Laden with Fruit (rukkharūpama): Like climbing a tree for fruit while someone else chops it down at the root, leading to a disastrous fall.

5. Distinction Between Objective and Defilement Sensual Pleasures

Sensual pleasure operates on two levels: objective sensual objects (vatthu-kāma) and internal defilement-pleasure (kilesa-kāma). Wealth and money serve as an open invitation to lay life (vatthu-kāma), but the true cause of suffering is the internal defilement of craving within the mind.

6. The Gradual Path of Thinning Out Craving (Dāna, Sīla, Bhāvanā)

Dāna (Giving): Relinquishes objective attachment (vatthu-kāma) and cleanses the mind of the stain of stinginess (maccheramala), yielding spiritual joy (nirāmisa prīti) and delight in relinquishment (vossaggarato).

Sīla (Virtue): Disciplines body and speech through determination, giving rise to the happiness of blamelessness (anavajja-sukha).

Bhāvanā (Meditation): Calms the scattered mind on a sign of concentration (samādhi-nimitta) to address deep-seated defilements.

7. Root Cause of Craving: Ignorance (Avijjā) and Self-Illusion (Sakkāya-Diṭṭhi)

Craving is driven by the conceit of "I" (avijjā) and the concept of "Mine" (taṇhā). Objects in the external world do not create craving by themselves; rather, craving arises from within due to underlying tendencies (anusaya).

8. Restraint of Signs (Nimitta) and Accessory Features (Anuvyañjana)

The mind gets dragged into saṃsāric entanglement by grasping at minor features or signs (nimitta) and adding proliferation or accessory details (anuvyañjana). Through radical/wise attention (yoniso manasikāra), the meditator notes sensory inputs directly (e.g., "form, form," "sound, sound"), subduing attachment and establishing equanimity (upekkhā).

9. The Threefold Thirst as the Leash of Existence (Bhavanetti)

Craving manifests as craving for sensual pleasures (kāma-taṇhā), craving for continued existence (bhava-taṇhā), and craving for non-existence/annihilation (vibhava-taṇhā). Suicide or self-destruction (vibhava-taṇhā) cannot solve suffering; only the wisdom (paññā) of the Noble Eightfold Path can sever the leash of existence.

10. Completion of the Path through Right Deliverance (Sammā-Vimutti)

By anchoring oneself in the factors of enlightenment (bodhipakkhiya-dhamma)—faith, energy, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom (saddhā, viriya, sati, samādhi, paññā)—and penetrating the Four Noble Truths, the practitioner advances through the stages of noble attainment (magga-phala) to realize complete freedom from Saṃsāra in Nibbāna.
