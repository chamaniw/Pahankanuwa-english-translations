Sermon 006 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #6 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #6

Primary Theme: The gradual, moment-by-moment removal of mental defilements (anupubba-citta-visuddhi) through incremental practice (thoka thokaṃ khaṇe khaṇe), inspired by the intrinsic luminosity of the mind (pabhassara-citta) and driven by spiritual urgency (saṃvega).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: A faithful unnamed Brahmin living near a monastery assembly spot.

The Interaction: The Brahmin stood watching the monks gather early in the morning to adjust their robes before setting out into the village for alms-round.

The Disaster / Turning Point: The Brahmin observed a series of environmental hazards harassing the monks:

1. Morning dew on tall grass wet the hems of their robes.

2. Clearing the grass created dust that soiled their robes.

3. The harsh morning sun caused the monks to sweat excessively.

4. Sudden heavy rainstorms drenched the monks while assembling.

The Result / Resolution: Driven by faith, the Brahmin incrementally addressed each issue: he cleared the grass, spread white sand, built a small shelter, and ultimately constructed an assembly hall. Upon offering the hall and alms to the Buddha and the Saṅgha, he explained his step-by-step effort. The Buddha commended his gradual wholesome deeds and delivered Dhammapada Verse 239, illustrating how a wise person purifies their mind in the exact same step-by-step manner.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Material Homage (Āmisa-Pūjā) vs. Offering of Practice (Paṭipatti-Pūjā)

External decorations (flags, lanterns) and material offerings (flowers, lamps, food) are valid starting points. However, the Buddha explicitly praised internal decorations—cultivating virtue (sīla), concentration (samādhi), and wisdom (paññā)—as the superior offering of practice (paṭipatti-pūjā).

2. The Principle of Gradual Training (Anupubba-Sikkhā / Paṭipadā)

Spiritual progress is naturally sequential (anupubbena), likened to the great ocean (mahāsamudda), which slopes and deepens gradually rather than abruptly (na āyatakeneva papāto), or to climbing a staircase step-by-step. The Dispensation allows anyone to start at their current level and deepen their realization progressively.

3. The "Perception of Possibility" (Thoka Thokaṃ Khaṇe Khaṇe)

The phrase "little by little, moment by moment" generates a practical mindset of feasibility. It counters the common excuse of having "no time for Dhamma." Just as a water pot fills drop by drop (udabindu nipātena udakumbhopi pūrati), accumulating micro-moments of wholesome effort leads to profound spiritual realization.

4. The Luminous Mind (Pabhassara Citta) and Adventitious Defilements (Āgantuka Upakkilesa)

Drawing on the metaphor of a silversmith removing dross (kammāro rajatasseva), the Thero highlights that the mind is inherently radiant (pabhassarāmidaṃ cittaṃ), but obscured by external, visiting defilements (āgantukehi upakkilesehi upakkiliṭṭhaṃ). Discerning this latent purity provides inspiration and courage to systematically cleanse the mind-state (citta-santāna).

5. Self-Reflection via the Dhamma-Mirror (Dhammādāsa)

Citing the Ambalaṭṭhikā Rāhulovāda Sutta, the Thero emphasizes continuous self-examination (paccavekkhaṇā). Before, during, and after any action of body, speech, or mind, one must reflect in the Dhamma-mirror whether it leads to self-harm, harm to others, or harm to both (attabyābādhāya / parabyābādhāya / ubhayabyābādhāya). Unwholesome actions must be confessed and restrained in the future (āyati-saṃvara).

6. The Power of Short Practice-Intervals (Accharāsaṅghāta Sutta)

Developing loving-kindness (mettā), insight into impermanence (anicca-saññā), or recollections of the Triple Gem (buddhānussati, etc.) even for the brief duration of a finger-snap (accharāsaṅghāta-mattaṃ) ensures that a monk's practice is not empty and makes one worthy of consuming the world's alms-food.

7. Sources of Urgency (Saṃvega-Vatthu) as Spiritual Fuel

Contemplation of the eight sources of urgency—birth (jāti), aging (jarā), illness (vyādhi), death (cuti), lower realms (apāya), past/future rounds of suffering (vaṭṭa-dukkha), and the toil of seeking sustenance (āhāra-gavesi-dukkha)—acts as the essential fuel/speed (saṃvega) required to overcome spiritual laziness and delay.

8. Ignorance as the Ultimate Rust (Avijjā Paramaṃ Malaṃ)

While evil deeds are destructive, the Dhammapada identifies ignorance—the non-comprehension of the Four Noble Truths—as the supreme rust (avijjā paramaṃ malaṃ). Cleansing this primary impurity requires walking the Noble Eightfold Path.

9. Systematic Integration of the Noble Eightfold Path (Ariyassa Aṭṭhaṅgikassa Magga)

Path factors unfold interconnectedly: Right View (sammā-diṭṭhi) reorients one’s vision of life; Right Intention (sammā-saṅkappa) cleanses inner thoughts; Right Speech, Action, and Livelihood (sammā-vācā, kammanta, ājīva) purify external conduct; Right Effort (sammā-vāyāma) guards the unwholesome and fosters the wholesome; Right Mindfulness (sammā-sati) establishes the satipaṭṭhāna foundation; and Right Concentration (sammā-samādhi) unifies the entire personality (cittekaggatā).

10. Immediate Efficacy of the Dhamma (Svākkhāta-Guṇa)

The Dhamma is directly visible (sandiṭṭhika), timeless (akālika), inviting empirical verification (ehipassika), progressively inward-leading (opanayika), and personally verifiable by the wise (paccattaṃ veditabbo viññūhi). Realization does not depend on external bestowal, but occurs right on the spot when path factors are fulfilled.

11. Reflecting on the Boundlessness of Saṃsāra (Anamatagga Saṃyutta)

Citing the Buddha’s discourse to thirty monks from Pāvā, the sermon draws attention to the terrifying scope of rebirth—where the blood shed by a single being's decapitation across past animal and human lifetimes exceeds the water in the four great oceans. Recalling this instills non-attachment and propels immediate effort toward Nibbāna.
