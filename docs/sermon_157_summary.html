Sermon 157 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #157 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #157

Primary Theme: The nature of an Arahant’s liberated mind—which has no attachment to past, future, or present five aggregates (pañcakkhandha)—and how practicing the Middle Path (majjhimā paṭipadā) through insight (vipassanā) leads to the complete extinguishment of suffering in Nibbāna.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The lay devotee Visākha and his wife Dhammadinnā (later Theri Dhammadinnā).

The Interaction: Visākha listened to the Dhamma from the Buddha and attained the stage of Non-returner (anāgāmi-phala). Upon returning home, he acted in a quiet, detached manner—eating in silence and neglecting to greet Dhammadinnā from the window as was their custom. He then offered to hand over all his wealth and possessions to her so he could set himself free.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Refusing to accept the riches—which she likened to taking up a lump of spit spat out by her husband—Dhammadinnā requested permission to ordain. After receiving higher ordination (upasampadā), she retreated to a remote province to meditate and quickly attained Arahatship along with the four analytical knowledges (paṭisambhidā). When she returned to Rājagaha, Visākha questioned her on the stages of the path. She answered flawlessly from Stream-entry (sotāpatti) up to Arahatship (arahattamagga), but stopped him when he asked what lies beyond Nibbāna, noting that he had overstepped the limits of questioning.

The Result / Resolution: Visākha reported the entire dialogue to the Buddha. The Blessed One praised Dhammadinnā's wisdom, confirmed her answers, declared her foremost among bhikkhunīs who are Dhamma-speakers (dhammakathikā), and uttered Dhammapada verse 421 in praise of her Arahatship. (This dialogue is preserved as the Cūḷavedalla Sutta of the Majjhima Nikāya).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Dhammapada Verse 421 & The Definition of a "Brahmin" (Brāhmaṇa)

Pali Stanza: "Yassa pure ca pacchā ca, majjhe ca natthi kiñcanaṃ; Akiñcanaṃ anādānaṃ, tamahaṃ brūmi brāhmaṇaṃ."

The Buddha uses the term "Brahmin" to denote an Arahant. An Arahant is one who has no attachment, desire, or longing (kiñcanaṃ) for past aggregates (pure), future aggregates (pacchā), or present aggregates (majjhe), being entirely possessionless (akiñcana) and unattached (anādāna).

2. Renunciation in Household Life (Nekkhamma-saṅkappa)

The story illustrates how the thought of renunciation (nekkhamma-saṅkappa), a primary component of Right Resolution (sammā-saṅkappa), can mature within a lay household. Visākha peacefully facilitated his wife's ordination, while Dhammadinnā treated material wealth as a "lump of spit" (kheḷapiṇḍa), renouncing it without hesitation to seek liberation.

3. Deep Doctrinal Meaning of Kiñcana and *Akiñcana*

Beyond material possessions, kiñcana deeply refers to greed (lobha), hatred (dosa), and delusion (moha). Things in the world become significant "things" to an individual through these three defilements. Because an Arahant is completely free from greed, hatred, and delusion, they are true akiñcana (owning/possessing nothing).

4. The Present Moment (Majjhe) as the Deepest Point of Insight

While past Preparations (saṅkhāra) are gone and future ones have not arrived, the present is the hardest point to penetrate due to the subtle conceit "I am" (asmi-māna). In mindfulness of breathing (ānāpānasati), one must anchor mindfulness right at the point of contact (parimukhaṃ satiṃ upaṭṭhapetvā) without chasing the incoming or outgoing breath into past or future.

5. Similes for Maintaining Mindfulness in the Middle

The Two-handed Saw Simile: Just as woodcutters must look strictly at the point where the saw teeth cut the log—rather than at each other or the ends of the saw—a meditator must keep attention anchored at the touch-point (nose/upper lip).

The Swing Simile: Just as a person pushing a swing must keep their eyes fixed in the middle rather than following the swing to its extreme ends, a meditator stays focused on the middle point of breath contact to catch the sign (nimitta).

6. Analysis of Feelings (Vedanā) and Underlying Tendencies (Anusaya)

Pleasant feeling (sukha vedanā) harbors the underlying tendency of lust (rāgānusaya).

Painful feeling (dukkha vedanā) harbors the underlying tendency of aversion/hatred (paṭighānusaya).

Neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling (adukkhamasukha vedanā) harbors the underlying tendency of ignorance (avijjānusaya).

When ignorance is eradicated through insight, neutral feeling is directly experienced as a peaceful, sublime state superior to coarse worldly pleasure.

7. The Limit of Questioning and the Nature of Nibbāna

When Visākha asked what lies beyond Nibbāna, Dhammadinnā explained that he had overstepped the limit. Nibbāna is the ultimate culmination (brahmacariya) of the spiritual life; there is nothing beyond it. Nibbāna is not a physical world or place, but the extinguishment (nivīma) of the fires of birth (jāti), decay (jarā), and death (maraṇa).

8. The Five Aggregates as Māra (The Rādha Sutta)

As shown in the Buddha's advice to Ven. Rādha, form (rūpa), feeling (vedanā), perception (saññā), Preparations (saṅkhāra), and consciousness (viññāṇa) are themselves Māra (Death/impermanence).

Everyday life is a continuous "sword fight" against Māra. Seeing the aggregates as Māra constitutes Right View (sammā-dassana), which produces revulsion (nibbidā), dispassion (virāga), liberation (vimutti), and extinguishment (Nibbāna).

9. Perception of Compactness (Ghana-saññā) vs. Knowledge of Dissolution (Bhaṅga-ñāṇa)

Due to the perception of compactness (ghana-saññā), uninstructed people misperceive the rapidly changing flow of physical and mental events as a solid self ("I am").

Through insight (vipassanā), meditators develop the knowledge of dissolution (bhaṅga-ñāṇa), perceiving that Preparations break apart far faster than they arise (phussa phussa vayaṃ passaṃ), leading to disenchantment.

10. Three Characteristics of the Liberated Mind

The state of liberation and Nibbāna is described through three terms:

Animitta (signless): Free from the signs created by greed, hatred, and delusion.

Appaṇihita (desireless/undirected): Free from all worldly aspirations and longings.

Suññata (void/empty): Devoid of any core of self or permanent substance.

11. Nibbāna as Unfelt Pleasure (Avedayita-Sukha)

Worldly beings equate happiness with feeling (vedanā). However, supramundane happiness (lokuttara-sukha) is avedayita-sukha (pleasure unfelt)—a supreme, tranquil bliss that exists when one is entirely freed from feeling and the conceit "I am" (asmi-māna).

12. The Noble Eightfold Path as the Practical Middle Path

The Eightfold Path avoids both the extreme of devotion to sensual pleasures (kāmasukhallikānuyoga) and the extreme of self-mortification (attakilamathānuyoga). By starting with Right View (sammā-diṭṭhi), it removes ignorance (avijjā), cultivates equanimity, and provides the framework to escape the suffering (dukkha) of saṃsāra.
