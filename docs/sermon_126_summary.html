Sermon 126 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #126 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #126

Primary Theme: The absolute inescapability and automated retribution of the wheel of kamma (kammacakka / kamma-yanta), and how the Wheel of Dhamma (dhammacakka) through the Noble Eightfold Path is the sole means to untangle the knot of saṃsāra.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Three groups of monks who witnessed or experienced distinct catastrophes while traveling to see the Blessed Buddha at Jetavana Monastery, along with the past-life figures whose actions caused these events:

1. A farmer in Sāvatthī (reborn as a crow).

2. A householder’s wife in Bārāṇasī (reborn as a ship captain’s wife).

3. Seven cowherd boys (reborn as seven monks).

The Interaction: Monks traveling from distant regions met on the road to Jetavana and shared three strange occurrences they had witnessed, agreeing to ask the Buddha to explain the hidden karmic causes behind them.

The Disaster / Turning Point:

1. Incident 1: A crow flying over a village was caught mid-air by a floating ring of burning thatch from a hearth, burnt to death, and fell down.

2. Incident 2: A sea vessel inexplicably froze in the middle of the ocean. Lots drawn three times identified the captain's beautiful young wife as the source of bad luck; she was stripped of her ornaments, bound with a basket of sand around her neck, and thrown overboard to drown.

3. Incident 3: Seven guest monks sleeping in a cave were trapped for seven days without food or water when a massive boulder rolled down and sealed the entrance, rolling away on its own on the seventh day.

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha revealed the past karmic retributions:

1. The farmer had burnt an untamable ox alive with straw; after boiling in hell, he was burnt in mid-air as a crow for seven lifetimes.

2. The householder’s wife had tied a sand-filled basket to her loyal dog’s neck and drowned it in a well out of anger at being teased; after boiling in hell, she was drowned with a sand basket around her neck for 100 lifetimes.

3. The seven boys had trapped a monitor lizard in an anthill with twigs for seven days before releasing it; because they did not kill it and showed compassion, they avoided hell but suffered seven days of starvation while trapped for 14 lifetimes.

Upon revealing these causes, the Buddha proclaimed Dhammapada stanza 127, emphasizing that no location in the universe—whether sky, ocean, or mountain cave—can shelter anyone from the retributions of evil deeds.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Automated Machine of Kamma (Kamma-yanta)

Kamma is not an arbitrary system imposed by an external judge, but an automated mechanism (kamma-yanta). Volition (cetanā) deposits seeds in consciousness (viññāṇa), creating an attractive force that automatically draws together the necessary elemental elements (earth, water, fire, wind), individuals, and circumstances in the universe to execute its retribution.

2. The Inescapability of Retribution

There is no physical refuge anywhere in the cosmos—neither in the sky, mid-ocean, nor inside mountain clefts—to escape unwholesome kamma. Even enlightened beings, such as Venerable Maha Moggallāna (killed by bandits due to past matricide/patricide) and Venerable Aṅgulimāla (struck by stray stones), experienced physical retributions of residual past evil deeds.

3. Self-Constructed Death Traps (Kammasakatā)

The dangerous circumstances and "death traps" encountered in life are self-made. By acting out of greed, hatred, or delusion, one acts as one's own worst enemy. The universe merely aligns to exhaust the volitional energy generated by one's own actions.

4. Exponential Amplification of Karmic Retribution

Just as a small plant seed grows into a vast tree, a minor act of unwholesome volition produces retributions magnified thousands of times over long aeons (kappa). Unwholesome roots (lobha, dosa, moha) draw continuous force into the karmic seed, prolonging samsaric suffering.

5. The Entangled Nature of Saṃsāra (Jaṭā)

Saṃsāra is an intricate "inner and outer tangle" (anto jaṭā bahi jaṭā), like a tangled skein of thread (tantākulakajātā). When performing evil deeds, external individuals are dragged in as instruments of retribution. Unaware of the underlying karmic law, victims respond with fresh hatred and revenge, compounding the knot indefinitely.

6. The Counter-Wheel: Dhammacakka vs. Kammacakka

The automatic revolving wheel of kamma (kammacakka) can only be halted or weakened by revolving the Wheel of Dhamma (dhammacakka). This counter-effort consists of the practice of virtue (sīla), concentration (samādhi), and wisdom (paññā).

7. The Psychological Function of Virtue (Sīla)

Virtue (such as the Five Precepts or Ājīva-aṭṭhamaka-sīla) is a mental technique to tame body and speech. Undertaking training precepts (sikkhāpada) instills a firm determination in the mind, enabling wisdom to issue restraining commands when unwholesome emotional impulses urge one to harm living beings.

8. Concentration (Samādhi) as Mental Power

The ordinary mind is scattered like unchanneled water. By applying a wholesome meditation subject (kammaṭṭhāna—such as buddhānussati, mettā, or maraṇānussati), the mind is confined and concentrated (citta-bhāvanā), generating the inner power required to calm restless mental preparations (saṅkhāra).

9. Insight Wisdom (Vipassanā-paññā) and De-construction of Self

The underlying root of evil volition is the illusion of self (sakkāya-diṭṭhi) and the notion of "I" and "mine" rooted in ignorance (avijjā). Direct penetrative seeing (vipassanā) of the Three Characteristics—Impermanence (anicca), Suffering (dukkha), and Not-self (anattā)—extinguishes self-illusion and dries up the karmic seeds of future existences.

10. The Upstream Path (Paṭisotagāmī)

The Dhamma path runs directly counter to the worldly stream of craving (bhava-taṇhā). Escaping saṃsāra requires active, deliberate Right Effort (sammā-vāyāma) to swim against the current of unwholesome habits, progressively weakening karmic forces through the four noble stages of awakening (sotāpatti, sakadāgāmī, anāgāmī, arahatta).
