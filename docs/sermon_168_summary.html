Sermon 168 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #168 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #168 (Sinhala Audio Sermon No. 120)

Primary Theme: Penetrating the illusory nature of birth (jāti) and becoming (bhava) through dependent co-arising (paṭiccasamuppāda), mind-centric reality (manomayā), and ascending the "palace of wisdom" (paññā-pāsāda) by purifying the naturally luminous mind (pabhassara citta).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Venerable Mahā Kassapa Thera (and the Buddha observing him from Jetavana Monastery).

The Interaction: Venerable Mahā Kassapa Thera returned to Pipphalī Cave in Rājagaha after his alms-round, completed his meal, and projected his divine eye (dibba-cakkhu) to observe beings passing away (cuti) and being born (jāti) across various realms (earth, water, etc.). Concurrently, the Buddha at Jetavana projected a ray of light to address Venerable Mahā Kassapa.

The Disaster / Turning Point: The Buddha noted that observing the full scope of beings passing away and arising—especially obscure cases like unannounced embryonic deaths—belongs strictly to the infinite domain of Buddha-knowledge (Buddha-ñāṇa). However, to praise Venerable Mahā Kassapa’s meditative abiding and realization of Arahantship, the Buddha uttered Dhammapada Verse 28.

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha declared that when a wise person dispels negligence through diligence, ascending the sorrowless "palace of wisdom" (paññā-pāsāda), they look down upon the suffering, ignorant world (sokiniṃ pajaṃ) like a steadfast person standing atop a mountain looking at those below on the ground.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Vision of Knowledge (Ñāṇa-Dassana) vs. Physical Vision:

Unlike physical tools (microscopes, telescopes) that rely on external light and physical sense organs, spiritual vision relies on the internally generated light of absorption (jhāna-āloka) in the fourth absorption (catuttha jhāna). This produces an uncovered, unobstructed, luminous mind (sappabhāsaṃ cittaṃ). However, the divine eye (dibba-cakkhu) is secondary to the ultimate knowledge of the destruction of taints (āsavakkhaya-ñāṇa), which completely ends birth (jāti).

2. Critique of Antarābhava (Intermediate State) and Western Speculations:

Focusing excessively on theories of an intermediate state (antarābhava), hypnotism, and Western psychological theories traps practitioners in self-illusion (sakkāya-diṭṭhi) and soul-views. The core problem is not an intermediate state, but the suffering inherent in becoming itself (bhava-dukkha), which must be understood through dependent co-arising (paṭiccasamuppāda).

3. The "First Photograph" and Embryonic Grasping (Kalala Form):

The first physical manifestation in a mother’s womb is the kalala form (a mud-like stain). Rebirth occurs when a slipping consciousness (viññāṇa) objectifies an image based on Name-and-Form (nāmarūpa). Driven by craving for becoming (bhava-taṇhā), consciousness grasps (upādāna) the womb (gabbha), taking root in dependence on the mother's four elements.

4. Craving (Taṇhā) as the Fuel/Grasping Element (Vacchagotta Sutta):

Referencing the conversation with the wanderer Vacchagotta, birth (jāti) occurs only where there is fuel/grasping (upādāna), just as fire burns only with fuel. When a being lays down the physical body at death, craving (taṇhā) itself serves as the fuel/grasping element (upādāna) connecting consciousness to a new existence without requiring a migrating soul (jīva).

5. Simile of the Deer and the Mirage (Saññā-Khandha):

The entire aggregate of perception (saññā-kkhandha) is compared to a mirage (marīci). Just as a deer misinterprets a mirage created by environmental factors as real water and runs after it (creating a false duality of "I am here, water is over there"), ignorant beings create a dualistic split between subject and object. Consciousness recedes endlessly as one chases sense objects across all six sense doors.

6. The Four Primary Elements (Mahābhūta) as Void Hauntings:

The four primary elements—earth (pathavī), water (āpo), fire (tejo), and air (vāyo)—do not exist as separate, isolated physical entities. They are interconnected, void (suñña), and function as "hauntings" or mental projections. The world misinterprets their qualities (hardness, heat) as permanent, solid things due to perverted perception (vipallāsa) and the perception of permanence (nicca-saññā).

7. Mind-Centric Philosophy (Manopubbaṅgamā Dhammā) and Refutation of Materialism:

Buddhism is strictly mind-centric, as stated in Dhammapada 1: mind precedes all phenomena, mind is supreme, and phenomena are mind-made (manomayā). Physical science incorrectly assumes dhammapubbaṅgamo mano (matter precedes mind). This principle is illustrated by Venerable Kumāra Kassapa’s debate with Chieftain Pāyāsi: a soul cannot be trapped or seen escaping a sealed pot, because rebirth operates like a dream world—driven by mental karma-energy (kamma-vega / cetanā), not physical movement through space.

8. Deconstruction of Preparations (Saṅkhāra) via Insight:

The world grasps at Volitional Formations/Preparations (saṅkhāra) as if they contain a solid core. The Buddha compares saṅkhāra to the trunk of a banana tree (kadali-kkhandha). Through systematic inspection (sammasana) in insight meditation (vipassanā), one peels away the layers and realizes there is no heartwood/core—only empty, impermanent sheaths.

9. Penetrative Wisdom of Arising and Passing Away (Udayatthagāminī Paññā):

True liberation requires cultivating insight into arising and passing away (udayabbaya). As contemplation quickens, the impression of solid "things" dissolves into a rapid stream of continuous movement. Seeing through Name-and-Form (nāmarūpa) breaks the sign (nimitta), turning consciousness into a non-manifestative state (anidassana-viññāṇa) free from image-casting.

10. The Palace of Wisdom (Paññā-Pāsāda) and the Luminous Mind (Pabhassara Citta):

Ascending the "palace of wisdom" does not mean accumulating external academic knowledge (like climbing a multi-story library). It refers to uncovering the mind's naturally luminous state (pabhassara citta) by systematically scrubbing away visiting defilements (āgantuka upakkilesa)—lust (rāga), hatred (dosa), and delusion (moha)—just as one cleans rust off a copper vessel using virtue (sīla), concentration (samādhi), and wisdom (paññā).

11. Interdependence of Consciousness and Name-and-Form (Viññāṇa-Nāmarūpa):

As Venerable Sāriputta explained, consciousness (viññāṇa) and Name-and-Form (nāmarūpa) are mutually dependent, like two sheaves of reeds (naḷakalāpa) propped against each other; if one is pulled away, the other falls. They are also compared to a blind man carrying a lame man. Wise attention (yoniso manasikāra) reflects on the source of their arising, halting the dualistic running in saṃsāra.
