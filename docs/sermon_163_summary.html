Sermon 163 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #163 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #163 (Audio Sermon No. 125)

Primary Theme: The critical necessity of grounding faith (saddhā) in wisdom (paññā) and wise reflection (yoniso manasikāra) to avoid blind devotion; the criteria for evaluating true virtue, purity, fortitude, and wisdom; and the distinction between fruitful right effort (sammā-vāyāma) in a well-expounded Dispensation versus futile wrong effort (micchā-vāyāma) in misplaced or commercialized practices.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: King Pasenadi of Kosala, the Buddha, and thirty-five non-Buddhist ascetics belonging to five distinct sectarian groups (seven matted-hair ascetics [jaṭilas], seven Jains [nigaṇṭhas], seven naked ascetics [acelakas], seven single-robe ascetics [ekasāṭakas], and seven wanderers [paribbājakas]).

The Interaction: While the Buddha is standing in seclusion outside the gate of the Eastern Monastery (Pūrvārāma) in Sāvatthi after his evening meditation, King Pasenadi approaches and pays homage. Suddenly, thirty-five ascetics with long hair, body hair, nails, and carrying monastic requisites walk past. King Pasenadi stands up, turns toward them, clasps his hands in reverence, and announces his name three times: "Venerable sirs, I am King Pasenadi of Kosala."

The Disaster / Turning Point: After the ascetics depart, King Pasenadi declares to the Buddha that if there are any Arahants or individuals on the path to Arahantship in the world, these ascetics must be among them. The King mistakes outward ascetic attire and severe appearances for genuine spiritual realization. He reinforces this superficial standard by describing his own covert royal spies—criminals who commit covert acts of intrigue in distant provinces, yet later wash off the dust, put on fine white garments, apply ointments, shave their beards, and enjoy sensual pleasures unnoticed as fine gentlemen.

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha gently rebukes the King’s naive judgment, pointing out that a householder immersed in sensual pleasures, family life, Kāsi silk, perfumes, and money cannot easily discern who is an Arahant. The Buddha expounds four golden rules of evaluation—requiring long association, careful attention, and wisdom—and utters an Udāna solemn utterance (Udāna 6.4) warning against misplaced effort, subservience, dependency, and commercializing the Dhamma.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Faith (Saddhā) Must Be Firmly Established Through Wisdom (Paññā)

Faith that lacks the guidance of wisdom degenerates into blind faith, mere emotional devotion, or superficial admiration of external appearances. True faith (ākāravatī saddhā) functions as an initial catalyst for energy (viriya), but must be continuously tested, refined, and perfected through personal practice and wise reflection (yoniso manasikāra).

2. Four Golden Principles for Assessing Spiritual Integrity

The Buddha presents four principles to discern a person’s true spiritual standing, emphasizing that each requires long-term observation, close attention, and wisdom:

Virtue (Sīla): To be known through living together (saṃvāsa).

Purity (Soceyya): To be known through social intercourse and conversation (saṃvohāra).

Fortitude/Strength (Thāma): To be known through times of adversity (āpadāsu).

Wisdom (Paññā): To be known through open discussion (sākacchā).

3. Exegesis of Udāna Stanza 6.4

Na vāyameyya sabbattha: One should not exert effort everywhere; effort must be directed to the right place and teaching.

Nāññassa puriso siyā: One should not be another’s man or servant; one must avoid blind subservience to sectarian leaders or worldly rulers.

Nāññaṃ nissāya jīveyya: One should not live dependent on another in a state of spiritual subjection.

Dhammena na vāṇijaṃ care: One should not trade in the Dhamma or turn the teachings into a commercial commodity.

4. The Gravity of Wrong View (Micchā-Diṭṭhi) vs. Right View (Sammā-Diṭṭhi)

The Buddha states that wrong view is the greatest of all faults (micchādiṭṭhiparamāhaṃ... vajjaṃ vadāmi). All physical (kāya-kamma), verbal (vacī-kamma), and mental actions (mano-kamma), as well as intentions (cetanā), aspirations (patthanā), and volitional formations (saṅkhāra) rooted in wrong view, inevitably yield disagreeable, painful results—just as a planted seed of neem (kohomba) or bitter gourd (karavila) converts all soil nutrients into a bitter taste. Conversely, actions rooted in right view yield sweet, agreeable fruits, like seeds of hill-paddy (häl), sugarcane, or grape.

5. Futile Effort (Micchā-Vāyāma) vs. Fruitful Effort (Sammā-Vāyāma)

Exerting energy within an ill-expounded Dispensation (durakkhāta) is wasteful and brings suffering to the energetic practitioner while the indolent person dwells happily. Using the four similes taught to Venerable Bhūmija, the Buddha illustrates futile effort:

Squeezing moist sand to extract oil.

Pulling a young cow by the horn to get milk.

Churning pure water in a vessel to get butter.

Rubbing a wet, water-logged stick with a fire-drill to kindle fire.

Conversely, exerting effort in a well-expounded Dhamma (svākkhāta)—like squeezing sesame seeds, pulling a cow's udder, churning curd, or rubbing dry wood—generates unworldly joy (nirāmisā pīti), concentration (samādhi), and path-fruits.

6. Pure vs. Impure Preaching of the Dhamma (Dhamma-Desanā)

Teaching the Dhamma to gain requisites, fame, or material offerings (āmissa) constitutes commercializing the Dhamma (dhammena na vāṇijaṃ care). A pure Dhamma teacher must establish five internal qualities:

Preaching in a progressive sequence (anupubbikathaṃ).

Expounding reasons and causes (pariyāyadassāvī).

Motivated by kindness and compassion (anuddayataṃ paṭicca).

Free from expectation of material gain (na āmisantaro).

Preaching without self-praise or disparagement of others.

7. Fortitude (Thāma) and the Eight Worldly Conditions (Aṭṭha-Loka-Dhamma)

A person's fortitude is revealed during life calamities such as the loss of relatives (ñāti-vyasana), loss of wealth (bhoga-vyasana), or severe illness (roga-vyasana). An unwise person loses balance, weeps, wails, and faints. A wise person recognizes that the world revolves around the eight worldly conditions—gain (lābha) and loss (alābha), disrepute (ayasa) and fame (yasa), blame (nindā) and praise (pasaṃsā), pleasure (sukha) and pain (dukkha)—analgous to a Ferris wheel (kathuru oñchillāva), maintaining an unshakeable mind.

8. Discerning Wisdom Through Discussion (Sākacchā)

Wisdom is evaluated by how a person presents arguments, answers questions, and engages with deep topics. The Buddha compares this evaluation to a sighted man watching ripples on a lake:

The Foolish Person: Handled topics reveal a lack of depth, missing the peaceful, subtle, beyond-logic (atakkāvacara) Dhamma. Like a small fish (kaṇayā) surfacing, its minor ripples reveal its small size.

The Wise Person: Explains deep, subtle matters briefly or in detail with clarity. Like a large fish (lūlā) surfacing, its powerful movement reveals its magnitude.

9. The Two Conditions for Arising Right View

Right view (sammā-diṭṭhi) depends on two distinct conditions:

The voice of another (paratoghosa): Hearing or reading the true Dhamma from an external source.

Radical/Wise attention (yoniso manasikāra): Deeply reflecting and investigating the root causes of the teaching. Hearing Dhamma without wise reflection leads only to passive acceptance and blind devotion.

10. The Elephant-Tracker Simile and Verified Faith (Cūḷahatthipadopama Sutta)

Faith (saddhā) acts as an initial guide. Like an elephant tracker who spots footprint after footprint in a dense forest, a practitioner does not leap to premature conclusions nor abandon the search. As more evidence is gathered through progressive practice, faith is confirmed step by step until complete certainty (ākāravatī saddhā) is established upon realizing Nibbāna.

11. The 37 Factors of Enlightenment (Bodhipakkhiya-Dhamma) as the Practical Core

The Buddha summarized his entire teaching into the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, divided into seven groups: the 4 Foundations of Mindfulness (satipaṭṭhāna), 4 Right Efforts (sammappadhānā), 4 Bases of Power (iddhipādā), 5 Spiritual Faculties (indriya), 5 Spiritual Powers (bala), 7 Factors of Enlightenment (bojjhaṅgā), and the Noble Eightfold Path (ariya aṭṭhaṅgika magga). These thirty-seven factors are practical, experiential mental qualities to be cultivated through direct practice, rather than mere items of intellectual study.

12. The Dhamma-Vinaya as the Ever-Present Teacher

Following the Buddha's Parinibbāna, the Dhamma and Vinaya remain the Supreme Teacher. Because the Dhamma is well-expounded (svākkhāta), its results are visible here and now (sandiṭṭhika), timeless (akālika), inviting all to come and see (ehipassika), leading progressively onward (opanayika), and to be experienced individually by the wise (paccattaṃ veditabbo viññūhi). By turning away from the mirage of craving (taṇhā) and eliminating ignorance (avijjā), the practitioner applies right effort (sammā-vāyāma) to realize the paths and fruits of Stream-entry, Once-returning, Non-returning, and Arahantship.
