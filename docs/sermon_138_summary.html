Sermon 138 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #138 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #138 (Sinhala Audio Dhamma Sermon No. 48)

Primary Theme: The distinct spiritual qualities of a true person (sappurisa-guṇa), the power of past merit and sufficing conditions (upanissaya-sampatti), the supreme value of the Seven Noble Treasures (sapta-ariya-dhana), and how perfected noble deportment (ākappa-sampatti) transforms wrong views and leads to Nibbāna.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Cūla Subhaddā, the second daughter of Treasurer Anāthapiṇḍika of Sāvatthī, who was a devoted Sotāpanna (stream-enterer) laywoman.

The Interaction: Anāthapiṇḍika and Treasurer Ugga of Ugga city had a childhood pact to marry their children to one another. Upon seeing Cūla Subhaddā's refined deportment and hospitality, Ugga requested her in marriage for his son. Although Ugga was a follower of naked ascetics (Nigaṇṭhas), the Buddha foresaw the inherent Dhamma law (dhammatā) and permitted the alliance. Cūla Subhaddā moved to Ugga city accompanied by eight reputable noblemen as guardians.

The Disaster / Turning Point: During wedding celebrations, Ugga invited unclothed Acelakas (naked Nigaṇṭha ascetics) and asked Cūla Subhaddā to pay them homage. Disgusted by their shamelessness, she refused to participate. Ugga became enraged and threatened to throw her out. The eight noble guardians acquitted her of any fault. When her mother-in-law asked what kind of ascetics she truly revered, Cūla Subhaddā recited verses praising the serene bodily, verbal, and mental conduct of the Buddha and His Arahants. Challenged to show them, she threw eight handfuls of jasmine flowers into the sky from her roof, making a solemn determination of faith to invite the Buddha for alms.

The Result / Resolution: The jasmine flowers miraculously travelled through the air to form a canopy over the Buddha at Jetavana Monastery. The Buddha uttered Dhammapada Verse 304, explaining that true persons (sappurisā) shine from afar like the Himalayas. The next morning, the Buddha and five hundred Arahants traveled through the air to Ugga city. Following a seven-day grand alms-giving and Dhamma discourse, thousands of inhabitants converted to the Dhamma, and a great Arahant was stationed in Ugga city to establish the Sāsana there.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Nature of a True Person (Sappurisa-guṇa) and Sufficing Conditions (Upanissaya-sampatti)

A true person (sappurisa) in the Dhamma is not merely someone who avoids overt wrongdoing, but an individual rich in past wholesome merit (puñña) and spiritual potential (upanissaya-sampatti). Such individuals possess an intrinsic spiritual power that draws the attention of the Buddha's eye, making them visible even across vast distances (dūre santo pakāsenti). In contrast, untrue persons (asappurisā) remain spiritually invisible to the noble ones despite close physical proximity (asant’ettha na dissanti), like an arrow shot in pitch darkness.

2. The Aspiration for Association with the Wise (Sappurisa-saṃsevo)

The standard Buddhist verse recited after meritorious actions ("Iminā puñña-kammena mā me bāla-samāgamo...") expresses a deep aspiration not just for this lifetime, but "until Nibbāna" (yāva-Nibbāna). Associating with true persons (sappurisa-saṃsevo) is the primary aid and the first factor of stream-entry (sotāpatti-aṅga). It leads directly to hearing the true Dhamma (saddhamma-savana), wise reflection (yoniso manasikāra), and practice in accordance with the Dhamma (dhammānudhamma-paṭipatti).

3. Distinction Between Wholesome (Kusala) and Meritorious (Puñña)

Kusala signifies ultimate skillfulness—the ability to turn away from unwholesome roots (lobha, dosa, moha) and navigate out of the terrifying wilderness of saṃsāra. Puñña represents the beneficial, fruit-bearing energy generated through wholesome actions. While worldly currencies constantly depreciate due to economic inflation, merit functions as an inexhaustible, eternal treasure (acoraharaṇo nidhī) that follows the mental continuum like an un-stealable shadow (anugāmiko).

4. Perfection of Deportment (Ākappa-sampatti) and Ascetic Qualities (Samaṇa-guṇa)

The visual impact of disciplined physical and verbal behavior (ākappa-sampatti) is vital for sustaining confidence in the Sāsana. Monastics are admonished to reflect constantly: "Añño me ākappa karaṇīyo" ("My deportment must be distinct from that of a layperson"). Cūla Subhaddā inspired her family by highlighting how Arahants remain composed, with downcast eyes (okkhittacakkhū) and measured speech (mitabhāṇī), unswayed by the eight worldly conditions (aṭṭha-lokadhamma). This contrasts sharply with the ignoble, unrefined practices of wrong-believers (such as those observing canine vows).

5. The Purpose of Disciplinary Rules (Vinaya-nīti)

The Buddha promulgated Vinaya rules for two distinct social and spiritual objectives: to generate confidence in those who currently lack faith (appasannānaṃ vā pasādāya) and to deepen the devotion of those who already possess faith (pasannānaṃ vā bhiyyobhāvāya). True spiritual values do not pander to worldly popularity or foolish praise, as "better is the censure of the wise than the praise of fools" (garahā va viññūhi yañce bālappasaṃsanaṃ).

6. The Seven Noble Treasures (Sapta-Ariya-Dhana)

True wealth consists of internal spiritual assets rather than material possessions:

Faith (Saddhā-dhana): The primary organ and foundation, likened to an elephant's trunk.

Morality (Sīla-dhana): Disciplined physical and verbal conduct that prevents mental degeneration into animalistic or demonic states.

Moral Shame (Hiri-dhana) & Moral Dread (Ottappa-dhana): Divine qualities (deva-dhamma) that cause the mind to shrink away from wrongdoing and fear its harmful consequences.

Learning (Suta-dhana): Understanding the Buddha's teachings to purify one's view (diṭṭhijukamma).

Generosity/Renunciation (Cāga-dhana): The internal power of letting go, which protects the mind during sudden real-world losses.

Wisdom (Paññā-dhana): The ultimate seventh treasure that penetrates impermanence and cuts through delusion (moha).

7. Gradual Renunciation from External Giving to the Internal Aggregates

The practice of generosity (cāga) serves as the essential preparation for ultimate liberation. By first relinquishing external property, one gradually builds the mental capacity to abandon unwholesome verbal/physical habits, eventually giving up attachment to the five aggregates (pañcakkhandha): form (rūpa), feeling (vedanā), perception (saññā), mental formations (saṅkhāra), and consciousness (viññāṇa).

8. Simile of the Heated Iron Plate: Unmasking Saṃsāric Thrashing

Sensory existence and worldly entertainments are fundamentally deceptive. To the uncultivated mind, worldly activities, music, and dance appear delightful; however, the Buddha famously observed: "Ummatthakamidaṃ naccaṃ" ("Dance is like madness, and song is like weeping"). To one practicing insight (vipassanā), human beings thrashing about in saṃsāra under the distress of constant friction and preparations (saṅkhāra) resemble actors forced to jump around on a red-hot iron plate—what appears from a distance to be a pleasant dance is in truth frantic movement driven by intolerable pain.

9. Insatiability of Craving (Taṇhā-dāso)

The mundane world is inherently deficient and incapable of offering lasting contentment: "Ūṇo loko atitto, taṇhā-dāso" ("The world is incomplete, insatiable, a slave to craving"). Craving acts like a fire that burns hotter as more fuel is added. Real peace is not achieved by attempting to fulfill endless desires, but by stilling the process of volitional preparation (saṅkhāra-upasama) through disenchantment (nibbidā).

10. Mental Quietude (Citta-viveka) via Moral Discipline and Meditation

Spiritual leisure and quietude are not geographic or social states, but internal mental conditions (citta-viveka). By maintaining moral discipline (sīla) free from remorse, one stabilizes the mind using concentration subjects (kammaṭṭhāna) such as loving-kindness (mettā) or recollection of the Buddha (buddhānussati). Gathering the scattered force of thoughts allows insight-wisdom (vipassanā-paññā) to discern the constant arising and passing away of phenomena, leading to the full realization of Nibbāna.
