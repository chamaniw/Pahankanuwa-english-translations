Sermon 197 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #197 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #197 (Paticca Samuppada Sermon 15)

Primary Theme: Contact (phassa) as the central turning point between the current of becoming (bhavasota) and the cessation of becoming (Nibbāna), deconstructed through rapid radical attention (yoniso manasikāra) to expose the self-created illusion of consciousness (viññāṇa) across the six sense-spheres.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: A assembly of Elder Monks residing at Isipatana in Benares (referencing context from the Majjhe Sutta, AN 6-s, alongside references to Ven. Mahā Kaccāna and the forest monk accused of fragrance-theft).

The Interaction: After their alms-round, the Elder monks gather in the assembly hall to conduct a Dhamma symposium on a cryptic verse uttered by the Buddha to the youth Metteyya in the Pārāyaṇa Vagga (Sn): "Yo ubhante viditvāna, majjhe mantā na lippati..." ("He who, having understood both ends, does not get attached to the middle with wisdom...").

The Disaster / Turning Point: The challenge of unraveling the cryptic fourfold riddle implicit in the verse: What is the first end? What is the second end? What is the middle? And who is the seamstress (sibbanī) who stitches them together?

The Result / Resolution: The Fifth Elder monk provides the key structural interpretation: Internal sense-spheres (ajjhattikāni āyatanāni) form one end, external sense-spheres (bāhirāni āyatanāni) form the second end, consciousness (viññāṇa) is the middle, and craving (taṇhā) is the seamstress. Craving stitches the two ends together while ignoring the middle (viññāṇa), creating the illusion of contact and solid objects. By applying wisdom/radical attention (yoniso manasikāra) to the middle/point of contact, the seamstress is bypassed, breaking the cycle of becoming (bhava).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Contact (Phassa) as the Turning Point of Existence

Drawing from the Dvayatānupassanā Sutta, contact is highlighted as the pivotal juncture dividing worldlings swept along the current of becoming (bhavasota) from Noble Disciples flowing in the Dhamma-current (Dhammasota). Suffering arises dependent on contact (phassa paccayā dukkhasambhavo), and with its remainderless cessation, suffering ceases.

2. Refutation of the 62 Wrong Views (Brahmajāla Sutta)

All 62 speculative views are dismissed by the Buddha with the phrase “Tadapi phassa paccayā” ("That too is due to contact"). All recluses and Brahmins experience the world by contacting again and again (phussa phussa paṭisaṁvedenti), generating craving, grasping, and becoming because they remain enslaved to contact.

3. Deconstruction of the Threefold Concurrence (Tiṇṇaṁ Saṅgati Phasso)

Citing the Madhupiṇḍika Sutta, the formula “cakkhuñcāvuso paṭicca rūpe ca uppajjati cakkhuviññāṇaṁ, tiṇṇaṁ saṅgati phasso” shows that eye-consciousness arises dependently. Delusion falsely treats eye, form, and consciousness as three independent entities. Contact arises precisely when delusion separates these three and counts them as distinct.

4. The Seamstress Metaphor (Sibbanī) and Craving (Taṇhā)

In the Majjhe Sutta, craving is characterized as a seamstress who possesses three attributes: ponobhavikā (leading to renewed birth), nandirāgasahagatā (accompanied by delight and lust), and tatratatrābhinandinī (delighting now here, now there). She ignores the middle—consciousness—and stitches internal and external sense-spheres together.

5. Radical Attention (Yoniso Manasikāra) as Attention to the Matrix

Yoni means the place of origin/matrix, which is contact (phassa). Yonisomanasikāra is defined as attending to the point of contact before conceptual proliferation (papañca) sets in. This speed of attention is illustrated through two similes:

Table-Tennis Simile: Returning Māra’s "form-ball" with the racket of anicca (impermanence) before it bumps off the table into papañca.

Sword-Fight Simile: Instantly parrying every strike from the opponent through continuous, rapid mindfulness.

6. Deconstruction of Eye and Ear Sensations

Eye: Perception (saññā) is compared to a mirage (mṛgatṛsṇikā), where the gap/consciousness between seer and object is ignored. Per the Indriyabhāvanā Sutta, speed of attention to forms should be as fast as opening and closing one's eyes.

Ear: Illustrated via the song of the cicada (rähäyyā). By breaking the perception of the compact (ghanasaññā) into a perception of a heap (rāsisaññā), one realizes Nibbāna in the subtle gap between auditory pulses.

7. Deconstruction of Nose, Tongue, and Body Sensations

Nose: Illustrated by the forest monk accused by a deity of being a "thief of fragrance" (gandhatthena) for smelling a lotus, showing that subtle attachment perpetuates the mirage.

Tongue: Saliva is the bearer of taste. Craving for taste is checked by the Buddha’s terrifying simile in the Nālaka Sutta: “Khuradhārūpamo bhava”—licking honey off a razor's edge.

Body: The feeling of touch is clarified through the Puttamaṁsa Sutta simile of the skinned cow (niccammā gāvī). The entire body is an open wound; skin is merely a "bandage" worldlings decorate.

8. Mind (Mano) and Mind-Objects (Dhammā) as Mind-Made (Manomayā)

In “Manopubbaṅgamā dhammā... manomayā”, mind-objects are revealed to be manufactured by the mind itself through manasikāra (doing-in-the-mind). In the Indriyabhāvanā Sutta, mental events are compared to drops of water falling into an intensely heated iron cauldron—ceasing and evaporating instantaneously upon contact.

9. The Structural Root of Phenomenal Reality (Kimmūlaka Sutta)

The origin of all phenomena follows a precise structure:

Chandamūlakā sabbe dhammā: Desire/interest (chanda) is the root (the hidden "computer mouse").

Manasikāra sambhavā: Attention (manasikāra) brings them up (the "cursor").

Phassa samudayā: Contact (phassa) is the arising point (the "menu").

10. The Raft, Relay Chariots, and the Selfless Victory over Māra

Synthesizing the Alagaddūpama Sutta (raft simile) and Rathavinīta Sutta (relay chariots), the Dhamma is pragmatic (nittharaṇatthāya—for crossing over) and relative (no gahaṇatthāya—not for holding onto).

The 4-Runner Relay Race: To illustrate the Four Paths and Fruits (magga-phala), four runners run consecutively; each passes the baton and drops dead. The winning post is reached, but no runner survives to claim a personal prize. This refutes charges of annihilation (ucchedadiṭṭhi) and affirms that Nibbāna is simply the cessation of suffering: "Dukkhameva uppajjamānaṁ uppajjati, dukkhaṁ nirujjhamānaṁ nirujjhati" ("Only suffering arises, only suffering ceases").
