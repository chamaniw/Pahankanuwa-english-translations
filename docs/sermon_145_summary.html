Sermon 145 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #145 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #145

Primary Theme: The priority of personal practice over precept ("Attānam eva paṭhamaṃ, patirūpe nivesaye"), contrasting the "silent sermon" of an exemplary life rooted in fewness of wishes (appicchatā) with hypocritical, material-driven Dhamma preaching (āmisadāyāda).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Venerable Upananda Sakyaputta, a monk of the Sakyan clan who was highly eloquent in preaching the Dhamma on the virtue of fewness of wishes (appicchatā), but possessed intense internal greed and covetousness.

The Interaction: Prior to the Rains Retreat (vassa), Ven. Upananda visited four different countryside monasteries to inquire about the quantity of retreat robes (vassāvāsika) distributed at each. Upon learning they offered one, two, three, and four robes respectively, he left personal items (sandals, a walking stick, and a water vessel) at the first three monasteries under the pretext of returning, but stayed at the fourth to secure the largest share. After the retreat, he improperly claimed requisites from all four locations.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Traveling with a cart laden with accumulated requisites, Upananda encountered two young monks arguing over the division of two cloths and a valuable woolen blanket. The monks asked him to arbitrate. Upananda assigned one cloth to each monk and claimed the valuable woolen blanket for himself, declaring, "This blanket belongs to me who preaches the Dhamma."

The Result / Resolution: The aggrieved monks reported the matter to the Buddha. The Blessed One severely censured Upananda as a "foolish man" (mogha purisa), laid down a training rule (sikkhāpada), and uttered Dhammapada verse 158: "First, one should establish oneself in the proper practice. Only then should one instruct another. By doing so, the wise person does not become defiled by receiving the blame and censure of others."

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Self-Establishment in Practice (Attānam eva paṭhamaṃ patirūpe nivesaye):

The Buddha emphasizes that effective instruction requires a foundation of personal practice. Advising others without living the practice exposes one to censure, self-defilement, and blame, diminishing the transformational power of the Dhamma.

2. Dhamma Inheritance vs. Material Inheritance (Dhammadāyāda vs. Āmisadāyāda):

Monks are admonished to be heirs to the Dhamma, not heirs to material requisites (āmisa). Requisites such as robes and alms food act as "bait" (āmisa) to the greedy. Obsession with accumulating requisites turns one into an āmisagaru (one who honors material things over Truth).

3. Pure vs. Impure Dhamma Preaching (Dhamma-desanā):

Impure Preaching: Teaching with a selfish motive, hoping the audience will become inspired and offer gifts and requisites.

Pure Preaching: Teaching out of pure compassion (dayā) and kindness, reflecting first on the noble qualities of the Dhamma (svākkhāto, sandiṭṭhiko, akāliko, ehipassiko, opaneyyiko, paccattaṃ veditabbo viññūhi), wishing only for the listeners' realization and liberation.

4. The "Silent Sermon" (Mona-Desanā / Example of Character):

True Dhamma instruction is delivered through the flawless example of one's character rather than mere vocal rhetoric. Ven. Mahākassapa Thero personified the "silent sermon"; the Buddha pointed to his silent, unblemished life of asceticism as the gold standard for future generations (paścimā janatā).

5. Ascetic Recycling and Non-Wastefulness (The Model of Ven. Ānanda):

Demonstrating true fewness of wishes (appicchatā), Ven. Ānanda accepted 500 robes from King Udena’s royal court and detailed a systematically thrifty cycle of usage: new robes given to needy monks → old robes used as bedspreads → pillowcases → floor rugs → foot-wipes → dust-rags → shredded and kneaded into clay plaster for monastery walls.

6. The Fragrance of Virtue (Sīla-Gandha):

While physical scents (sandalwood, jasmine) travel only with the wind, the scent of a virtuous life (sīla-gandha) travels both with and against the wind ("satañca gandho paṭivātameti"). A householder or monastic living in pure morality broadcasts an invisible, uplifting influence across society.

7. Restraint through Wisdom (Ñāṇa-Saṃvara) and Requisite Reflection:

Monastics guard against greed for the four requisites not through extreme deprivation (such as Jain nudity), but through wise reflection (yoniso manasikāra). By contemplating requisites as mere combinations of the four great elements (paṭhavī, āpo, tejo, vāyo) and noting their inherent repulsiveness (asubha) upon contact with the body, one achieves restraint through wisdom (ñāṇa-saṃvara).

8. Superiority of the Miracle of Instruction (Anusāsanī-Pāṭihāriya):

The Buddha minimized the spiritual value of physical miracles (iddhipāṭihāriya) and mind-reading (ādesanā-pāṭihāriya), declaring the miracle of instruction to be supreme: "Think in this way, do not think in that way; reflect in this way, do not reflect in that way; abandon this, enter and dwell in that."

9. The Thirty-Seven Factors of Enlightenment (Sattissa-Bodhipakkhiya-Dhamma):

At the end of his life, the Buddha summarized his entire 45 years of teaching from a purely practical standpoint through the 37 Factors of Enlightenment (including the Four Foundations of Mindfulness, Noble Eightfold Path, etc.). The Dhamma is an experiential path of direct practice (paṭipadā), not an abstract academic exercise.

10. The Danger of Logical Intellectualization and Half-Truths:

Subjecting the Dhamma—which is beyond mere logic (atakkāvacara)—to purely academic or logical analysis separates instruction from daily practice. This results in the distortion of training rules, creation of dangerous half-truths, and propagation of wrong views (micchā-diṭṭhi).

11. True Gift of Fearlessness (Abhayadāna):

Rather than conspicuous public displays, quietly and strictly keeping the Five Precepts (pañca-sīla) serves as an authentic abhayadāna—granting absolute safety and fearlessness to all living beings—and establishes a localized environment of peace.
