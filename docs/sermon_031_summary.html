Sermon 031 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #31 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #31

Primary Theme: The profound meaning of Lokavidū (Knower of the World) through the awakening of the Eye of Wisdom (paññā-cakkhu), redefining the "world" (loka) as an experiential process tied to the six sense bases (saḷāyatana), and demonstrating why speculative metaphysical questions are set aside (ṭhapanīya).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Various ascetics, brahmins, and wanderers of rival sects in Sāvatthī, a King of Sāvatthī, and a group of men born blind (jātyandha).

The Interaction: Monk disciples

on alms-round in Sāvatthī witness rival ascetics engaged in fierce, abusive quarrels over ten speculative views regarding the eternity/finitude of the world, the identity of the soul (jīva) versus the body, and the post-mortem status of a liberated person (Tathāgata). The monks report these disputes to the Buddha at Jeta’s Grove.

The Disaster / Turning Point: To illustrate the plight of these ascetics, the Buddha recounts a parable: A king orders his officer to gather men born blind (jātyandha) and present an elephant to them. Each blind man is allowed to feel only a single part of the elephant (head, ear, tusk, trunk, body, foot, thigh, tail, tuft). Consequently, they engage in violent brawling, each asserting his partial tactile perception (ekāṅgadassino) as the absolute truth—claiming an elephant is like a water jar, winnowing basket, plowshare, pillar, storehouse, or broom.

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha explains that non-Buddhist sectarians are spiritually blind (andhā) and eyeless (acakkhukā), arguing endlessly because they lack the Eye of Wisdom (paññā-cakkhu). True liberation and total understanding (Lokavidū) come not from intellectual speculation or partial sensory grasping, but from penetrating the arising, ceasing, and transcendence of the six sense bases (saḷāyatana-nirodha).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Fourfold Classification of Questions (Pañha-vyākaraṇa):

The Buddha categorized questions into four types:

Ekaṁsa-vyākaraṇīya (to be answered directly as yes/no)

Vibhajja-vyākaraṇīya (to be answered analytically/by dividing)

Paṭipucchā-vyākaraṇīya (to be answered with a counter-question)

Ṭhapanīya (to be set aside/rejected)

The ten speculative questions regarding the world, soul, and Tathāgata belong to ṭhapanīya because they are built upon a fundamentally flawed premise—the illusion of a fixed self (sakkāya-diṭṭhi).

2. The Eye of Wisdom (Paññā-cakkhu) vs. Physical and Divine Eyes:

While the divine eye (dibba-cakkhu) develops on the foundation of the physical eye (maṁsa-cakkhu) and can see past lives or cosmic realms, the Eye of Wisdom (paññā-cakkhu) is unique to the Buddha’s teaching. It directly perceives the ultimate law of Dependent Arising: "Whatever is of the nature to arise, all that is of the nature to cease" (yaṁ kiñci samudayadhammaṁ sabbaṁ taṁ nirodhadhammaṁ), thereby dismantling the perception of compactness (ghana-saññā).

3. Phenomenological Redefinition of the "World" (Loka):

In response to Ven. Samiddhi, the Buddha defined the "world" not as an objective geographical or physical space, but as an experiential phenomenon occurring wherever the six sense bases (saḷāyatana)—eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind—interact with their respective sense objects and consciousness (viññāṇa). Where sense bases cease, the world ceases.

4. Etymological Definition of Loka as Crumbling (Paloka-dhamma):

Answering Ven. Ānanda, the Buddha derived the word loka from paloka-dhammaṁ, meaning "that which is of a crumbling, disintegrating nature" (lujjati palujjati). Sense bases, sense objects, sense-consciousness, and resulting feelings crumble moment by moment. Expecting permanence in a disintegrating process is like trying to measure water with a sieve or leaky ladle.

5. Finding the End of the World Within (Byāmamatte Kaḷevare):

Referring to the story of the deva Rohitassa—who traveled through the air at arrow-speed for a hundred years without finding the end of the world—the Buddha declared that physical traveling can never reach the world's end. Instead, he proclaimed: "Within this very fathom-long body, endowed with perception and mind, lies the world, the origin of the world, the cessation of the world, and the path leading to the cessation of the world."

6. All Speculative Views Rooted in Contact (Phassa):

In the Brahmajāla Sutta, the Buddha encompasses all sixty-two worldly views (including those of yogis with psychic powers) and demonstrates that every view is constructed through contact (phassa) via the six sense bases. The noble disciple transcends this entire "net of views" by penetrating five aspects of the sense bases: their arising (samudaya), passing away (vaya), gratification (assāda), danger (ādīnava), and escape (nissaraṇa).

7. Rejection of the Soul (Jīva) and Body Dualism:

Questions asking whether the soul/self (jīva) and body are identical or separate are set aside as foolish notions (kevala-paripūro bāladhammo). Because the Buddha’s insight reveals absolute not-self (anattā) across all phenomena, asserting a relationship between a non-existent "self" and the physical body is a logical trap resting on delusion.

8. The Riddle of the Tathāgata and the Extinguished Fire (Nibbuta):

When the wanderer Vacchagotta asks four-cornered tetralemma questions regarding whether a liberated person (Tathāgata) exists after death, the Buddha replies that such terms "do not apply" (na upeti). Using the simile of a fire that goes out due to lack of fuel (grass and wood), the Buddha explains that an Arahant is freed from the fuel of the five aggregates of grasping (pañcupādānakkhandha). The only fitting description is nibbuta (extinguished/cool).

9. Consciousness (Viññāṇa) and the Whirling Firebrand (Alāta-cakka):

Even among those who grasp anattā in form or feeling, consciousness (viññāṇa) is frequently misapprehended as an enduring soul. The Buddha describes consciousness as rapidly changing (lahuparivattaṁ). Like a whirling firebrand (alāta-cakka) that creates the illusion of a continuous circle of fire, the lightning-fast arising and ceasing of consciousness tricks the uninstructed worldling into assuming a permanent self.

10. Definition of a "Being" (Satta) and the Path of Letting Go:

In the Rādha Sutta, the Buddha defines a "being" (satta) simply as one who is stuck, attached, or bound (satto, visatto) to the five aggregates through desire (chanda), lust (rāga), delight (nandī), and craving (taṇhā). When attachment and fuel are exhausted (khīṇabījā), becoming ceases (bhava-nirodha), which is Nibbāna itself—a supramundane state (lokuttara) realized in this very life (diṭṭheva dhamme).
