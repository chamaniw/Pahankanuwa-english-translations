Sermon 166 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #166 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #166 (Audio No. 20)

Primary Theme: The essential necessity and practical methodology of taming the mind (citta-damatha), overcoming unwholesome thought streams through the five strategies of the Vitakkasaṇṭhāna Sutta, and dismantling the accumulated momentum of volitional formations (saṅkhāra) across the five aggregates (pañcakkhandha) via wise attention (yoniso manasikāra).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: A group of sixty monks meditating in Mātigāma; Mātika Mātā (a highly attained female lay devotee); and a monk who traveled to her village seeking material comfort and mind-reading interactions.

The Interaction:

Sixty monks obtain meditation subjects from the Buddha and reside in Mātigāma for the rainy season retreat (vassa), invited by Mātika Mātā.

The monks agree to live and practice in absolute solitude, gathering only when a wooden gong (gaṇḍi) is struck during emergencies.

Mātika Mātā inquires about their ascetic duties (samaṇa-dhamma), learns bodily repulsiveness and impermanence meditation, practices it herself, and swiftly attains Non-returning (Anāgāmi-phala) along with the five supernormal powers (pañca-abhiññā) and four analytical knowledges (paṭisambhidā-ñāṇa).

Using her divine eye (dibba-cakkhu), she realizes the monks are still worldlings (puthujjana) struggling due to a lack of suitable food (āhāra-sappāya). She provides tailored meals, calming their physical ailments, enabling them all to reach Arahantship before the retreat ends.

The Disaster / Turning Point:

Hearing of her hospitality and telepathic powers, another monk goes to Mātigāma expecting comfort. As he harbors consecutive internal desires—sweeping the lodging, jaggery drinks, rice gruel, sweets, and seeing her bring alms directly—Mātika Mātā fulfills every single wish using her mind-reading ability (paracittavijānana).

When the monk directly asks if she reads minds, she gives a humble yet pointed response: "Those who do not know the minds of others are the ones who act in this way."

Struck with intense spiritual urgency (saṃvega) and paralyzed by fear that his unwholesome thoughts will be exposed like a caught thief, the monk flees back to the Buddha, declaring he cannot stay in such a place.

The Result / Resolution:

The Buddha instructs the monk to return to that very place, identifying it as his suitable environment (sappāya), and assigns him a single task: to guard his own mind.

On this occasion, the Buddha utters Dhammapada Verse 35 ("Dunniggahassa lahuno...").

The monk returns, successfully guards his mind while supported by Mātika Mātā, and ultimately achieves the noble fruit of Arahantship.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Nature of the Untamed Mind (Citta-Vagga, Verse 35):

The Buddha describes the uncultivated mind through three distinct characteristics:

Dunniggaha: Difficult to restrain, suppress, or subdue.

Lahu: Arising and vanishing with extreme rapidity.

Yatthakāmanipātin: Alighting unpredictably on whatever sensory objects it pleases.

Taming such a mind (cittaṃ dantaṃ) is declared as the sole path to true happiness (sukhāvahaṃ).

2. Lay Spiritual Potential and Support:

The story of Mātika Mātā demonstrates that high spiritual realization is not restricted to the ordained Sangha. Lay practitioners can generate the perception of capability (kabbattā-saññā), attain deep insight/supernormal attainments (Anāgāmi-phala, abhiññā), and provide both material (āhāra-sappāya) and spiritual guidance to practitioners.

3. Method 1 of Thought Control – Replacement (Peg Simile):

When evil unwholesome thoughts (pāpaka akusala vitakka) rooted in greed, hatred, or delusion arise, one must intentionally replace them with a wholesome object (e.g., counteracting anger with loving-kindness, mettā). This is like a skilled carpenter driving out a coarse, stuck wooden peg using a fine peg.

4. Method 2 of Thought Control – Contemplating Danger (Carcass Simile):

If replacement fails, one contemplates the disadvantage and grave danger (ādīnava) of unwholesome thoughts to arouse moral shame (hiri) and moral dread (ottappa). This is compared to a well-groomed youth who feels immense disgust and horror when the carcass of a snake, dog, or human is hung around their neck.

5. Method 3 of Thought Control – Non-Attention (Closing the Eyes Simile):

When unwholesome thoughts persist due to repeated recollection, one applies non-recollection and non-attention (amanasikāra). This involves intentionally looking away or closing one's mental eyes to the object, cutting off the sensory feeding of that thought stream.

6. Method 4 of Thought Control – Structural Deconstruction (Posture Simile):

One investigates the underlying construction and speed of the arising thought, gradually calming its momentum. This is likened to a fast-walking man who systematically slows down: fast walking → slow walking → standing still → sitting down → lying down.

7. Method 5 of Thought Control – Forceful Suppression (Strong Man Simile):

When an unwholesome thought is extremely persistent, one uses sheer wholesome mental strength to forcefully subdue, crush, and obliterate it (niggaha). This is compared to a strong man seizing a weaker man by the head or shoulders and pinning him to the ground.

8. Gaining Mastery Over Thoughts (Citta-vasībhāva):

By applying these five practical methods from the Vitakkasaṇṭhāna Sutta in their proper context, a meditator becomes a master of their mind—thinking only the thoughts they wish to think and effortlessly discarding unwanted thoughts.

9. Wise Attention (Yoniso Manasikāra) vs. Unwise Attention (Ayoniso Manasikāra):

Ayoniso manasikāra projects far outside to external objects, accumulating kamma like a thirsty deer running after a mirage.

Yoniso manasikāra turns the eye of wisdom inward to the exact point of origin—sensory contact (phassa)—nipping unwholesome perceptions in the bud before they build into volitional activities (saṅkhāra).

10. Element Contemplation (Dhātu-Manasikāra) and Dismantling the Five Aggregates:

By contemplating the aggregate of form (rūpakkhandha) as a temporary construct of the four great elements (mahābhūta: earth, water, fire, air), attachment to the body dissolves. This leads to equanimity (upekkhā) toward feelings (vedanā), reduction of volitional formations (saṅkhāra), and exposure of consciousness (viññāṇa) as an illusory construct, yielding disenchantment (nibbidā).

11. The World Within the Fathom-Long Body (Byāmamatta Kāya):

The Buddha emphasized that Dukkha, its origin, its cessation (Nibbāna), and the path to its cessation are all contained within this very fathom-long body endowed with perception and mind. Escaping saṃsāra is not a physical journey, but the stilling of the momentum of saṅkhāra (saṅkhāra-samatha).

12. Virtue (Sīla) as the Firm Foundation for Concentration (Samādhi):

Restraining body and speech through virtue (such as ājīv-aṭṭhamaka-sīla or Uposatha precepts) provides the unshakeable foundation needed for meditation. Just as a man cannot lift a heavy rock while sinking in mud, one cannot develop deep mental concentration (samādhi) or insight wisdom (paññā) without the firm ground of purified conduct.
