Sermon 040 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #40 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #40

Primary Theme: Understanding the present moment (paccuppanna) through the Bhaddekaratta teachings, breaking temporal clinging (past/future) via insight meditation (vipassanā), and realizing supreme solitude (upadhi-viveka) by dismantling self-illusion (sakkāya-diṭṭhi).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Monks including Ven. Samiddhi, Ven. Lomasakaṅgiya, and the monk named Thera (Theranāmako), alongside visiting celestial beings such as the young deity Candana.

The Interaction: Deities approach monks late at night or after bathing (e.g., Candana approaching Ven. Lomasakaṅgiya at Nigrodhārāma, or a deity approaching Ven. Samiddhi at the Tapoda hot springs) to inquire whether they know the summary (uddesa) and exposition (vibhaṅga) of "one who has an auspicious day" (bhaddekaratta). Similarly, the Buddha questions Ven. Thera on his daily habit of living and walking alone (ekavihārī).

The Disaster / Turning Point: The realization that both humans and deities are ignorant of these vital verses, coupled with the profound reminder of inescapable mortality—that there is no bargaining with Death (maccu) and his great army—prompts the monks to urgently seek out the Buddha or Ven. Mahākaccāna to receive the full exposition.

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha and Ven. Mahākaccāna clarify that true solitude (eka) is not mere physical isolation (kāya-viveka), but the internal abandonment of desire and lust (chandarāgo) for past, future, and present aggregates (khandha) or sense-bases (saḷāyatana). Through this exposition, monks are taught how to cultivate direct insight (vipassanā) to reach Nibbāna—the ultimate seclusion from all assets/defilements (upadhi-viveka).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Definition of Bhaddekaratta (Supreme Solitude):

Bhaddekaratta signifies one who is delighted in noble seclusion or solitude (bhadra = auspicious; ratta = attached/delighted; eka = solitude/seclusion).

Citing the Theranāmako Sutta, true solitary dwelling (ekavihārī) is not merely staying physically apart from the crowd, but abandoning the past (atītaṃ pahīnaṃ), relinquishing the future (anāgataṃ paṭinissaṭṭhaṃ), and subduing desire and lust (chandarāgo) for present forms of existence (paccuppannesu attabhāvapaṭilābhesu).

2. Three Levels of Seclusion (Viveka):

Physical Seclusion (kāya-viveka): Living alone in the forest; helpful for practice, but the lowest level of seclusion.

Mental Seclusion (citta-viveka): Suppressing the five hindrances (nīvaraṇa) and attaining meditative absorptions (jhāna).

Seclusion from Assets/Defilements (upadhi-viveka): The deepest seclusion, representing Nibbāna and Arahantship. It is the total relinquishment of all samsaric assets (sabbūpadhipatinissaggo).

3. Nature of Assets (Upadhi) and Aggregates (Khandha):

Mundane upadhi refers to gold, land, spouse, and family. In the deeper Dhamma, upadhi refers to the five groups of grasping (pañca-upādānakkhandha): form (rūpa), feeling (vedanā), perception (saññā), preparations (saṅkhāra), and consciousness (viññāṇa).

Each aggregate consists of eleven aspects (atītānāgatapaccuppannaṃ, ajjhattaṃ vā bahiddhā vā...): past/future/present, internal/external, gross/subtle, inferior/superior, far/near. When relied upon as the basis for self-identity, grasping (upādāna) becomes an asset (upadhi).

4. Temporal Clinging and How to Overcome It:

Past (Atīta): Running after the past occurs by generating delight (nandi—a form of craving/taṇhā) toward past experiences.

Future (Anāgata): Yearning for the future occurs when wishing/hoping for unarisen aggregates or sense-forms.

Present (Paccuppanna): Being drawn into (saṃhīrati) or provoked by (saṅkuppati) present states occurs through the dogma of self-illusion (sakkāya-diṭṭhi), framing present aggregates in four ways relative to self. Non-entanglement occurs by applying the perspective of non-self (anattā).

5. Dual Analytical Frameworks for Insight (Vipassanā):

The Bhaddekaratta teachings can be applied through two equivalent approaches:

1. The Five Groups of Grasping (pañca-upādānakkhandha) as shown in the primary Bhaddekaratta Sutta.

2. The Six Sense-Bases (saḷāyatana)—eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind—as expounded by Ven. Mahākaccāna. Both paths systematically lead to Nibbāna.

6. The Practical Tool of Non-Self: The Letter Na ("Not") and the Hand-Plane Simile:

Worldlings conceptualize reality through craving, conceit, and view: "This is mine, this I am, this is my self" (etaṃ mama, esohamasmi, eso me attā).

The Buddha provides a practical tool by adding the letter na ("not"): "This is not mine, this I am not, this is not my self" (n'etaṃ mama, n'esohamasmi, na m'eso attā).

Simile: The letter na acts like the blade of a carpenter's hand-plane (yatukæṭaya), used by the practitioner (yogāvacara) to shave off the swollen distortions of craving (taṇhā), conceit (māna), and view (diṭṭhi).

7. Deconstruction of Preparations (Saṅkhāra) and Clinging:

Banana Tree Trunk Simile: A person peeling the sheaths of a banana trunk finds no heartwood at the center. Similarly, investigating preparations layer by layer reveals no central self.

Investigation vs. Grasping: Distinguishes tight clinging/grasping (parāmarsa) from gentle, reflective investigation (sammasana). Insight breaks the worldling's perception of compactness (ghana-saññā) down into a perception of a composite heap (rāsi-saññā).

8. Analysis of Name-and-Form (Nāmarūpa):

Experience consists of Nāma (feeling/vedanā, perception/saññā, intention/cetanā, contact/phassa, attention/manasikāra) and designated Rūpa (built on the four great elements: earth, water, fire, air).

Feeling (vedanā) is primary; it triggers perception, which activates intention, leading to the direction of attention onto contact (phassa).

9. Progression of Insight Knowledges (Vipassanā-Ñāṇa):

Knowledge of Arising and Passing Away (udayabbaya-ñāṇa): Seeing preparations rapidly arise and fall in the present moment.

Knowledge of Dissolution (bhaṅga-ñāṇa): Observing objects and the observing mind itself passing away faster than they arise.

Simile: Like walking a narrow mountain path between two precipices holding a decayed handrail—realizing the handrail itself is crumbling produces intense spiritual urgency (saṃvega).

Fear (Bhaya), Disadvantage (Ādīnava), and Revulsion (Nibbidā): Realizing existence offers nothing stable to grasp.

Simile: Revulsion (nibbidā) is like biting into a bitter gooseberry (nelli); though initially distasteful to worldlings bound to existence (bhava-taṇhā), thorough reflection yields an ambrosial, sweet aftertaste that opens the door to Nibbāna.

10. Equanimity Toward Preparations (Saṅkhārupekkhā-Ñāṇa) and Liberation:

Recognizing there is no "I" being destroyed shifts the meditator from fear to equanimity (saṅkhārupekkhā). Preparations arise and fall without penetrating the mind.

Simile 1 (Express Train): Like a passenger on a moving train watching scenery flash by; objects pass too quickly to be taken inside or grabbed.

Simile 2 (Lotus Leaf): Like water drops rolling off a lotus leaf without wetting it; defilements and preparations fail to stain the mind of the liberated practitioner.

11. Imperative of Immediate Effort (Ātappa):

Verse emphasis: "Today itself the effort should be made, who knows if death will come tomorrow? For there is no bargaining with Death and his great army" (ajjeva kiccamātappaṃ, ko jaññā maraṇaṃ suve...).

Even in Tāvatiṃsa heaven—where one day equals 100 human years—the Buddha preached this core message of urgency to the deities, demonstrating its infinitely greater relevance for human beings facing imminent internal and external decay.
