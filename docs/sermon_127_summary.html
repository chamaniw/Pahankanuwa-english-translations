Sermon 127 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #127 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #127

Primary Theme: Subduing anger (kodha / dosa) and entrenched resentment (āghāta) through forbearance (khanti), right effort (sammā vāyāma), and the cultivation of an attitude of loving-kindness (mettā), emphasizing internal mental transformation over external retaliatory conflict.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Venerable Sāriputta (the Chief Disciple foremost in wisdom) and a heretical Brahmin residing in Sāvatthī.

The Interaction: While lay followers in Sāvatthī were praising Venerable Sāriputta’s unshakeable patience and total freedom from anger, a heretical Brahmin challenged the claim, making a bet that he could successfully provoke the Elder to anger.

The Disaster / Turning Point: As Ven. Sāriputta walked for alms, the Brahmin approached from behind and struck him extremely hard on the back. Without turning around or breaking his stride, Ven. Sāriputta calmly continued walking forward at his natural pace.

The Result / Resolution: Overcome with remorse and awe at the Elder’s supreme composure, the Brahmin fell at Ven. Sāriputta's feet to beg for forgiveness, which was immediately granted. The Brahmin then invited him for alms. Ven. Sāriputta accepted, subsequently used situational wisdom (sthānōcita prajñā) to shield the transformed Brahmin from a club-wielding mob seeking retribution, and quelled an uproar among the monks at Jetavana. The Buddha used the event to expound Dhammapada verses 389–390, defining a true Brahmin (an Arahant) as one who neither strikes nor retaliates with wrath.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Subtle Gratification in Conflict and Aversion (Dosa): Although attachment (lobha) and aversion (dosa) appear to be opposite mental states, beings repeatedly engage in clashing and revenge because they derive a subtle gratification (assāda) from seeing an opponent fall into Dukkha (suffering/stress).

2. The True Definition of a "Brahmin": Reaffirming the Vasala Sutta, the Buddha clarifies that in his Dispensation a true Brahmin is an Arahant. A true Brahmin never strikes another (na brāhmaṇassa pahareyya), nor does he unleash hatred or retaliatory wrath (nāssa muñcetha brāhmaṇo) when harmed.

3. Restraining the Mind from What is "Dear" (Yadā nisedho manaso piyehi): For an untrained mind, taking revenge and reacting in anger feels "dear" or pleasing (piya). Restraining the mind from this impulse is a noble achievement because, according to natural law (dharmatā), turning back the violent mind (yato yato hiṁsamano nivattati) calms one’s samsaric suffering (sammati-c-eva dukkhaṁ) to that exact extent.

4. The Power of Silent Preaching and Situational Wisdom (Sthānōcita Prajñā): Ven. Sāriputta’s refusal to react or even look back served as a "silent sermon" that instantly transformed the heretical assailant into a devoted follower. Furthermore, his tactful questioning of the mob ("Did he strike you or me?") prevented escalated violence and public chaos.

5. Simile of the Cracked Bronze Vessel (Kaṁso Upahato Yathā): Retaliatory speech acts like an intact bronze bell that reverberates with echoed thoughts of revenge. Restraining retaliatory words makes the mind like a cracked bronze vessel (kaṁso upahato yathā) whose sound ceases immediately upon impact, allowing one to experience a temporary taste of Nibbāna (tadaṅga-nibbāna).

6. Refutation of "Justifiable Anger" (Sādhāraṇa Kōpaya): Dhamma analysis reveals that anger is never justifiable. A mind dominated by aversion (dosa) loses the capacity to see things as they truly are (yathābhūta-ñāṇadassana) and falls into bias (dosa-agati), inevitably leading to unwholesome actions.

7. Immediate Neutralization of Arisen Anger: Anger must be pacified at its inception before it escalates into multi-lifetime hatred (baddha vairaya). The sermon highlights two similes:

The Runaway Chariot: Quickly bringing an off-track chariot back to the road (rathaṁ bhantaṁva vāraye).

Snake Venom: Immediately applying medicine (osadha) to stop the rapid spread of poison (visataṁ sappavisaṁva osadhehi).

8. Building an Attitude of Loving-Kindness (Mettā-ākalpaya): The primary objective of daily mettā practice is not merely formal sitting or vocalizing words, but shaping an underlying mental attitude (ākalpaya). An established attitude of loving-kindness ensures that sudden daily conflicts are met with non-harming (avihiṁsā) rather than reactive hostility.

9. Ven. Sāriputta’s Five Methods for Subduing Resentment (Āghāta-paṭivinaya):

The Dirty Rag: If a person has unrefined bodily conduct but refined speech, focus solely on their clean words, just as a rag-robe monk uses his feet to tear a clean piece from a dirty cloth.

The Algae-Covered Pond: If a person has unrefined speech but refined bodily conduct, push aside the verbal "moss" with both hands to drink the clean water of their good deeds.

The Cow's Hoof-print Puddle: If a person’s body and speech are mostly improper, look for rare moments of wholesome thought, lapping up the tiny puddle of virtue like a thirsty traveler on his knees.

The Sick Traveler: If a person is 100% unwholesome with no redeeming conduct, abandon expectations and arouse pure compassion (karuṇā) and sympathy (dayānukampā), seeing them as a critically ill traveler devoid of medicine.

The Delightful Clean Pond: If a person is thoroughly virtuous, dispel irrational resentment immediately by reflecting on their vast heap of virtue.

10. The Structural Triad of Samsaric Wandering: Delusion (moha) creates the core illusion of "I" and "mine" (sakkāya-diṭṭhi). Based on this focal point, attraction (lobha) arises toward what the self likes, while repulsion/hatred (dosa) arises toward whatever threatens or displeases it, keeping the mind trapped between attachment and clashing.

11. Internal Struggle vs. External Conflict: Worldly mindsets view external provocations as challenges to assert power through retaliation (making $1+1=2$, escalating to 4, 8, 16). The Dhamma reframes every external provocation as an internal challenge—an opportunity to apply Right Effort (sammā vāyāma), shape the mind, and conquer unwholesome states at their root.
