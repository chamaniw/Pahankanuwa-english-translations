Sermon 064 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #64 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #64

Primary Theme: The cultivation of the Four Noble Lineages (ariyavaṃsā) through radical contentment with basic requisites (santuṭṭhi), the practice of ascetic effacement (dhutanga / sallēkha), and unwavering delight in meditation (bhāvanārāmatā) and abandoning defilements (pahānārāmatā) as the essential foundation for transcending Mara’s snares and realizing Nibbāna.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The Supreme Buddha, delivering the discourse to the Sangha at Jetavana monastery in Sāvatthi; supported historically by figures such as Ven. Mahinda Maha Arahant, Ven. Maha Kassapa, Ven. Cakkhupāla, Ven. Rahula, and King Vohārika Tissa of Sri Lanka.

The Interaction: Without any external prompt or crisis, an intention arose spontaneously within the Buddha to highlight the core dispensational practices that touch the hearts of the Sangha. Historically, this discourse (Ariyavamsa Sutta, AN 4.28) was introduced to Sri Lanka by Ven. Mahinda and became central to the island's Buddhist culture, with kings establishing funds for overnight recitations across the nation.

The Disaster / Turning Point: The existential plight of beings trapped in saṃsāra, who succumb to Mara’s bait (lokāmisa) through sensual craving for refined clothing, food, and lodging, alongside spiritual paralysis caused by discontent (arati) and sloth/torpor (thīna-middha). In contemporary times, this is compounded by the fading of dispensational practices—a state where "the lamp of the Sasana is about to go out."

The Result / Resolution: By establishing oneself in the four ancient, unadulterated noble lineages and utilizing the four requisites with the wisdom of deliverance (nissaraṇa-paññā), a practitioner leaps clear of Mara's snares like a wise deer (pāsa), conquers both discontent (arati) and sensual delight (rati), dispels all kamma (sabbakammavihāyinaṃ), earns the praise of Devas and Brahmas, and realizes Nibbāna (sabba-dukkha-nissaraṇa-nibbāna).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Four Noble Lineages (Ariyavaṃsā):

The sermon centers on the four ancient (porāṇā), foremost (aggaññā), long-standing (vaṃsaññā), and unadulterated (asambhinnā) qualities recognized by wise ascetics:

Contentment with any robe whatsoever (itari-itara cīvara santosa).

Contentment with any alms-food whatsoever (itari-itara piṇḍapāta santosa).

Contentment with any lodging whatsoever (itari-itara senāsana santosa).

Delight in meditation and delight in abandoning defilements (bhāvanārāmo hoti bhāvanārato, pahānārāmo hoti pahānārato).

2. Conquering Discontent (Arati) vs. Sensual Delight (Rati):

The sutta emphasizes that a monk established in these lineages conquers discontent (arati)—defined as a lack of joy in monastic life/meditation and the impulse to return to lay life—regardless of where he lives. The wise practitioner (dhīro) conquers both spiritual discontent (arati) and sensual delight (rati) in the five cords of pleasure (arati-rati-saho hi dhīro).

3. Mapping the 13 Ascetic Practices (Dhutanga) to the Lineages:

Ven. Nanananda explains how ancient commentators linked the four lineages directly to the thirteen dhutangas:

Robes (2): Paṃsukūlika (refuse-rag-robe wearer) and Tecīvarika (triple-robe wearer).

Alms-Food (5): Piṇḍapātika (alms-food eater), Sapadānacārika (uninterrupted house-to-house alms round), Ekāsanika (one-session eater), Pattapiṇḍika (bowl-eater), and Khalupacchābhattika (refuser of extra food after starting the meal).

Lodging (5): Āraññika (forest-dweller), Rukkhamūlika (tree-root dweller), Abbhokāsika (open-air dweller), Sosānika (cemetery/charnel-ground dweller), and Yathāsanthatika (acceptor of whatever seat is assigned).

Meditation/Abandoning (1): Nesajjika (sitting practice, abstaining from lying down).

4. The Four Supports (Nissaya) and Monastic Fundamentals:

At higher ordination (upasampadā), a monk is reminded of the minimum expectations for living: refuse-rag robes (paṃsukūlacīvaraṃ), alms-food (piṇḍapātabhojanaṃ), tree-root lodging (rukkhamūlasenāsanaṃ), and fermented urine medicine (pūtimuttabhesajjaṃ). Household offerings are extra gains; the core practice demands fewness of wishes (alpeccatā) and ease of support (subharatā).

5. Wisdom of Deliverance (Nissaraṇa-Paññā) vs. Worldly Bait (Lokāmisa):

Requisites must be consumed without greed (giddha), attachment, or self-exaltation. Using requisites with nissaraṇa-paññā involves wise reflection (paccavekkhaṇā): food is consumed not for amusement, intoxication, fattening, or beautification (n'eva davāya, na madāya, na maṇḍanāya, na vibhūsanāya), but solely to appease hunger and maintain the body for Nibbāna. Unreflective consumption turns requisites into Mara's bait (lokāmisa).

6. The Simile of Mara’s Snare (Pāsarāsi Sutta / Ariya Pariyesana Sutta):

Requisites act as snares (pāsa) set by the deer-hunter (Mara). Unwise practitioners swallow the bait and get trapped. The wise monk acts like the deer that lies down directly on top of the snare with sensory restraint; when Mara approaches, the monk effortlessly leaps away uncaught.

7. The Paradox of Using Food to Abandon Food (Āhāraṃ Nissāya Āhāraṃ Pajahatha):

The Buddha teaches that to eliminate all four types of nutriment—physical food (kabaḷīkārāhāra), contact (phassa), mental volition (manosañcetanā), and consciousness (viññāṇa)—one must strategically support oneself on physical food (āhāraṃ nissāya āhāraṃ pajahatha) with wise reflection, turning physical nourishment into a tool for ultimate liberation.

8. Effacement (Sallēkha) through the Four Right Efforts (Sammappadhāna):

The fourth noble lineage—delighting in meditation and abandoning—is aligned with the four right efforts: preventing unarisen unwholesome states, abandoning arisen unwholesome states (using specific dhutangas to target personal defilements), rousing unarisen wholesome states (meditation/awakening factors), and maintaining arisen wholesome states.

9. Monastic Disfigurement (Vivaṇṇiya) vs. Lay Colorfulness (Vevaṇṇiya):

Citing the Dasa-dhamma Sutta, Ven. Nanananda highlights the monk's constant reflection: "vevaṇṇiyaṃ bhāvaṃ upagato’smi" ("I have entered upon a state of disfigurement"). Lay life pursues colorfulness/beautification (vevaṇṇiya); monastic life embraces disfigurement (vivaṇṇiya) of the body (shaving hair/beard) and of requisites (wearing dull rag-robes and carrying a black bowl) to constantly reinforce non-attachment.

10. Historical and Spiritual Significance in Sri Lanka:

The Ariyavamsa Sutta (also known as Catu-paccaya-santosa-bhāvanārāma-ariyavaṃsa Sutta) was historically the bedrock of Sri Lankan monastic life. Ancient kings (e.g., King Vohārika Tissa) and great reciters (e.g., Dīghabhāṇaka Abhaya) mobilized entire populations to listen to its preaching throughout all three watches of the night, fostering a collective culture centered on renunciation and spiritual exertion.
