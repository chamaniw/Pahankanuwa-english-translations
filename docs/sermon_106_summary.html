Sermon 106 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #106 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #106 (Audio Sermon No. 13)

Primary Theme: Cultivating the wisdom eye (paññā-cakkhu) through mindfulness of death (maraṇasati), shattering the intoxication with life (jīvita-mada), and developing urgency (saṁvega) to realize Nibbāna.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The young daughter of a cloth-weaver in the city of Āḷavī (and subsequently her father).

The Interaction: Three years after hearing a sermon by the Buddha in Āḷavī on maraṇasati, the weaver's daughter continuously practiced mindfulness of death. Cognizant of her mature wisdom and her impending death, the Buddha traveled to Āḷavī with 500 bhikkhus. As she hurried toward the assembly carrying a shuttle, the Buddha made direct eye contact with her, signaling her to approach.

The Disaster / Turning Point: The Buddha engaged her in a series of four enigmatic questions in front of an agitated crowd:

1. "Where have you come from?" — She answered, "I do not know" (meaning her past life origin).

2. "Where are you going?" — She answered, "I do not know" (meaning her future destination after death).

3. "Do you not know?" — She answered, "I do know" (meaning the certainty of her death).

4. "Do you know?" — She answered, "I do not know" (meaning the exact time and place of her death).

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha praised her profound responses and recited Dhammapada 174, whereupon she attained the fruit of Stream-entry (sotāpatti-phala). Immediately upon returning to the weaving shed, she was accidentally struck by a spinning shuttle and died instantly. The Buddha comforted her grieving father by pointing out the ocean of tears shed across Saṁsāra; her father subsequently ordained, received higher ordination (upasampadā), and attained Arahantship.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Three Types of Eyes (Tīṇi Cakkhūni)

Physical vision (maṁsa-cakkhu): The standard eye of flesh.

Divine vision (dibba-cakkhu): Celestial vision developed via absorption (jhāna) and direct knowledges (abhiññā).

Wisdom eye (paññā-cakkhu): The eye of insight (vipassanā) that sees things as they truly are. The Buddha considers those lacking the wisdom eye as spiritually blind (andhabhūto), regardless of their physical sight.

2. Intoxication with Life (Jīvita-Mada)

The subconscious, deluded delusion that "others will die, but I will not die so easily." This latent pride hides the reality of death, causes negligence (pamāda), and leads worldlings into committing unwholesome actions (akusala-kamma).

3. Urgency (Saṁvega) as the Driving Force

Contemplating death acts as a primary base of urgency (saṁvega-vatthu). This sense of urgency generates diligence (appamāda) and eliminates procrastination in spiritual practice, accelerating the path to Nibbāna.

4. The Fine Speed and Momentum of *Maraṇasati*

In a cited discourse, the Buddha evaluated monks practicing maraṇasati. Those who planned to practice for a day, half a day, or the duration of a meal were declared negligent (pamatta). Only those who contemplated mortality with every single morsel of food eaten or between every in-breath and out-breath possessed the requisite sharpness for liberation.

5. The Araka Sutta & Seven Similes on Impermanence

Ancient teacher Araka taught disciples during an era when human lifespan was 60,000 years with only six minor afflictions (cold, hot, hunger, thirst, defecation, urination). He taught the extreme brevity of life (appakaṁ jīvitaṁ) using seven similes:

1. Dewdrop on a blade of grass at sunrise.

2. Water bubble bursting in torrential rain.

3. Line drawn on water with a stick.

4. Mountain river rushing rapidly downward.

5. Glob of spit easily spat out by a strong man.

6. Piece of meat thrown onto a red-hot iron pan.

7. Cow being led step-by-step to the slaughterhouse.

6. Deconstruction of "A Century" (Analytical Computation of Life)

To shatter the illusion that living a hundred years is long, the Buddha systematically unraveled a 100-year lifespan into 300 seasons (hemanta, gimhāna, vassāna), 1,200 months, 2,400 fortnights, 36,000 nights, and under 72,000 meals.

He further subtracted missed meals (bhattantarāya) caused by five factors: anger, grief, illness, observing Uposatha, or failing to obtain food.

7. Other-Referential Contemplation (Parūpamāya) vs. Self-Referential (Attūpamāya)

While loving-kindness (mettā) is cultivated using oneself as the metric (attūpamāya), death contemplation must be cultivated using others as the metric (parūpamāya): seeing others die and immediately applying it to oneself: "This is my nature too."

8. Radical Attention (Yoniso Manasikāra)

Translating daily occurrences—falling leaves, decaying flowers, or observing funerals—into objects of insight meditation (kammaṭṭhāna) rather than superficial entertainment, thereby nurturing Right View (sammā-diṭṭhi).

9. The Attractive Power of Dhamma Practice

The story illustrates that intense, continuous internal meditation generates a spiritual magnet (Dhamma-force) capable of drawing the Buddha himself to assist a practitioner prior to unavoidable karmic events.

10. Development of the Five Spiritual Faculties (Indriya)

True progress is evaluated not through physical senses but through the growth of faith (saddhā), energy (viriya), mindfulness (sati), concentration (samādhi), and wisdom (paññā), all of which are cultivated while actively listening to and contemplating the Dhamma.
