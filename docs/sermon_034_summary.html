Sermon 034 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #34 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #34

Primary Theme: The Four Bases of Sympathy (saṅgaha-vatthu) as the essential framework for individual harmony, true altruism, and social leadership, and their deeper transcendental development into spiritual powers (saṅgaha-balaṁ) leading to Nibbāna.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Hatthaka Āḷavaka, an Anāgāmī (non-returner) lay disciple who was declared by the Buddha as chief (etadagga) among lay disciples who minister to an assembly using the four bases of sympathy.

The Interaction: While residing at the Āgāḷava Shrine in Āḷavī, the Buddha observes Hatthaka Āḷavaka approaching with a large retinue of 500 followers. The Buddha asks him how he manages to minister to and retain such a vast assembly.

The Disaster / Turning Point: The underlying challenge of leading, unifying, and counseling human assemblies without social friction, factionalism, or personal pride, and how social leadership connects to the core practice of the Dhamma.

The Result / Resolution: Hatthaka explains that he ministers to his group strictly through the four bases of sympathy (dāna, peyyavajja, atthacariyā, samānattatā), sincerely tailoring his approach to each individual's needs. The Buddha praises him ("Sādhu! Sādhu!"), affirming that all past, present, and future assembly leaders succeed through these exact selfsame factors. The Buddha then reveals to the Sangha that Hatthaka possesses eight marvelous qualities: the seven noble treasures (saddhā, sīla, hiri, ottappa, suta, cāga, paññā) and fewness of wishes (appiccha).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Four Bases of Sympathy (Saṅgaha-Vatthu):

The Buddha presents four societal linchpins (rathassāṇīva yāyato—like the pin holding a chariot wheel) essential for progress and social cohesion:

Giving (Dāna): Material sacrifice and fulfilling practical needs.

Pleasant Speech (Peyyavajja): Faultless, endearing, polite, and heart-penetrating speech (nelā kaṇṇasukhā pemaniyā hadayaṅgamā porī).

Purposeful Activity (Atthacariyā): Altruistic action and guidance directed toward others' economic and spiritual progress.

Equality/Impartiality (Samānattatā): Practicing non-discrimination, equanimity, and sharing joys and sorrows regardless of class or caste.

2. Underlying Connection to the Four Sublime States (Brahma-Vihāra):

The four bases of sympathy are the practical, outward expressions of the four Brahma-vihāras:

Dāna corresponds to loving-kindness (mettā).

Peyyavajja corresponds to compassion (karuṇā), including constructive, compassionate discipline.

Atthacariyā corresponds to sympathetic

joy (muditā), taking joy in others' welfare and growth.

Samānattatā corresponds to equanimity (upekkhā), abandoning caste/class pride and treating others with equal care.

3. Parents as the Primary Model (Brahmā ti Mātāpitaro):

Parents earn honor and respect (mānatā pūjā) from their children precisely because they implement these four qualities: feeding children starting with mother's milk (dāna), using lullabies and protective discipline (peyyavajja), teaching arts and sciences (atthacariyā), and handing over heritages and caring for them equanimously (samānattatā).

4. The Transcendental Elevation of the Four Bases (Saṅgaha-Balaṁ):

At the highest level, the Buddha elevates the four bases into a spiritual power aimed directly at Nibbāna:

Highest Giving (Etadaggaṁ dānānaṁ): The gift of Dhamma (dhammadāna).

Highest Pleasant Speech (Etadaggaṁ peyyavajjānaṁ): Repeatedly preaching the Dhamma (punappunaṁ dhammaṁ deseti) to those who eagerly bend their ear to listen (ohitasotassa).

Highest Purposeful Activity (Etadaggaṁ atthacariyānam): Establishing an unfaithful person in faith (saddhā), an immoral person in virtue (sīla), a stingy person in generosity (cāga), and an unwise person in wisdom (paññā).

Highest Equality (Etadaggaṁ samānattatānaṁ): Spiritual equality based on noble path-attainments (a Sotāpanna equal to a Sotāpanna, a Sakadāgāmī to a Sakadāgāmī, an Anāgāmī to an Anāgāmī, and an Arahant to an Arahant).

5. The Four Mental Powers (Bala):

To engage in true service and achieve spiritual release, one must cultivate four internal powers:

Power of Wisdom (Paññā-balaṁ): Discriminating between wholesome (kusala) and unwholesome (akusala), blameworthy (sāvajja) and blameless (anavajja), dark (kaṇha) and bright (sukka) teachings.

Power of Energy (Viriya-balaṁ): Exercising the fourfold right effort (sammappadhāna) to abandon bad states and cultivate good ones (chandaṁ janeti vāyamati viriyaṁ ārabhati...).

Power of Blamelessness (Anavajja-balaṁ): Maintaining faultless bodily, verbal, and mental conduct.

Power of Sympathy (Saṅgaha-balaṁ): Mastering the four bases of sympathy in their highest sense.

6. Overcoming the Five Social and Spiritual Fears:

A noble disciple endowed with the four powers transcends five major fears that paralyze ordinary beings:

Fear regarding livelihood (ājīvika-bhaya)

Fear of ill-repute/censure (asiloka-bhaya)

Fear of assemblies/stage fright (parisasārajja-bhaya)

Fear of death (maraṇa-bhaya)

Fear of evil destinations (duggati-bhaya)

7. Analysis of the Four Types of Individuals (Attahita vs. Parahita):

The Buddha categorizes individuals based on personal welfare (attahita) and others' welfare (parahita):

Neva attahitāya na parahitāya: Neither works for self nor others (like a funeral pyre firebrand, alātakaṁ, smeared with excrement—completely useless).

Parahitāya paṭipanno no attahitāya: Works for others, but not self.

Attahitāya paṭipanno no parahitāya: Works for self, but not others (declared superior to the second person, because one standing in a mud pit cannot extract another from the mud).

Attahitāya ca parahitāya ca: Works for both self and others (the supreme, most excellent individual).

8. Proper Context of Dhammapada Verse 166:

"Attadatthaṁ paratthena, bahunāpi na hāpaye..." ("For the sake of another's benefit, however great, one should not neglect one's own true benefit"). One must not commit unwholesome actions (violating precepts or damaging spiritual progress) under the pretense of "social service" or helping others. Spiritual self-development is the prerequisite for true altruism.

9. Sympathetic Joy (Muditā) vs. Social Envy (Issā):

Envy is identified as a major social parasite. Modern competition often generates hatred and envy instead of celebrating achievements. The cultivation of muditā (feeling joy in others' success) is essential to counteract envy and ensure genuine, non-possessive altruism.

10. Critique of Commercialized Counseling and the Three Types of Sight:

Effective guidance/counseling cannot be done with a selfish or commercial mindset. The Buddha classifies people into:

The Blind (Andha): Lacks vision of both worldly and spiritual welfare.

The One-Eyed (Eka-cakkhu): Short-sighted, focused only on worldly/economic gain through devious means.

The Two-Eyed (Dvi-cakkhu): Understands both worldly welfare and the spiritual path leading to Nibbāna. True counselors must possess two-eyed vision.

11. Graduated Teaching Method (Anupubbi-Kathā):

The Buddha leads individuals gradually from basic social virtues to liberation: starting with talk on giving (dāna-kathā), virtue (sīla-kathā), heavenly rewards (sagga-kathā), the danger of sensual pleasures (kāmānaṁ ādīnava), and culminating in the Four Noble Truths and Nibbāna.
