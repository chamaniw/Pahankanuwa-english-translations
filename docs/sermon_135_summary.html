Sermon 135 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #135 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #135 (Pahankaṇuwa Sinhala Audio Sermon No. 139)

Primary Theme: Redefining true asceticism (Samaṇa) through the Buddha's fourfold classification of ascetics, the refutation of passive/naive virtue, and the central role of resolute, multi-staged Right Effort (Sammappadhāna) in realizing Nibbāna.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Cunda the smith (Cunda Kammāraputta), a devoted lay follower, goldsmith, and creator of fine gold jewelry, who offered the Buddha his final alms-meal at Pāvā.

The Interaction: Cunda hosted the Buddha and the Sangha for a meal featuring a dish called sūkara-maddava (interpreted by Ven. Nanananda as a medicinal rejuvenator prepared to ease the Buddha's illness). Cunda served curries and delicacies to the monks in high-value golden vessels.

The Disaster / Turning Point: After the meal, while waiting to listen to the Dhamma, Cunda observed a corrupt monk secretly steal one of the golden vessels and conceal it in his shoulder bag. To prevent disrepute to the Dispensation and avoid giving leverage to external sectarians, Cunda remained silent, but a troubling doubt arose in his mind: "Can such corrupt, thieves in yellow robes truly be considered ascetics (Samaṇas)?"

The Result / Resolution: Seeking clarity, Cunda approached the Buddha that evening and asked how many types of Samaṇas exist in the world (Kati loke samaṇā...?). The Buddha delivered the Cunda Sutta (Sutta Nipāta), classifying ascetics into four distinct categories and reassuring Cunda that "not all are alike" (sabbe nekātādiso), thereby restoring his unshakeable faith (saddhā).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Fourfold Typology of Ascetics (Caturo Samaṇā):

The Buddha asserts that there are strictly four types of ascetics in the world, with no fifth (na pañcamo'tthī):

Conqueror of the Path (Maggajina): The Buddha himself, who rediscovered the lost path to Nibbāna.

Teacher of the Path (Maggadesaka / Maggadesī): The Arahant who, having fully realized Nibbāna, uses analytical knowledges (paṭisambhidā) to fearlessly explain the Dhamma.

One Living on the Path (Maggajīvī): The trainees (Sekha—from Stream-enterer to Non-returner) and the virtuous noble worldling (Kalyāṇa-puthujjana) who practice restraint and mindfulness.

Defiler of the Path (Maggadūsī): The corrupt monk who merely assumes the outward appearance of an ascetic while destroying the path.

2. Analytical Anatomy of the Path-Defiler (Maggadūsī):

The sermon highlights terms used by the Buddha to characterize corrupt ascetics (Samaṇa-patirūpaka):

Pakkhandī: An intruder who pushes himself forward based on seniority or worldly wealth.

Kuladūsako: A corruptor of lay families' faith.

Palāpo: An empty husk or chaff—appearing like a genuine grain of paddy from the outside, but hollow within.

3. Discernment of the Wise Lay Disciple (Ariya-sāvaka):

An intelligent householder recognizes that "not all are alike" (sabbe nekātādiso). By distinguishing between authentic path-walkers and counterfeit defilers, the lay disciple avoids losing faith in the Triple Gem when encountering corrupt individuals.

4. Exclusive Presence of True Ascetics (Mahāsīhanāda Sutta):

The Buddha issued a lion's roar declaring that the four true ascetics (the four pairs of Noble Disciples) exist exclusively within his Dispensation (Idheva samaṇo... suññā parappavādā samaṇehi aññehitī), because external traditions lack the direct realization of gratification (assāda), danger (ādīnava), and escape (nissaraṇa).

5. The Three Core Ascetic Duties (Samaṇa-karaṇīyāni):

True asceticism is defined by an intense, keen desire (tibba chanda) to train in three higher trainings:

Higher Virtue (Adhisīla-sikkhā)

Higher Mind / Concentration (Adhicitta-sikkhā)

Higher Wisdom (Adhipaññā-sikkhā)

6. The Irony of False Ascetics (The Donkey Simile):

The Buddha compared a monk who lacks zeal for the three higher trainings to a donkey following a herd of cows, braying "Aham-api amhā, aham-api amhā" ("I too am a cow!"). Though such a monk cries "Aham-api bhikkhu" ("I too am a monk!"), he lacks the voice, color, and conduct of a true monastic.

7. Critique of Passive/Naive Virtue (Samaṇamaṇḍikā Sutta):

The wanderer Uggāhamāna claimed that anyone who refrains from evil bodily/verbal actions, evil thoughts, and evil livelihood is a supreme Samaṇa. The Buddha refuted this by pointing out that by such logic, a helpless infant lying on its back (dārako mandāsayāno) would be a supreme ascetic. True purity requires active eradication of latent defilements (anusaya), not mere passive physical incapacity.

8. The Ten Factors of the Fully Accomplished Ascetic (Dasa-aṅga):

An Arahant is defined not merely by the Noble Eightfold Path, but by ten factors—adding Right Knowledge (Sammā-ñāṇa) and Right Liberation (Sammā-vimutti) once the path is fully traversed.

9. The Fourfold Division of Right Effort (Sammappadhāna):

Practical spiritual striving consists of four dynamic tasks (illustrated via the analogy of building a bridge):

Restraint (Saṃvara): Preventing unarisen unwholesome states.

Abandoning (Pahāna): Eliminating existing unwholesome states.

Development (Bhāvanā): Arousing unarisen wholesome states.

Protection/Maintenance (Anurakkhaṇā): Preserving and expanding already arisen wholesome states.

10. The Five-Stage Spectrum of Energy Generation:

The Buddha’s precise vocabulary details the psychological progression of authentic effort:

Chandaṃ janeti: Arousing initial wholesome interest/desire.

Vāyamati: Striving; initial effort and preparation (like placing a lever under a boulder).

Viriyaṃ ārabhati: Mobilizing energetic manly strength (purisa-thāma).

Cittaṃ paggaṇhāti: Fortifying/exerting the mind against emerging obstacles.

Padahati: Resolute, life-risking ultimate exertion.

11. Ultimate Resolute Striving (Caturanga-samannāgata-viriya):

The ultimate striving (padhāna) is modeled on the Buddha's determination under the Bodhi tree: accepting that skin (taco), sinews (nahāru), and bones (aṭṭhi) may remain while flesh and blood (maṃsa-lohitaṃ) dry up, so long as the goal is not yet reached through manly perseverance.

12. The Final Alms-Food and Exoneration of Cunda:

The sermon contextualizes Cunda’s offering of sūkara-maddava, clarifying that its consumption re-triggered a suppressed illness (lohita-pakkhandikā) rather than causing it. The Buddha explicitly instructed Ānanda to relieve Cunda of any guilt, declaring that the alms-meal directly preceding Parinibbāna bears equal fruit and result to the milk-rice offered by Sujātā prior to Supreme Enlightenment.
