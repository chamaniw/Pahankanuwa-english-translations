Sermon 111 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #111 Summary

Sermon #111 Summary

---

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #111 (Sinhala Audio Sermon No. 16)

Primary Theme: The supreme value of resolute spiritual effort (vīriya) in the offering of practice (paṭipatti-pūjā), illustrating how acute mental distress and conflict (ghattana) can be transmuted through wise attention (yoniso manasikāra) and recollection of virtue (sīlānussati) into immediate liberation (arahatta).

---

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: A young noble from Sāvatthī who ordained under the Buddha, later known in the Dispensation as Venerable Sappadāsaka ("the monk whose servant was a serpent").

The Interaction: After receiving higher ordination (upasampadā), he became discontented (anabhirati) with monastic life and contemplated disrobing. Out of self-respect, however, he determined that dying as a monk was far superior to returning to the lay state.

The Disaster / Turning Point: He actively sought ways to commit suicide. First, he attempted to force a venomous cobra (found by other monks) to bite him—even putting his finger into its mouth—but the snake refused (having been his devoted servant in his third past life). Subsequently, during a hair-shaving session, he took a razor, went to a tree, leaned his neck back, and placed the sharp blade against his windpipe, fully prepared to cut his own throat.

The Result / Resolution: At the critical moment before slicing his throat, a sudden reflection (āvarjana) arose regarding his spotless virtue (sīla) since higher ordination. This triggered the recollection of virtue (sīlānussati), suffusing his body with intense rapture (pīti) and concentration (samādhi). Calming the rapture, he immediately directed his mind to insight-knowledge (vipassanā-ñāṇa), using the "razor of wisdom" to sever all underlying defilements (kilesas), realizing supreme Arahantship on the spot.

---

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Primacy of the Offering of Practice (*Paṭipatti-Pūjā*) over Material Offerings (*Āmisa-Pūjā*)

While Vesak traditionally prompts lavish material offerings (āmisa-pūjā), the Buddha esteemed paṭipatti-pūjā as highest. The monumental effort (vīriya) exerted by the Buddha over four asaṅkheyya and one hundred thousand kalpas, as well as His six years of self-mortification, is honored only when disciples exert firm fourfold energy to tread the Noble Eightfold Path.

2. Recollection of Virtue (*Sīlānussati*) as a Gateway to Deep Concentration (*Samādhi*)

In moments of profound crisis, reflecting on immaculate moral conduct (sīla) acts as a potent meditation subject (kammaṭṭhāna). Venerable Sappadāsaka’s sudden awareness of his unbroken virtue generated a massive wave of meditative rapture (pīti), which stilled his mind and provided the requisite meditative baseline for insight.

3. Distinction Between Investigation (*Sammasana*) and Clinging/Grasping (*Parāmasana*)

Ven. Nanananda Thero highlights a linguistic and doctrinal distinction using the razor metaphor:

Sammasana (comprehension/delicate touching): Holding the razor delicately as in shaving; metaphorically, applying gentle, wise contemplation to investigate the Three Characteristics (tilakkhaṇa) of mental phenomena without self-entanglement.

Parāmasana (grasping/clinging): Holding the blade tightly to cut/brandish; metaphorically, the rigid, unyielding grasp of a personal self-notion (attā-saññā) that turns mental conflicts into destructive violence or addiction.

4. The Interplay Between the Wheel of Kamma (*Kamma-Cakka*) and the Wheel of Dhamma (*Dhamma-Cakka*)

Sappadāsaka's suicidal inclination stemmed from past unwholesome karma during Kassapa Buddha's era, where—out of greed (lobha) for requisites (pirikara)—he encouraged a fellow monk to disrobe. That karma spun back, pointing a razor at his own neck. Conversely, his capacity for instant enlightenment was undergirded by twenty thousand years of previous monastic practice (samaṇa-dhamma).

5. Refutation of Suicide as a Solution to Suffering

Physical destruction of the body driven by self-hatred (dosa) leaves the latent tendency of craving (taṇhānusaye) intact. As stated in Dhammapada 112's contextual teachings, just as a felled tree sprouts anew if its root remains undamaged, saṁsāric suffering (dukkha) recurs endlessly until latency (anusaya) is eradicated through insight, not bodily violence.

6. Transmuting Mental Conflict (*Ghattana*) into Perception of Non-Self (*Anattā-Saññā*)

Mental crises, worldly distress, and loss are opportunities for yoniso manasikāra (wise attention). By abandoning self-referential aversion ("Why is this happening to me?") and observing mental conflict simply as an impermanent, uncontrollable flow of conditions, one converts emotional sorrow into the "taste of Nibbāna," following the precedent of disciples like Paṭācārā and Kisāgotamī.

7. Swiftness of Mind (*Lahuparivatta Citta*) and Timelessness (*Akālika*) of Realization

For a practitioner of aroused energy (āraddhavīriya), the mind can penetrate the Truths with extreme speed—even before a raised foot touches the ground. The Dhamma is sandiṭṭhika (directly visible) and akālika (timeless); liberation depends not on long duration, but on the intensity of insight applied to the swiftly changing mind.

8. The Three Stages of Saṁsāric Experience: Gratification (*Assāda*), Danger (*Ādīnava*), and Escape (*Nissaraṇa*)

Assāda: Sensory gratification, the initial trap where uninstructed worldlings spend their lives.

Ādīnava: Recognizing the inherent suffering, volatility, and karmic danger of sense pleasures.

Nissaraṇa: Leaping out of the circular ring of Dependent Arising (paṭicca-samuppāda). Realizing nissaraṇa requires active energy (vīriya) to break out of habitual saṁsāric loops.

9. Mindfulness and Clear Comprehension (*Sati-Sampajañña*) as Insulation

Continuous mindfulness (sati) and wise awareness (sampajañña) act like rubber insulation around an electric wire. By clearly comprehending mental and physical activities (rising, passing away, bodily movements), defilements cannot find a footing to generate new karmic momentum.

10. Maturation of the Five Spiritual Faculties (*Indriya*) into Governing Powers (*Bala*)

Listening to and practicing Dhamma transforms the five spiritual faculties—Faith (saddhā), Energy (vīriya), Mindfulness (sati), Concentration (samādhi), and Wisdom (paññā)—into ruling powers (indriya-bala). When these faculties exercise sovereign control over the mental continuum, the mind easily transcends worldly conditions and realizes Nibbāna.
