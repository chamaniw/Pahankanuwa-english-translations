Sermon 080 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #80 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #80 (Sinhala Audio Sermon No. 178)

Primary Theme: Calming defiled intentions (saṅkappa-vūpasama) through the direct contemplation of the Three Characteristics (tilakkhaṇa), and understanding the radical Middle Path as an instrumental process of non-grasping (anupādā) leading to the cessation of becoming (bhava-nirodha).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist:

The Brahmin Koṇḍañña (later Venerable Aññā Koṇḍañña), who possessed past pāramīs to become the chief monk of long standing (rattaññū etadagga) in the Buddha's Dispensation, along with the other four Brahmins who formed the Group of Five Monks (Pañcavaggiya: Bhaddiya, Vappa, Mahānāma, and Assaji).

The Interaction:

As a young Brahmin skilled in physiognomy, Koṇḍañña alone raised a single finger at Prince Siddhartha’s naming ceremony, definitively predicting he would become a fully Awakened Buddha. Decades later, when Prince Siddhartha renounced the world, Koṇḍañña recruited four companions to accompany the Bodhisatta, attending upon him for six years during his intense self-mortification (duṣkarakriyā) at Uruvelā.

The Disaster / Turning Point:

When the Bodhisatta realized the futility of extreme self-mortification, adopted the Middle Path (majjhimā paṭipadā), and accepted solid almsfood, the Group of Five misperceived his actions as abandoning spiritual effort for a life of luxury. Disillusioned, they abandoned him and went to Isipatana in Varanasi.

After his Enlightenment, when the Buddha approached them at Isipatana, they stubbornly agreed not to pay homage, receive his robes, or address him properly, addressing him merely as 'āvuso' ("friend"). Thrice they rejected his declaration of Buddhahood, believing Enlightenment was impossible without bodily mortification (attakilamathānuyoga).

The Result / Resolution:

The Buddha performed the "miracle of truth," asking if he had ever spoken to them in such a manner before. Awakened to faith, they listened to the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta. Ven. Koṇḍañña became the first human to attain Stream-entry (sotāpatti-phala), prompting the Buddha's inspired utterance (udāna): "Aññāsi vata bho Koṇḍañño!"

Later, after training under the Buddha in pairs while others gathered alms, all five attained Arahantship upon hearing the Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta. Ven. Koṇḍañña subsequently spent twelve years dwelling in humble, quiet retreat in the Chaddanta forest in the Himalayas, served by wild elephants, where he uttered the thematic verses of Dhammapada 277–279.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Preeminence and Humility of Ven. Aññā Koṇḍañña (Rattaññū):

Ven. Koṇḍañña held the foremost position (etadagga) among monks of long standing (rattaññū—those who had spent the most nights in the Dispensation) and was the first human to realize the Dhamma. Despite receiving veneration from chief disciples like Sāriputta and Moggallāna, his profound humility led him to seek absolute solitude in the wilderness to abide in meditation.

2. Calming Defiled Intentions (Saṅkappa-Vūpasama) Through Wisdom:

Worldly objects (vicitrāni) agitate lustful thoughts (saṅkappa) through the perception of beauty (subha-saññā). Just as a sudden downpour calms a cloud of wind-blown dust (rajamūhatam ca vātena yathā meghopasammaye), penetrating insight into the Three Characteristics (tilakkhaṇa) instantly stills these defiled, discursive thoughts.

3. The Doctrinal Nuance Between Saṅkhāra and Dhamma:

The sermon highlights a crucial linguistic and doctrinal distinction in Dhammapada 277–279:

"Sabbe saṅkhārā aniccā" & "Sabbe saṅkhārā dukkhā": All conditioned formations/preparations (saṅkhāra) are impermanent and unsatisfactory.

"Sabbe dhammā anattā": All phenomena/things (dhammā—including unconditioned states, concepts, and mind-objects) are not-self.

4. Mind-Objects (Dhammā) as the Final Citadel of Non-Grasping:

Drawing from the Girimānanda Sutta, where anicca-saññā applies to the five aggregates while anattā-saññā extends across the six internal and external sense bases, Ven. Nanananda highlights that the ultimate object to be seen as anattā is the mind-object (dhammā) itself (mano anattā, dhammā anattā). Liberation requires relinquishing even the conceptual object of "Nibbāna" (Mūlapariyāya Sutta).

5. The Middle Path (Majjhimā Paṭipadā) Beyond Physical Extremes:

The Middle Path is not merely avoiding bodily indulgence (kāmasukhallikānuyoga) and self-mortification (attakilamathānuyoga). At its deepest level, it is rooted in Dependent Arising (paṭicca-samuppāda). It consists of using spiritual phenomena as temporary supports to cross the flood of Saṁsāra (nissāya nissāya oghassa nittharaṇā) without clinging to those supports.

6. The Simile of the Raft (Kullūpama Sutta / Alagaddūpama Sutta):

The Dhamma is taught for the purpose of crossing over (nittharaṇatthāya), not for holding onto or carrying on one's back (no gahaṇatthāya). Just as a traveler constructs a raft out of twigs and grass to cross a wild river and then abandons it upon reaching the far shore, all spiritual concepts, meditation techniques, and views must ultimately be relinquished.

7. Progressive Purification via the Seven Chariots (Rathavinīta Sutta):

The journey to Nibbāna operates like King Kosala’s relay of seven horse-chariots. Each of the seven purifications (satta-visuddhi—from virtue up to knowledge and vision) exists solely to propel the practitioner to the next stage. The final purification is developed not for its own sake, but for anupādā parinibbāna—extinguishment through non-grasping.

8. Cessation of Becoming (Bhava-Nirodha) as True Nibbāna:

Worldlings falsely conceptualize Nibbāna as a realm or a novel world. However, Dhamma reveals that birth, decay, and death (jāti-jarā-maraṇa) are condition-bound phenomena within Dependent Arising. Grasping conditions becoming (upādāna-paccayā bhavo). Nibbāna is simply the complete cessation of becoming (bhava-nirodha) brought about by total letting go.

9. Navigating the Four Floods (Oghataraṇa Sutta):

Saṁsāra is a series of four sweeping floods: sensuality (kāma), becoming (bhava), views (diṭṭhi), and ignorance (avijjā). Wild struggling (self-mortification) causes one to sink, while standing still (sensual indulgence) causes one to be swept away. The Middle Path utilizes the momentum of the current step-by-step to cross safely.

10. The Eleven Modes of Comprehensive Investigation (Sammasana):

To cultivate disillusionment (nibbidā), one must actively contemplate each of the five aggregates of grasping (pañcupādānakkhandha) across eleven analytical modes: as impermanent (aniccato), suffering (dukkhato), a disease (rogato), a boil (gaṇḍato), a dart (sallato), a calamity (aghato), an affliction (ābādhato), alien (parato), disintegrating (palokato), empty (suññato), and not-self (anattato).

11. Overcoming Clinging (Parāmasana) vs. Investigative Reflection (Sammasana):

Uninstructed worldlings practice misapprehension and tight clinging (parāmasana) toward mental and physical phenomena. The noble disciple replaces this with comprehensive investigation (sammasana), recognizing phenomena as empty of a self or what belongs to a self (suññamidaṁ attena vā attaniyena vā).

12. The Dynamics of Expectation and Spontaneous Realization:

While right determination and effort are essential, active expectation (āsa) acts as an obstacle to insight (vipassanā). Path-realization (magga-phala) typically occurs naturally and unexpectedly when the conditions of insight mature in the present moment, precisely when all subtle expectations and mental graspings fall away.
