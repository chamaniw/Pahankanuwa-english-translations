Sermon 091 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #91 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #91 (Sinhala Audio Sermon No. 33)

Primary Theme: The transcendent vision of birth (jāti/uppatti) and death (cuti), the true nature of a "being" (satta) as attachment to the five aggregates, and the total extinguishing (nirodha) of the fire of existence (bhava) through insight wisdom (vipassanā-ñāṇa).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The Brahmin Vaṅgīsa of Rājagaha, who possessed a supernormal skill/mantra of tapping the skull of a deceased person to declare their exact realm of rebirth.

The Interaction: Opportunistic Brahmins exploited Vaṅgīsa's ability for monetary gain and prestige, taking him from village to village. Arriving near Jetavana Monastery in Sāvatthi, they challenged the Buddha's lay followers, claiming Vaṅgīsa was superior to the Buddha. The debate led the Brahmins to visit the Buddha to test their skill.

The Disaster / Turning Point: The Buddha prepared five skulls: four belonging to beings reborn in hell, the animal realm, the human world, and the deva realm, and a fifth belonging to an Arahant. Vaṅgīsa correctly identified the first four rebirths, receiving the Buddha’s praise. However, upon tapping the Arahant’s skull, Vaṅgīsa was rendered completely speechless, unable to discern any destination. Desiring to learn this superior "mantra," Vaṅgīsa agreed to ordain under the Buddha.

The Result / Resolution: Utilizing skill in means (upāya-kosalla), the Buddha gave Vaṅgīsa the tacapañcaka (five body parts ending with skin) and the 32 parts of the body (asubha bhāvanā), focusing naturally on bones/skulls (aṭṭhi). Vaṅgīsa meditated with deep commitment (chanda), saw through the void nature of the aggregates, and attained Arahantship. The Buddha subsequently declared Dhammapada verses 419–420, confirming that Vaṅgīsa was a true Brahmin (brāhmaṇa) whose path and destination (gati) are entirely unknown to devas, gandharvas, or humans.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Ultimate Vision of Birth and Death (Cuti-Uppatti):

While worldly yogis may use mundane abhiññā to locate mundane rebirth destinations, the Buddha revealed the deepest vision: an Arahant completely transcends the dual concepts of birth (uppatti) and death (cuti). An Arahant is like an extinguished lamp (nibbuto), leaving no trace or destination (gati).

2. Re-Definition of a "Being" (Satta):

Citing the Rādha Sutta, the Thero highlights the Buddha’s radical etymological analysis of satta: a "being" is not an enduring entity, but simply one who is trapped by desire (chanda), lust (rāga), delight (nandī), and craving (taṇhā) attached (satta/visatta) to the five aggregates (pañcakkhandha). Liberation consists of becoming asatta (unattached).

3. Skillful Means (Upāya-Kosalla) through Foulness Meditation (Asubha Bhāvanā):

The Buddha tailored the tacapañcaka meditation (especially focusing on the 8th factor, aṭṭhi or bone/skull) directly to Vaṅgīsa's personality. Having tapped others' skulls for profit, Vaṅgīsa was led to "tap his own skull" internally, turning a worldly trick into a vehicle for liberation.

4. Contemplation of the Skull and Universal Law (Dhammatā):

Reflecting on a skull reveals the emptiness of sensory existence: where eyes, ears, nose, and tongue once sought pleasures, nothing remains. This brings urgency (saṃvega) and establishes insight into the Three Characteristics (tilakkhaṇa): Impermanence (anicca), Suffering (dukkha), and Not-self (anattā). To avoid morbidity or suicide, foulness must be seen not merely as personal disgust, but as a universal law of nature (dhammatā).

5. Deconstructing Solidity (Ghana-Saññā) into a Multitude (Rāsi-Saññā):

The worldly illusion of a solid self ("I am," asmi-māna) is sustained by the perception of solidity (ghana-saññā). Insight wisdom (vipassanā-ñāṇa) breaks down the five aggregates into a rapid flux of arisings and passings away, establishing the perception of a multitude (rāsi-saññā)—similar to seeing a solid waterfall break into countless individual water droplets upon closer inspection.

6. The Playhouse/Sandcastle Metaphor (Kīḷā-Geha):

Referencing the Buddha's simile to Ven. Rādha, the sermon compares the five aggregates to sandcastles built by children. Children fiercely protect their clay playhouses, but upon growing up, they naturally kick them apart and move on. Similarly, an insight meditator naturally "scatters" and relinquishes attachment to Form, Feeling, Perception, Preparations, and Consciousness through maturity of wisdom.

7. Existence (Bhava) as a Spreading Fire:

Existence is not a soul traveling through time, but a continuous fire. Grasping (upādāna) acts as fuel, fueled by the wind of Craving (taṇhā) and Ignorance (avijjā). Citing the historical Sinhala work Lōväḍa Saňgarā, the Thero emphasizes: just as a burning pile of wood is called "fire" rather than "wood," the so-called "body/person" is in reality only a heap of continuous Suffering (dukkha). Nibbāna is simply the extinguishing (nirodha) of this fire.

8. Wise Attention (Yoniso Manasikāra) vs. Unwise Attention (Ayoniso Manasikāra):

Unwise attention runs after sensory objects, capturing signs (nimitta) and minor features (anubyañjana), creating a dreamlike secondary reality that Kindles thoughts (vitakka) and Becoming (bhava). Wise attention turns back to the point of origin, subduing agitating objects by noting them neutrally ("form as mere form," "sound as mere sound"), bringing the mind to equanimity (upekkhā).

9. The Metaphor of the Tortoise (Kummūpama):

Just as a tortoise draws its limbs and head safely inside its shell upon encountering danger, an insight practitioner retracts mental wandering (manovitakka) and sensory proliferation, preventing Māra from establishing a foothold.

10. "Dying Before You Die" and the Three Gateways to Liberation (Vimokkha-Mukha):

Worldly death involves a desperate "tug-of-war" with Māra by grasping at the thread of life. The Dhamma teaches one to "die before dying" by letting go of ownership over the five aggregates while still alive. This is accomplished by penetrating the three gateways to liberation:

Signless (animitta): Erasing the sign-nature of objects.

Desireless (appaṇihita): Abandoning the search for a permanent stance or footing.

Void (suññatā): Realizing the hollow, empty, and non-self nature of all experienced phenomena.
