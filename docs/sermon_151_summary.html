Sermon 151 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #151 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #151 (Audio Sermon No. 46)

Primary Theme: The destructive nature of wealth when combined with craving (taṇhā) and stinginess (macchariya), contrasted with the true purpose of giving (dāna) and relinquishment (cāga) as an insight-oriented practice of letting go that leads toward Nibbāna.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The childless treasurer (seṭṭhi) of Sāvatthi, King Pasenadi of Kosala, and the Buddha.

The Interaction: King Pasenadi comes to visit the Buddha at midday, directly after supervising the official confiscation of the deceased treasurer's vast estate—consisting of nearly ten million gold coins alone—to the royal treasury. The king reveals that despite his immense fortune, the treasurer lived in squalor: eating broken rice and sour gruel, wearing coarse hempen rags, and driving a broken-down cart.

The Disaster / Turning Point: The Buddha discloses the kamma underlying the treasurer's paradox. In a past life, he ordered alms to be given to the Pacceka Buddha Tagarasikhi, but immediately regretted the gift afterward, thinking the food was wasted and would have been better given to laborers. In that same life, he murdered his brother's young son to inherit his estate.

The Result / Resolution: The momentary volition of ordering alms yielded seven heavenly births and seven lifetimes as a wealthy treasurer. However, his subsequent regret defiled the deed, causing him to be afflicted by the "rust of stinginess" (macchera-mala), incapable of enjoying his wealth or using it for meritorious deeds. For the murder, he suffered in hell for hundreds of thousands of years and lived seven lifetimes childless. Having exhausted all past merit without accumulating new merit, he was reborn in the Mahāroruva hell upon his death. The Buddha then uttered Dhammapada verse 355 to illustrate how craving for wealth destroys the foolish (dummedho), while seekers of the further shore (pāragavesino) utilize wealth without being ruined by it.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Destruction of the Foolish (Bhogā Dummedhaṃ Hanti): Wealth and prosperity are seen by the world as luck, but for a foolish person (dummedha) overcome by craving (taṇhā), material possessions become the instrument of self-destruction, blinding them to Nibbāna (no ca pāragavesino).

2. The Rust of Stinginess (Macchera-Mala): Stinginess is described in the Dhamma as a "rust" (maccheraṃ dadataṃ malaṃ). Just as rust corrodes metal and freezes door hinges, stinginess renders the mind impure, narrow, and structurally incapable of bending toward giving or enjoying life.

3. The Threefold Mind in Giving (Tividha-Cetanā): For a gift to be complete and yield great fruit and benefit (mahatphala mahānisaṃsa), three volitions must be purified:

Preliminary joy before giving (pūrva-cetanā / pubbeva dānā sumano).

Clarity/confidence during the act of giving (muñcana-cetanā / dadaṃ cittaṃ pasādaye).

Gladness after having given (apara-cetanā / datvā attamano hoti).

Regret or remorse (vipiṭisāra) defiles the mind and severely corrupts the ultimate result of the wholesome act.

4. The Three Factors of Success in Giving (Sampatti): The magnitude of a gift's fruit depends on:

The virtue and purity of intention of the giver (dāyaka-sampatti).

The fine quality of the offered object (deyya-dhamma-sampatti).

The spiritual virtue of the recipient (paṭigrāhaka-sampatti), who serves as a field of merit (puññakkhetta).

5. Consuming Past Merit vs. Creating New Merit (Purāṇa): Enjoying wealth from past merit without gathering new merit is like eating "stale food" (purāṇa) or a farmer consuming all seed-paddy without planting a new crop. This principle is emphasized through the story of Visākhā correcting her father-in-law, Treasurer Migāra.

6. Relinquishment as a Noble Treasure (Cāga-Dhana): Generosity (cāga) is one of the Seven Noble Treasures (sapta āriya-dhana). Developing the volition of relinquishment grants the practitioner the mental strength to remain composed and reconciled during life's loss, tragedy, or material destruction.

7. Adornment of the Mind (Cittālaṅkāra) and Recollection of Generosity (Cāgānussati): The deepest, noble purpose of giving is cittālaṅkāraṃ dānaṃ deti—giving for the adornment and preparation of the mind. By practicing the recollection of generosity (cāgānussati), reflecting on a mind free from stinginess (vigatamalamaccherena cetasā), five hindrances are discarded and concentration (samādhi) is attained.

8. Giving as an Insight Practice (Vipassanā): Relinquishing external objects (gold, lands, goods) serves as the primary exercise to erode the deep-seated illusion of "mine" (anattā). This preliminary practice of letting go prepares the mind for the ultimate relinquishment of internal identities—the five aggregates (pañcakkhandha).

9. The Deity Sērī's Example and True Conviction (Saddhā): The story of Deva Sērī demonstrates that being a giver (dāyaka), a lord of giving (dānapati), and one who praises giving (dānassa vaṇṇavādī) builds an inexhaustible store of merit (puñña), which serves as the true foundation (patiṭṭhā) for living beings in the world beyond.

10. The Universal Continuum of Letting Go (Dāna, Sīla, Bhāvanā): Relinquishment is the underlying thread of the entire Noble Path:

Dāna is letting go of external possessions.

Sīla (Virtue) is letting go of unwholesome bodily and verbal impulses driven by desire.

Bhāvanā (Mental Development) is the highest sacrifice, where one lets go of sensual distractions, old habits, and defilements (kilesa), culminating in insight-wisdom (vipassanā-paññā) and Nibbāna.
