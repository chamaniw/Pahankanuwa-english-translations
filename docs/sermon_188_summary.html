Sermon 188 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #188 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #188 (Paticca Samuppada Sermon 6)

Primary Theme: Ignorance (avijjā) as the root delusion driving the perpetual samsāric cycle of "thisness" (itthabhāva) and "otherwiseness" (aññathābhāva), and Dependent Arising (Paṭicca Samuppāda) as the true philosophical Middle Path (Majjhima Paṭipadā) that transcends linguistic dualities and conceptual imaginings (maññanā).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The Supreme Buddha and a congregation of sixty monks gathered at the Pubbārāma monastery in Sāvatthi.

The Interaction: On a moonlit full-moon night, the Buddha surveys the silent, rapt congregation with his Buddha-eye and initiates a profound sermon (Dvāyatānupassanā Sutta) detailing sixteen modes of contemplating dualities (dvayatānupassanā).

The Disaster / Turning Point: The existential predicament of beings endlessly wandering in Saṁsāra (jāti-maraṇa-saṁsāraṁ), trapped by the delusion of ignorance (avijjā), clinging to assets/aggregates (upadhi), and remaining imprisoned within extreme views of existence (atthitā) versus non-existence (natthitā).

The Result / Resolution: By attending to the fundamental dualities—specifically seeing that all suffering arises due to ignorance/assets and ceases with their remainderless fading away—all sixty listening monks extirpate all mental influxes (āsavakkhaya) and attain full Arahanthood.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Duality of Contemplation (Dvayatānupassanā)

True right view begins with contemplating core dualities: the arising of suffering via ignorance (avijjā) and assets (upadhi, the five aggregates of grasping), versus the absolute cessation of suffering through the remainderless fading away of ignorance.

2. The Dynamic of "Thisness" (Itthabhāva) and "Otherwiseness" (Aññathābhāva)

Birth (jāti) occurs when the mind grasps a phenomenon as a concrete identity or "THIS" (itthabhāva). The moment a "thisness" is established, it inevitably yields to the inexorable law of impermanence, turning into "otherwiseness" (aññathābhāva)—which constitutes decay and death (jarā-maraṇa).

3. Paṭicca Samuppāda as the Epistemological Middle Path (Majjhima Paṭipadā)

While the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta outlines a practical moral middle path between sensual self-indulgence and self-mortification, the Kaccānagotta Sutta reveals Paṭicca Samuppāda as the deeper philosophical Middle Path. It avoids the two extreme ontology-based views of eternalism ("everything exists" – sabbaṁ atthi) and annihilationism ("nothing exists" – sabbaṁ natthi) through the continuous vision of direct arising (samudaya) and ceasing (nirodha).

4. Refusal of Substantive and Locative Standpoints (Bāhiya Sutta Insight)

As taught to Bāhiya Dāruciriya, stopping short at raw sensory experience—where in the seen there is only the seen (diṭṭhe diṭṭhamattaṁ)—prevents the mind from conceiving an instrumental "by it" (tena) or locative "in it" (tattha). Abandoning these linguistic standpoints dismantles the implicit self-identity built into cognitive processes.

5. Non-Dependence and the Cessation of Motion (Nissitassa Calitaṁ)

To rest or lean on dualities (nissita) creates unsteadiness (calitaṁ). Non-leaning (anissita) brings calm (passaddhi), eliminating inclination/craving (nati), which halts coming-and-going (āgati-gati), thereby ending rebirth (cutūpapāto) and bringing an end to the entirety of suffering.

6. The Disease of Conceptual Imagining (Maññanā)

Delusion operates through self-referential mental conceiving (maññanā), such as "I am" (asmīti), "This am I" (ayamahamasmi), or projections regarding future existence (form, formless, percipient, non-percipient). As illustrated by the subtle mental bondage of the demon king Vepacitti (Vepacitti-bandhana), imagining is a disease (rogo), an abscess (gaṇḍo), and a barb (sallaṁ).

7. Radical Attention (Yonisomanasikāra) as the Discovery Method

The Buddhas do not discover Paṭicca Samuppāda from top to bottom, but by investigating radically from the outcome backward: asking what condition leads to decay and death (jarāmaraṇa), identifying birth (jāti), tracking it back through becoming (bhava), grasping (upādāna), craving (taṇhā), feeling (vedanā), contact (phassa), six sense spheres (saḷāyatana), to the vortex of mutual conditionality (aññamaññapacayatā) between consciousness (viññāṇa) and name-and-form (nāmarūpa).

8. Critique of Linguistic Conventions (Lokapaññatti)

Language naturally constructs static "things" out of dynamic processes, solidifying personality view (sakkāyadiṭṭhi). The Tathāgata utilizes worldly conventions, expressions, and usages (lokavohāra) for communication, but does so without grasping them (aparāmasaṁ), recognizing the Dhamma as ultimately beyond the sphere of logic (atakkāvacara).

9. Selective Perception of Compactness (Ghana-Saññā)

Worldlings perpetuate self-illusion by fixating exclusively on the arising phase (samudaya) while ignoring the ceasing phase (nirodha) of phenomena. This cognitive distortion is likened to a "magic kettle" or a reflection in a mirror, where an illusion of static personal identity is maintained despite continuous flux.

10. Deconstructing Self-Conceit via the Elevenfold Aggregates

The conceit "I am" (asmīti) is dismantled by examining each of the five aggregates of grasping (pañcupādānakkhandhā) across eleven modes: past, future, present, internal, external, gross, subtle, inferior, superior, far, and near. Systematic insight into these eleven modes eradicates the latent tendencies (anusaya) of selfhood rooted in past and future projections.
