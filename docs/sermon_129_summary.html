Sermon 129 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #129 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #129 (Pahankaṇuwa Sinhala Audio Dhamma Sermon No. 93)

Primary Theme: The inevitability of blame and praise (nindā-pasaṁsā) as part of the Eight Worldly Conditions (Aṭṭha Lokadhamma), the Buddha’s skillful use of censure (nigraha) and encouragement (pragraha/paggaha) in taming disciples out of compassion (anukampā), the danger of gain, honor, and renown (lābha, sakkāra, siloka), and the necessity of looking beyond foolish worldly yardsticks to establish true Dhamma standards for liberation.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The lay disciple Atula of Sāvatthī and his retinue of five hundred lay followers.

The Interaction: Seeking to listen to the Dhamma, Atula took his retinue to the Jetavana monastery. They first approached Venerable Revata, who remained completely silent in solitary dwelling. Displeased, they went to Venerable Sāriputta, who delivered a long, deep, and analytical sermon on the Abhidhamma. Finding it incomprehensible and overly lengthy, they left in anger and went to Venerable Ānanda, who gave a very brief and simple sermon. Angry that Ānanda spoke so little, Atula went to complain directly to the Blessed One.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Atula expressed his frustration that every monk failed to satisfy him—blaming silence, long sermons, and brief sermons alike. This highlighted the foolish inclination of worldlings to find fault regardless of speech or conduct.

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha uttered the stanzas from Dhammapada 227–228 (Porāṇametaṃ Atula...), pointing out that blame and praise have existed since antiquity and that no person is exclusively blamed or praised. He clarified that praise or blame from foolish, uninvestigative people (bāla) is meaningless, whereas only the censure or praise of the wise (viññū) carries real validity. Through this, Atula and his retinue were established in a proper Dhamma perspective regarding worldly judgment.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Universal Nature of Blame and Praise (Nindā-Pasaṁsā):

Blame and praise form an inescapable storm in the world that affects everyone, including Buddhas, kings, the sun, moon, and the great earth. Ordinary people judge using flawed yardsticks, finding fault with those who remain silent, those who speak at length, and those who speak in moderation.

2. The Validity of Wise Judgment over Foolish Opinion (Viññū vs. Bāla):

Uninvestigated blame or praise brings ruin and accumulates unwholesome kamma. Praise from fools (bālappasaṁsana) leads one astray, whereas the censure of the wise (viññūnaṁ garahā) guides one onto the right path (Garahāva seyyo viññūnaṁ, yañce bālappasaṁsanaṁ).

3. Non-Receptivity to Verbal Abuse (Refusal of Unwholesome "Gifts"):

Illustrated through the story of Brahmin Bhāradvāja, who abused the Buddha with vulgar language. The Buddha responded by explaining that just as unaccepted food offered to a guest remains with the host, unaccepted insults remain entirely with the abuser. Returning abuse for abuse is like "eating together" with the attacker.

4. Strength of Character and Equanimity in the Face of Slander:

Demonstrated through the false accusations leveled by Ciñcā Māṇavikā and the sectarian plot involving Sundarī. The Buddha confronted severe false charges not with panic, but with upright strength ("Sister, whether what you say is true or false, only you and I know"), allowing the natural operation of Dhamma to expose the falsehood.

5. Patient Endurance without Fleeing Conflict:

In Kosambī, when Queen Māgandiyā hired townspeople to revile the monks, Ven. Ānanda suggested fleeing to another city. The Buddha refused, stating that issues must be settled where they arise. He likened himself to an elephant in battle (nāgo va saṅgāme) enduring arrows, noting that groundless blame naturally subsides after seven days.

6. The Charioteer of Men (Purisadammasārathī) — Censure (Nigraha) and Encouragement (Pragraha/Paggaha):

Disciplining disciples requires both censure (nigraha) for wrong behavior (e.g., addressing monks as mogha purisa or strictly rebuking the Chabbaggiya monks) and praise (pragraha/paggaha) for right behavior. The Buddha tames beings with gentle words, harsh words, or a mixture of both (saṇhena pi vinemi, pharusena pi vinemi).

7. Censure Motivated Purely by Compassion (Anukampā):

In the dialogue with Prince Abhayarāja regarding Nigaṇṭha Nāthaputta's "two-horned dilemma" (ubhatokoṭika), the Buddha explained that he speaks disagreeable truths at the proper time solely out of compassion (anukampā), just as a father would forcefully pull a sharp pebble from his infant son's mouth even if it draws blood.

8. Establishing Standard Exemplars (Tulā) for Posterity:

Through the Etadagga Vagga (Chapter on the Foremost) and specific Sutta discourses, the Buddha established true standards (tulā) for future generations ("posterior folk"):

Monks: Ven. Sāriputta and Ven. Mahāmoggallāna

Nuns: Ven. Khemā and Ven. Uppalavaṇṇā

Laymen: Citta the Householder and Hatthaka of Āḷavī

Laywomen: Khujjuttarā and Nandamātā of Veḷukaṇṭaka

9. Skillful Pedagogical Strategies to Highlight Pupil Capability:

The Buddha deliberately created opportunities for his disciples to shine and teach—such as resting while Ven. Ānanda preached the Sekha Paṭipadā, leaving brief Dhamma statements for Ven. Mahākaccāna to elaborate, and publicly praising Ven. Visākha Pañcāliputta’s eloquent preaching—thereby ensuring the endurance of the Dispensation.

10. The Real Flag of the Sages (Isinaṁ Dhajo):

Quoting the verse Bhāsaye jotaye dhammaṁ..., the sermon highlights that the true "flag" or emblem of the Sages (Isi) is not a physical banner, but the illumination and preaching of the True Dhamma (subhāsitadhajā isayo, dhammo hi isinaṁ dhajo).

11. The Extreme Peril of Gain, Honor, and Renown (Lābha, Sakkāra, Siloka):

Praise and honor are described as dreadful (daruṇo), bitter (kaṭuko), and harsh (pharuso), often proving more dangerous than blame. The Buddha compares them to Māra's baited hook for fish and the destructive Verambha wind in the high sky that tears a bird apart.

12. Transcending the Eight Worldly Conditions (Aṭṭha Lokadhamma):

Gain/loss (lābho/alābho), fame/disrepute (yaso/ayaso), blame/praise (nindā/pasaṁsā), and pleasure/pain (sukha/dukkha) turn continuously like a chariot wheel and are entirely impermanent (anicca). The ultimate goal of practicing virtue, concentration, and wisdom (sīla, samādhi, paññā) is to extract practical Dhamma values from these conditions while remaining unattached, attaining liberation from the cycle of rebirth (saṁsāra) into the supreme deathless Nibbāna.
