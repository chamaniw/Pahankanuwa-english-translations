Sermon 140 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #140 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #140

Primary Theme: Safeguarding the Buddha Sāsana through internal spiritual cultivation (attano va avekkheyya), systematic self-reflection (paccavekkhaṇā), and non-reactive mind training, rather than external conflict or misdirected zeal.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: A devoted laywoman in Sāvatthī and Pāṭika, an Ājīvaka (non-Buddhist) ascetic whom she had supported and treated like her own son.

The Interaction: Inspiring reports from neighbors who had heard the Buddha led the laywoman to seek an opportunity to hear the Dhamma. When Pāṭika repeatedly forbade her from visiting the monastery, she secretly dispatched her son to invite the Buddha to their home for an alms-offering. Despite Pāṭika's attempts to sabotage the visit by tricking the boy into giving wrong directions, the Buddha arrived, accepted the alms, and began delivering the sermon of appreciation (anumōdanā).

The Disaster / Turning Point: Overcome by jealousy and rage upon hearing the laywoman express her devotion with cries of "Sādhu," Pāṭika burst out of an inner room where he was hiding, hurled vulgar insults at both the laywoman and the Buddha, and stormed off. The laywoman’s mind became deeply agitated (vikkhitta), preventing her from focusing on the Buddha's sermon.

The Result / Resolution: Observing her mental distress, the Buddha gently intervened and expounded Dhammapada Verse 50. He instructed her to ignore the misconduct and abusive words of others and focus entirely on her own wholesome deeds. Re-establishing her concentration (samādhi) and generating spiritual joy (pīti), the laywoman—along with other listeners—attained the Fruit of Stream-entry (Sotāpatti-phala).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Internal vs. External Protection of the Sāsana

The Sāsana is fundamentally a spiritual heritage passed "from heart to heart." It cannot be defended through external shouting, conflict, or misguided interference. Ven. Nanananda illustrates this with the fable of the donkey who tried to do the dog's job of barking at thieves; the donkey merely received a beating from its master while the thieves escaped. Misdirected actions under the guise of protecting the Dispensation only cause its internal collapse. True protection consists in strengthening internal virtue (sīla), concentration (samādhi), and wisdom (paññā).

2. Self-Observation over Fault-Finding (Na paresaṃ vilomāni)

Citing Dhammapada 50 and Dhammapada 252 (Sudassaṃ vajjaṃ aññesaṃ...), the sermon highlights the unwholesome human tendency to winnow the faults of others like chaff while concealing one's own like a cheating gambler. The Dhamma directs the practitioner to ignore the wrongdoings or negligence of others (na paresaṃ katākataṃ) and exclusively examine one’s own actions (attanova katāni akatāni ca).

3. Systematic Reflection via the "Mental Mirror" (Paccavekkhaṇā)

Drawing from the Ambalaṭṭhikā-Rāhulovāda Sutta, the Thero explains the mental capacity for reflection (paccavekkhaṇā) as an internal mirror. Practitioners must inspect all bodily (kāya-kamma), vocal (vacī-kamma), and mental actions (mano-kamma) across three distinct phases:

Before acting: Consider whether the intended action leads to self-harm, harm to others, or harm to both. If unwholesome, refrain completely.

During action: Continuously monitor the mind; if harm is detected mid-way, halt the action immediately.

After completion: If an unwholesome act was committed out of delusion, confess it to fellow practitioners (sabrahmacārī) and resolve upon future restraint (āyati-saṃvara). If wholesome, dwell in joy and spiritual gladness.

4. The Parable of the Flickering Lamp at the Lamp-Post (Pahan Kaṇuva)

Human existence in the dark night of saṃsāra is brief and unstable. Like tending a dying lamp amidst strong winds, practitioners must continuously feed the flame by pouring the oil of faith (saddhā), raising the wick of effort (viriya), and leaning on the light of a noble friend (kalyāṇa-mitta) to maintain internal clarity before life expires.

5. The Five Spheres of Liberation (Vimuttāyatana)

The sermon details the five formal occasions through which liberation and the realization of the Paths and Fruits (magga-phala) occur:

Listening to the Dhamma with dedicated attention.

Preaching the Dhamma to others while experiencing its meaning.

Reciting the Dhamma aloud to maintain alertness and clarity.

Mentally examining and contemplating the teachings.

Directing a well-developed theme of concentration (samādhi-nimitta) toward insight wisdom (paññā).

6. Experiential Comprehension (Attha-paṭisaṃvedī & Dhamma-paṭisaṃvedī)

Realization does not come from intellectual study for exams or degrees, but from experiencing both the meaning (attha) and the truth of the Dhamma (dhamma) with the intent of letting go of acquisitions (upadhi-paṭinissagga). This experiential engagement triggers a natural spiritual progression: Gladness (pāmojja) → Joy (pīti) → Tranquility (passaddhi) → Bliss (sukha) → Concentration (samādhi) → Wisdom (paññā).

7. Meditation as a "Spiritual Workshop" (Kammaṭṭhāna)

Defining kammaṭṭhāna (literally, "workplace/place of action") as an internal spiritual workshop, the Venerable Thero stresses that meditation requires reverent attention (cittīkāra). Practitioners must systematically abandon the five mental hindrances (nīvaraṇa)—sensual desire, ill-will, sloth/torpor, restlessness/worry, and doubt—to settle the mind and penetrate the Three Characteristics (ti-lakkhaṇa: anicca, dukkha, anattā).

8. Urgency of the Opportune Moment (Khaṇa-sampatti)

Human birth during a Buddha's dispensation is extraordinarily rare. Citing the Lōväḍa Saňgarāva and ancient Sanskrit verses, the Thero compares human life to an archer trying to shoot a single horsehair in pitch darkness using only the fleeting flash of a lightning strike. Missing this brief opportunity (khaṇa-sampatti) results in missing Nibbāna for uncounted eons.

9. The Noble Concept of Effacement (Sallēkha)

Drawing from the Sallekha Sutta, Ven. Nanananda contrasts the non-Buddhist concept of effacement (which focuses on extreme physical asceticism or nakedness) with the Buddha's refined definition. The Buddha clarified that deep meditative absorptions (jhāna) are merely "pleasant dwellings in this life" (diṭṭhadhamma-sukha-vihāra). True sallēkha (scrapers/effacement of defilements) is moral distinction and non-reactivity: When others are harmful, we shall be harmless (avihiṃsakā); when others destroy life, steal, or deceive, we shall refrain.

10. The Universal Role of Noble Friendship (Kalyāṇa-mittatā)

The Sāsana endures through the transmission of spiritual light between individuals. Reminding the listeners of the Buddha's declaration that the holy life (brahmacariya) is entirely comprised of noble friendship (kalyāṇa-mittatā), the sermon calls upon laypeople and monastics alike to strengthen their internal practice so they can act as guiding lights for others.
