Sermon 110 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #110 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #110

Primary Theme: Redefining true elderhood (thera) through internal spiritual purity rather than age or physical appearance, and establishing the Dhamma itself as the sole valid yardstick (dhammappamāṇo) for spiritual evaluation and practice.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Venerable Lakuṇṭaka Bhaddiya (a dwarfed, physically deformed Arahant monk), thirty visiting forest-dwelling bhikkhus, and the Supreme Buddha.

The Interaction: Thirty forest monks arrive at the Jetavana monastery in Sāvatthī to pay homage to the Buddha. On their way in, they pass Ven. Lakuṇṭaka Bhaddiya, who is leaving the Buddha's presence. When the Buddha asks if they saw the Elder who just departed, they reply that they only saw a young novice (sāmaṇera).

The Disaster / Turning Point: The forest monks misjudge Ven. Lakuṇṭaka Bhaddiya's spiritual standing due to his short stature and deformed body—a physical retribution (akusala saṅkhāra) resulting from a past life during Kassapa Buddha's era when he advised building a smaller relic stupa. Young novice monks frequently ridiculed him, physically pulling his nose and ears. This highlights the world's tragic tendency to measure spiritual worth by external appearance.

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha corrects the monks, revealing that Ven. Lakuṇṭaka Bhaddiya is a fully liberated Arahant of immense psychic power and majesty who has mastered all meditative attainments (samāpatti). The Buddha utters Dhammapada verses 260–261 and 81, declaring that gray hair or physical age alone makes one "decayed in vain" (moghajiṇṇo), whereas true elderhood is founded solely upon the realization of the Four Noble Truths, non-violence, virtue, and total eradication of mental defilements.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Definition of True Elderhood (Thera vs. Moghajiṇṇo):

Chronological age or graying hair does not constitute senior elderhood; a person who merely grows old without spiritual growth is termed "decayed in vain" (moghajiṇṇo). A true Elder (thera) is defined by five key spiritual qualities: realization of Truth (sacca), embodiment of Dhamma (dhammo), non-violence (ahiṁsā), restraint (saṁyamo), and self-control (damo), having completely discarded defilements (vantamalo).

2. Equanimity in the Face of Praise and Blame (Upekkhā):

Despite constant teasing and physical mistreatment by unthinking novices, Ven. Lakuṇṭaka Bhaddiya maintained unshakeable mental equanimity. The Buddha praised this using the simile of a single, solid mass of rock (selo yathā ekaghano) that remains unmoved by fierce windstorms, illustrating that the truly wise (paṇḍitā) are never perturbed by worldliness, blame, or praise (nindāpasaṁsāsu).

3. The Four Yardsticks of Judgment (Pamāṇa):

The Buddha categorizes four ways people evaluate others (Pamāṇa Sutta):

Rūpappamāṇo rūpappasanno: Judging and taking pleasure in physical appearance.

Ghosappamāṇo ghosappasanno: Judging and taking pleasure in fame, praise, and reputation.

Lūkhappamāṇo lūkhappasanno: Judging and taking pleasure in harsh, austere external practices (lūkha-paṭipadā).

Dhammappamāṇo dhammappasanno: Judging and taking pleasure in the Dhamma alone.

(The commentary notes that the vast majority of worldlings fall into the first three flawed categories, whereas only one in a hundred thousand utilizes the Dhamma as their yardstick).

4. Correcting Misconceptions of Austerity (Lūkha-paṭipadā):

In conversation with the wanderer Mahāsakuludāyī, the Buddha clarifies that disciples do not revere him for external ascetic habits (such as eating little, wearing coarse rag-robes, or dwelling in rough lodgings), as individual disciples sometimes kept stricter ascetic vows (dhutaṅga). Rather, they revere him for spiritual, Dhamma-based qualities: Noble Virtue (ariya-sīla), Knowledge and Vision (ñāṇa-dassana), incomparable Wisdom (paññā), and his mastery in teaching the Four Noble Truths.

5. Seeing the Dhamma vs. Clinging to the Form (Yo Dhammaṁ Passati So Maṁ Passati):

Through the narrative of Ven. Vakkali—who was obsessed with the Buddha’s physical beauty—the Buddha dismantles attachment to external form by asking, "What is the use to you of seeing this putrid body (pūtikāya)?" He establishes the ultimate criterion: "He who sees the Dhamma sees me, and he who sees me sees the Dhamma."

6. The Foolish Elder (Bālo Thero) vs. The Wise Elder (Paṇḍito Thero):

In response to brahmins who complained that the Buddha did not bow to elderly householders, the Buddha outlined two types of elders:

Bālo Thero: A person old in years who speaks out of season (akālavādī), falsely (abhūtavādī), unprofitably (anatthavādī), contrary to Dhamma and Vinaya, and without reason or purpose.

Paṇḍito Thero: A person who may be young in age with black hair (daharo), but speaks at the right time (kālavādī), truthfully (bhūtavādī), beneficially (atthavādī), in accordance with Dhamma and Vinaya, offering treasure-like words with clear causes and logic (sāpadesaṁ).

7. The Four Qualities Making an Elder (Therakaraṇa-Dhamma):

The Buddha formalizes four specific criteria for true elderhood:

Flawless virtue (sīlavanto).

Deep learning and penetration of the Dhamma (bahussuto), retained in mind, verbally recited, mentally pondered (manasānupekkhitā), and thoroughly penetrated by right view (diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā).

Mastery of the four fine-material absorptions/Jhanas (samādhi-balam / diṭṭhadhamma-sukhavihāra).

Eradication of all mental taints, attaining Arahantship.

8. Urgency for Mindfulness of the Body (Kāyagatāsati):

Ven. Lakuṇṭaka Bhaddiya expressed in the Theragāthā that if he could ask for one boon from the Buddha on behalf of the world, it would be that all beings constantly practice mindfulness of the body (kāyagatāsati). Realizing the impermanent, decaying nature of the flesh—which swells in youth, wrinkles in old age, and ultimately leaves only a skeleton—dispels worldlings' deluded judgments based on bodily appearances.

9. The Miracle of Instruction (Anusāsanī-Pāṭihāriya):

Rejecting calls (such as Kevaṭṭha's) to win over the world through psychic miracles (iddhi-pāṭihāriya) or mind-reading (ādesanā-pāṭihāriya), the Buddha asserts that the highest miracle is the Anusāsanī-pāṭihāriya (Miracle of Instruction). This involves precisely guiding the disciple’s mind: "Think thus, do not think thus (evaṁ vitakkehi, mā evaṁ vitakkayittha); abandon these things, enter and dwell in these things."

10. The Conditional Sequence of Spiritual Progress (Paṭicca-Samuppāda of Practice):

Spiritual growth occurs not through force or blind belief, but through a causal sequence:

Faith (saddhā) → Approaching a teacher (upasaṅkamati) → Attending on him (payirupāsati) → Hearing the Dhamma (suṇāti) → Retaining it (dhāreti) → Examining the meaning (upaparikkhati) → Agreement through reflection (nijjhānakkhamanti) → Arising of desire/enthusiasm (chanda) → Making initial effort (ussaha) → Balancing the spiritual faculties (tuleti) → Applying supreme, unrelenting energy (padahati) → Direct personal realization of Truth.

11. The Supremacy of Reverence for the Dhamma (Dhamma-Gāravatā):

Following his Enlightenment, the Buddha searched for a teacher to revere, but finding no superior being, he resolved to live honoring and respecting the Dhamma itself (dhammaṁ yeva sakkaronto dhammaṁ garukaronto). The Dhamma-element (dhammadhātu), dependent arising (paṭicca-samuppāda), and specific conditionality (idappaccayatā) are eternal truths that exist whether Tathāgatas arise or not; the Buddhas merely discover, penetrate, analyze, and make these truths plain. Therefore, respect for the Dhamma (dhamma-gāravatā) must always supersede personal or external veneration.
