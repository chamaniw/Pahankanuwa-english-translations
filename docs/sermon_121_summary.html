Sermon 121 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #121 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #121 (Pahankaṇuwa Sinhala Audio Dhamma Sermon No. 34)

Primary Theme: The deep relationship between karma and its result (vipāka), the avoidance of unwholesome actions that cause regret (anutappa), and how life's sudden conflicts and groundless accusations can be transformed through wise attention (yoniso manasikāra) and equanimity towards preparations (saṅkhārupekkhā-ñāṇa) into the realization of Stream-entry (sotāpatti-phala).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: An ordinary, poor farmer plowing his field near Jetavana monastery; the Buddha; and Venerable Ānanda.

The Interaction: After thieves dropped a stolen purse of gold coins (kahāpaṇa) by the edge of the field, the Buddha surveyed the world with Great Compassion (mahā-karuṇā-samāpatti). Seeing that the farmer possessed the supportive conditions (upanissaya-sampatti) for Stream-entry, the Buddha went there with Ven. Ānanda. Standing near the purse, the Buddha asked twice, "Do you see the serpent, Ānanda?" to which Ven. Ānanda replied, "Yes, Venerable Sir, the deadly, poisonous serpent." Hearing this, the farmer later approached the spot, found the purse, understood the Buddha called it a "serpent," but due to narrow worldly perspective, covered it with soil and resumed plowing while dreaming of wealth.

The Disaster / Turning Point: The owners of the stolen property tracked footprints to the field, falsely accused the innocent farmer, and took him before the King. The King sentenced him to death. As executioners whipped him along the road to his execution, instead of generating anger (dosa) or wailing, the farmer continuously recited the Buddha's dialogue like a mantra: "Do you see the serpent, Ānanda? Yes, Lord, the deadly, poisonous serpent."

The Result / Resolution: Surprised by his words, the guards brought him back before the King. The farmer cited the Buddha as his witness. The King took him to Jetavana to verify the story. The Buddha confirmed the incident and delivered Dhammapada verse 67 (Na taṃ kammaṃ kataṃ sādhu, yaṃ katvā anutappati...). Upon hearing the sermon, the farmer penetrated the Dhamma and attained the fruit of Stream-entry (sotāpatti-phala).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Superficial Perspective vs. Far-Reaching Wisdom on Karma (Karma-Vipāka):

Ordinary worldly people evaluate actions based on immediate, superficial, short-term pleasant results. Lacking deep foresight, they establish conventional notions of good and bad that lead them to commit unwholesome actions (akusala-karma) and suffer in the lower realms (apāya). Only a Buddha clearly sees the true link between volitional force (cētanā-śakti) deposited in consciousness (viññāṇa) and its far-reaching maturation (karma-vipāka).

2. The Metaphor of Wealth as a Deadly Serpent (Sarpa):

Mundane treasures, when picked up with a defiled or dishonest mind, act like a concealed, poisonous serpent. Although an object of wealth initially causes a person to dream pleasant dreams, its ultimate "bite" produces intense unsatisfactoriness and suffering (dukkha).

3. Groundless Accusations as Karmic Ripening (Vipāka-Vāra):

The Buddha understands what is a ground (ṭhāna) and what is not a ground (aṭṭhāna). Groundless accusations and unjust life crises are frequently the ripening turn (vipāka-vāra) of past unwholesome karma. Struggling against these results with greed (lobha), hatred (dosa), or delusion (moha) merely entangles one further in saṃsāra.

4. Transforming Life Shocks via Wise Attention (Yoniso Manasikāra):

Instead of reacting with anger (dosa) or unwise attention (ayoniso manasikāra) during severe life conflicts, one must apply wise attention (yoniso manasikāra). Life’s shocks contain the dormant raw materials for insight; contemplating them correctly allows hidden insight-knowledges (vipassanā-ñāṇa) to unfold.

5. Sequential Progression of Insight-Knowledges (Vipassanā-Ñāṇa):

The path to Stream-entry involves a mental transformation through specific insight stages:

Knowledge of arising and passing away (udayabbaya-ñāṇa)

Knowledge of dissolution (bhaṅga-ñāṇa)

Knowledge of the appearance of terror (bhayatupaṭṭhāna-ñāṇa) regarding impermanent formations

Knowledge of disadvantage (ādīnava-ñāṇa) and disillusionment

Desire for deliverance (muñcitukamyatā-ñāṇa)

Knowledge of reflective contemplation (paṭisaṅkhā-ñāṇa)

Equanimity towards preparations/formations (saṅkhārupekkhā-ñāṇa)

6. Equanimity Towards Formations (Saṅkhārupekkhā-Ñāṇa) as "Untangling the Rope":

When karmic results manifest, struggling adds more knots to the thread of existence (bhava). Equanimity (upekkhā) involves looking on mindfully as karmic forces break up and dissolve—much like allowing a twisted rope to untangle naturally without grasping (upādāna) or resisting.

7. Integration of Serenity (Samatha) and Recollection of the Buddha (Buddhānussati):

While being whipped, the farmer calmed his mind by recollecting the Buddha's omniscient quality (lokavidū). This combined serenity meditation (samatha-bhāvanā) with situational wisdom (sthānocita-prajñā), preventing hatred from arising and suppressing mental agitation.

8. Characteristics of the Fool (Bāla) vs. The Wise (Paṇḍita):

According to the Bālapandita Sutta, age or academic learning does not define wisdom. A fool (bāla) acts, speaks, or thinks through defiled roots (lobha, dosa, moha), leading to regret (anutappa). A wise person (paṇḍita) operates through non-greed (alobha), non-hatred (adosa), and non-delusion (amoha).

9. The Four Fears (Bhaya) and the Simile of the Mountain Shadow:

Sinful acts bring four severe fears: self-reproach (attānuvāda-bhaya), others' reproach (parānuvāda-bhaya), punishment (daṇḍa-bhaya), and evil destinations (duggati-bhaya). The Buddha compared the remorse of a guilty mind to the shadow of a great mountain in the evening—it hangs continuously over the fool's body wherever he sits or lies down, tormenting him in this life before yielding extreme torment in hell (apāya).

10. The Core Purpose of the Tathāgata's Appearance:

As highlighted by the ancient verse (Na hi sīlavataṃ hetu...), Buddhas do not appear merely to teach conventional moral vows (sīla), but to reveal the three universal characteristics—impermanence (anicca), unsatisfactoriness (dukkha), and non-self (anattā)—which lead to complete disillusionment with saṃsāra.

11. Guarding the Three Doors via Moral Precepts (Ājīva-Aṭṭhamaka-Sīla):

Unwholesome deeds enter through the three doors of action, speech, and thought. Practicing moral conduct with livelihood as the eighth (ājīva-aṭṭhamaka-sīla) guards these doors against regret, providing the blameless happiness (anavajja-sukha) required for deep reflection.

12. The Supramundane Mastery of the Stream-Enterer (Sotāpanna):

A Stream-enterer is described as one who has directly seen, reached, and understood the Dhamma (diṭṭhadhammo, pattadhammo, viditadhammo), crossed over doubt (tiṇṇavicikiccho), and attained unwavering courage/confidence (vesārajjappatto) in the Buddha's dispensation, realizing Nibbāna right within the experiences of daily life.
