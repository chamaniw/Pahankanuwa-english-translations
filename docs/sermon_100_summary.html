Sermon 100 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #100 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #100

Primary Theme: The criteria, pedagogical principles, and spiritual responsibilities involved in preaching, listening to, and preserving the True Dhamma (Saddhamma), highlighted through the exemplary discourse of Venerable Visākha Pañcālaputta and the Buddha's instructions.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Venerable Visākha Pañcālaputta (son of a regional governor from Magadha who ordained, attained Arahantship with higher spiritual powers (abhiññā), and became a master expounder of the Dhamma).

The Interaction: While the Buddha was residing at the Gabled Hall (Kūṭāgāra Sālā) in the Great Forest (Mahāvana) near Vesālī, Venerable Visākha Pañcālaputta delivered a Dhamma instruction to the Sangha assembled in the hall, characterized by exceptional clarity, eloquence, and freedom from worldly attachment.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Upon emerging from evening seclusion, the Buddha inquired about the speaker who inspired the Sangha with such pure vocabulary and noble delivery. Recognizing Venerable Visākha's exemplary sermon, the Buddha issued a call to action (Itivuttaka 105), challenging all disciples to hoist and uphold the true "banner of the seers" (isinaṃ dhajaṃ)—which is the Dhamma itself.

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha praised Venerable Visākha ("Sādhu, sādhu, Visākha"), establishing a definitive standard for how the Dhamma should be expounded, honored, preserved, and internalized by both preacher and listener across generations.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Rhetorical and Linguistic Excellence in Dhamma Preaching

A complete Dhamma discourse must exhibit four functional delivery characteristics: sandasseti (demonstration via clear similes/illustrations), samādapeti (thorough convincing/instigation), samuttejeti (encouraging effort/inspiring energy), and sampahaṁseti (gladdening/bringing spiritual joy).

It must also be endowed with five linguistic standards: poriyā vācā (polite, refined, refined-townsfolk language), vissaṭṭhā (clear, smooth pronunciation), anelagalā (faultless, unhindered flow), atthassa viññāpanīya (conveying explicit meaning clearly), and anissitā (unattached to becoming/bhava).

2. Ethical Standard of the Dhamma Expounder (Theragāthā of Visākha)

A authentic preacher avoids self-exaltation (na ukkhipe), does not belittle or criticize others (no pi pare / na okkhipe), does not disparage Arahants who have crossed the ocean of existence (pāragataṁ na eraye), refrains from self-praise in assemblies (na cattavaṇṇaṁ parisāsu byāhare), remains un-excited/un-haughty (anuddhato), speaks with measured proportion (sammitabhāṇi), and maintains pure conduct (subbato).

3. Supremacy of Respect for the Dhamma (Dhamma-Garavatā)

Respect for the Dhamma transcends respect for individuals. Shortly after awakening under the Ajapāla Banyan tree, the Buddha reflected that living without a teacher is painful (dukkha), yet seeing no superior in virtue (sīla), concentration (samādadhi), or wisdom (paññā), he resolved to live honoring and respecting the Dhamma he awakened to—a universal principle confirmed by Sahampati Brahmā (esā buddhāna dhammatā).

4. Four Causes for the Disappearance of the True Dhamma (Saddhamma-Antaradhāna)

The Buddha warned of four factors leading to the decline of the Dispensation:

1. Monks learning the Dhamma incorrectly with corrupted phrasing (vyañjana), leading to distorted meaning (attha).

2. Monks becoming undisciplined, disobedient, and unreceptive to instruction.

3. Keepers of the Dhamma (dhammadhara) and Discipline (vinayadhara) failing to pass down the suttas to disciples.

4. Elder monks leaning toward luxury, laxity, and losing delight in Nibbāna and seclusion.

5. Simile of the Āṇaka Drum and Future Dangers (Anāgata-Bhaya)

Like the ancient Dasāraha drum (Āṇaka) whose original frame completely vanished after being repeatedly repaired with temporary wooden pegs, future monks will ignore profound discourses dealing with supramundane emptiness (suññatā). Instead, they will favor ornate, secular, poetic compositions by outsiders, causing the true core of the Dhamma to be lost.

6. Five Prerequisites for Preaching the Dhamma (Instruction regarding Ven. Udāyī)

Correcting the popular but lax monk Udāyī, the Buddha laid down five internal resolves required before preaching to others:

1. Preaching in gradual order (ānupubbikathaṁ kathessāmīti).

2. Preaching with reasons and illustrative examples (pariyāyadassāvī).

3. Preaching out of compassion and kindness (anuddayataṁ paṭicca).

4. Preaching without seeking material gain or requisites (na āmisantaro).

5. Preaching without harming oneself (self-praise) or others (denigration) (attānañca parañca anupahacca).

7. Structure of the Gradual Discourse (Anupubbikathā)

The classic sequence of instruction moves systematically: talk on giving (dāna-kathā), virtue (sīla-kathā), heavenly realms (sagga-kathā), the danger, degradation, and defilement of sensual pleasures (kāmānaṁ ādīnavaṁ okāraṁ saṅkilesaṁ), and the benefits of renunciation (nekkhamma-ānisasaṁ). When the listener's mind becomes prepared, softened, hindrance-free, gladdened, and confident (kalla-, mudu-, vinīvaraṇa-, udagga-, pasanna-citta), the Buddha reveals the instruction unique to the Buddhas (sāmukkaṁsikā dhammadesanā): the Four Noble Truths.

8. Impartiality in Preaching: The Lion's Roar Analogy

Just as a hunting lion leaps upon its prey—whether an elephant, buffalo, leopard, or hare—with equal precision and maximum effort so as not to miss its target, the Tathāgata preaches the Dhamma with identical thoroughness and care to monks, nuns, kings, or destitute beggars. This is because Buddhas are respecters of the Dhamma (dhamma-garu), not respecters of material gain (āmisa-garu).

9. Prioritization of Spiritual Care: Similes of the Three Fields and Three Water Vessels

In response to Asibandhakaputta Gāmaṇi’s accusation of bias, the Buddha used two similes:

Three Fields: Fertile field = Monks/Nuns; Medium field = Laymen/Laywomen; Infertile clay soil = Recluses of other sects (aññatitthiya).

Three Vessels: Non-leaking vessel = Monks/Nuns; Slightly leaking vessel = Lay followers; Vessel with holes = Other sects.

Primary effort is directed to those taking full refuge, yet the Dhamma is still preached to outsiders in hopes that even a single sentence brings them long-term welfare.

10. The Four Methods of Answering Questions (Vyākaraṇa)

A skilled preacher must apply the Buddha's fourfold analytical methodology:

1. Ekaṁsa-vyākaraṇīya: Questions to be answered categorically (direct yes/no).

2. Paṭipucchā-vyākaraṇīya: Questions to be answered by counter-questioning.

3. Vibhajja-vyākaraṇīya: Questions requiring analytical breakdown.

4. Ṭhapanīya: Questions based on false premises that must be set aside.

11. Experiencing Meaning-Taste (Attha-Rasa) and Dhamma-Taste (Dhamma-Rasa)

Preaching and listening can involve three dynamics where either the preacher, listener, or both experience the meaning (attha) and experiential impact (dhamma) of the teaching. True listening requires an active attitude: atthikaṭvā (seeking benefit), manasikaritvā (attentively reflecting), sabba-cetaso samannāharitvā (engaging the whole mind), and ohitasoto (listening attentively).

Illustrated by Ven. Khemaka, who explained to sixty monks that despite surrendering self-identity in the five aggregates, a subtle conceit "I am" (asmi-māna) remained (compared to the lingering scent of a flower or residual soap smell on a clean cloth). During this discourse, both the listeners and Ven. Khemaka attained Arahantship.

12. The Fivefold Quality of Great Learning (Bahu-ssuta) vs. Technological Preservation

True preservation of the Dhamma does not occur via electronic machines, books, or golden letters, but through personal internalization described in five steps: bahu-ssutā (heard much), dhatā (retained/memorized), vacasā paricitā (familiarized by verbal recitation), manasānupekkhitā (examined mentally), and diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā (thoroughly penetrated by right view). Technology must be used without becoming enslaved by it or reducing the sacred Dhamma to mere academic data.
