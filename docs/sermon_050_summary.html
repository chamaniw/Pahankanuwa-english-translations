Sermon 050 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #50 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #50

Primary Theme: The comprehensive exposition of the influxes (āsavas), their origin, and their total destruction (āsavakkhaya) through Yoniso Manasikāra (wise/radical attention). The sermon details the seven methods of abandoning influxes from the Sabbāsava Sutta and demonstrates how navigating the saṁsāric flood (ogha) via the Middle Path (majjhimā paṭipadā) leads to the unshakeable island (dīpa) of Nibbāna.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: A visiting deity (devatā) inquiring of the Buddha (drawn from the Oghataraṇa Sutta, the opening discourse of the Saṁyutta Nikāya), set alongside the archetypal figure of the uninstructed worldling (assutavā puthujjana).

The Interaction: The deity approaches the Blessed One during the night and asks: "How, dear sir (mārisa), did you cross over the flood?" referring to the four treacherous saṁsāric floods (caturogha).

The Disaster / Turning Point: The existential crisis of the uninstructed worldling, who, driven by improper reflection (ayoniso manasikāra), projects an enduring self (ātman) across past, present, and future ("Was I?", "Am I?", "What will I be?"). This plunges the worldling into a "thicket of views" (diṭṭhigahana) and a wilderness of views, leaving them perpetually submerged in the current of the influxes. This dilemma is mirrored in the Buddha's pre-enlightenment trial between two extreme currents: sensual indulgence (kāmasukhallikānuyoga) where one sinks, and extreme ascetic self-mortification (attakilamathānuyoga) where one is swept away.

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha answers the deity with a riddle-like verse: "Without halting and without straining, I crossed over the flood" (appatiṭṭhaṁ anāyūhaṁ). Halting leads to sinking; straining leads to being swept away. By discovering the Middle Path (majjhimā paṭipadā) and using radical attention (yoniso manasikāra) to systematically dismantle the influxes, the noble disciple cuts across the flood and lands upon the unshakeable island (dīpa) of Arahantship (arahattaphala-vimutti).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Etymology and Dual Nature of Influxes (Āsava):

The term āsava originates from āsavantīti āsavā ("flowing in" or "oozing out"). Ven. Nanananda highlights two essential nuances:

Intoxication/Stagnation: Like alcoholic spirits that ferment and stagnate, long-term stagnation in sensuality (kāmāsava), existence (bhavāsava), and ignorance (avijjāsava) produces an intoxicating potency.

Flowing Current: It possesses an active, infectious power to flow into nearby mental states and transform them into an intoxicating state.

2. The Sixteenth Definition of Right View (Sammā-diṭṭhi):

In the Sammā-diṭṭhi Sutta, Ven. Sāriputta presents sixteen progressive definitions of Right View, culminating in the complete understanding of the influxes: knowing the āsavas, their origin (avijjāsamudayā āsavasamudayo), their cessation (avijjānirodhā āsavanirodho), and the Noble Eightfold Path leading to their cessation.

3. Ignorance (Avijjā) as Cause and Effect:

A profound doctrinal dilemma is resolved: Ignorance is listed both as an influx (avijjāsava) and as the cause of all influxes (avijjāsamudayā). The Ven. Thero explains that long-term stagnation in darkness continuously generates the tendency for influxes to drag on, which can only be broken by replacing ayoniso manasikāra with yoniso manasikāra.

4. Wise Attention (Yoniso Manasikāra) as the Primary Seed of Wisdom:

While full wisdom (paññā) belongs to Arahants, ordinary worldlings possess attention (manasikāra). Yoniso manasikāra is the "primary seed of wisdom" that breaks the backbone of ignorance. Ven. Nanananda uses the simile of a court trial: the Buddha caught attention (manasikāra)—which was previously the "chief thief" driving saṁsāra as ayoniso—pardoned it, and turned it into the primary "state witness" (yoniso) to reveal the truth of the Noble Truths.

5. The Wilderness of Self-Views (Diṭṭhigahana):

Unwise reflection puts the concept of "I" at the forefront of past, present, and future thinking. This locks the worldling into six self-views (e.g., "A self exists for me", "No self exists for me", "With self I perceive non-self"). The Buddha characterizes this state through vivid terms: a thicket of views (diṭṭhigataṁ), a wilderness of views (diṭṭhigahanaṁ), a desert of views (diṭṭhikantāraṁ), and a contortion of views (diṭṭhivisūkaṁ).

6. The Sevenfold Methods of Restraint and Abandoning Influxes (Sabbāsava Sutta):

The sermon systematically breaks down the seven methods for eradicating āsavas:

Dassanā-pahātabbā (Abandoned by Vision): Eradicating the first three fetters (sakkāya-diṭṭhi, vicikicchā, sīlabbata-parāmāsa) exclusively through viewing reality via the Four Noble Truths.

Saṁvarā-pahātabbā (Abandoned by Restraint): Guarding the six sense doors (guttadvāratā) to eliminate weariness and distress (vighāta-pariḷāha).

Paṭisevanā-pahātabbā (Abandoned by Using): Exercising "restraint through knowledge" (ñāṇa-saṁvara) by reflecting on the minimal utility (paṭisaṅkhā yoniso) of the four requisites (robe, food, lodging, medicine).

Adhivāsanā-pahātabbā (Abandoned by Tolerating): Enduring physical discomforts (heat, cold, insects) and harsh, ill-spoken words (duruttānaṁ durāgatānaṁ vacanapathānaṁ).

Parivajjanā-pahātabbā (Abandoned by Avoiding): Steering clear of physical dangers (wild animals, pits) and unwholesome companions/places (agocara).

Vinodanā-pahātabbā (Abandoned by Removing): Dispelling evil unwholesome thoughts (kāmavitakka, byāpādavitakka, vihiṁsāvitakka).

Bhāvanā-pahātabbā (Abandoned by Developing): Cultivating the seven Enlightenment Factors (bojjhaṅgas) based on seclusion (viveka-nissitaṁ), dispassion (virāga-nissitaṁ), and cessation (nirodha-nissitaṁ).

7. Simile for the 5-Stage Process of Dispelling Thoughts (Vinodana):

The sermon explains the sequence of words used for dispelling unwholesome thoughts—nādhivāseti, pajahati, vinodeti, byantīkaroti, anabhāvaṁ gameti—through the simile of an unruly drunkard entering a house:

Nādhivāseti: Refusing to tolerate or welcome him.

Pajahati: Telling him to leave.

Vinodeti: Pushing him out toward the yard.

Byantīkaroti: Escorting him all the way outside the front gate.

Anabhāvaṁ gameti: Locking the gate so he can never re-enter.

8. Crossing the Flood (Ogha) via the Dynamic Middle Path:

Connecting the Sabbāsava Sutta to the Oghataraṇa Sutta, the Ven. Thero explains that when crossing a turbulent river, one cannot walk in a rigid straight line. Halting (appatiṭṭhaṁ) causes one to sink into sensual pleasure; straining (anāyūhaṁ) causes one to be swept away by extreme austerities. Crossing requires yielding to the water's force while cutting across it—embodying the practical, relative application of the Middle Path.

9. Erasure of Linguistic Rubbish (Vacīkhara) and Conceptual Proliferation:

Quoting the Sundarika Bhāradvāja Sutta, the Thero highlights that an Arahant's influx of existence (bhavāsava) and vacīkhara are completely destroyed. Vacīkhara is defined not merely as harsh speech, but as "linguistic rubbish"—the conceptual proliferation (papañca) inherent in worldly language. Arahants use worldly conventions (loka-vohāra) without misapprehending them (aparāmasaṁ).

10. Nibbāna as the Unshakeable Island (Dīpa) and Objectless Mind (Anārammaṇa Citta):

Liberation is described as reaching an island (dīpa) untroubled by the saṁsāric flood. The Arahant's mind dwells in the pasture (gocara) of the Void (suññatā), the Signless (animitta), and the Undirected (appaṇihita). Free from sense-objects, their mind is imperturbable (āneñjappattaṁ) and objectless (anārammaṁ cittaṁ), leaving a track that cannot be traced—just like the path of birds flying through the sky (ākāseva sakuntānaṁ, padaṁ tassa durantayaṁ).
