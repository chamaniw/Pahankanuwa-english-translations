Pahankanuwa Sermon #81 Summary

Sermon #81 Summary

1. Overview

- Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #81

- Primary Theme: Piercing the darkness of ignorance (avijjā) and the intoxications of negligence (pamāda) by confronting the eleven fires of saṃsāric suffering (dukkha), cultivating spiritual urgency (saṃvega), and developing insight knowledges (vipassanā-ñāṇa) that lead to disenchantment (nibbidā) and final liberation.

---

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

- The Protagonist: Visākhā, the chief female lay disciple (mahā-upāsikā), and five hundred householders' wives in Sāvatthī who had been entrusted to her care as Dhamma pupils.

- The Interaction: During a seven-day city drinking festival, the five hundred women developed a taste for leftover alcohol. Under the guise of taking a walk in the park with Visākhā, they secretly consumed the liquor and became intoxicated. Visākhā admonished them, and upon returning home feigning illness, their husbands discovered the truth and severely punished them.

- The Disaster / Turning Point: On a subsequent festival, the women secretly smuggled alcohol in flower vessels while accompanying Visākhā to perform offerings to the Buddha at the monastery. Having drunk the alcohol beforehand, they entered the Buddha’s presence heavily intoxicated. Influenced by their mindlessness and possessed by deities of Māra's retinue (māra-kāyika devatā), they began singing, dancing, clapping, and laughing inappropriately right before the Blessed One as he began to preach.

- The Result / Resolution: The Buddha emitted a dense darkness from his eyebrows, invoking the imminent fear of death (santrāsa), which instantly dissipated their intoxication. He then exhorted them with Dhammapada verse 146 (Ko nu hāso kimānando...). Stirred by deep spiritual urgency (saṃvega), all five hundred laywomen attained the fruit of Stream-entry (sotāpatti-phala).

---

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Darkness of Ignorance (*Avijjā*) vs. the Light of Wisdom (*Paññā*)

Beings are caught in an endless heap of dukkha because their wisdom eye is blinded by the darkness of ignorance (avijjā-andhakāra). Realization of the truth—the dawning of Nibbāna—is described as the arising of the Dhamma-eye (dhamma-cakkhu) or the lighting of a lamp (ñāṇa-padīpa). Worldly beings remain trapped because negligence (pamāda) keeps them from realizing their own spiritual blindness.

2. The Eleven Fires (*Ekādasa-ggi*) of Saṃsāric Existence

The Buddha describes existence as continuously burning with eleven fires: the three unwholesome root defilements—lust (rāga), hatred (dosa), and delusion (moha)—along with eight manifestations of physical and mental suffering: birth (jāti), aging (jarā), death (maraṇa), sorrow (soka), lamentation (parideva), physical suffering (dukkha), grief (domanassa), and despair (upāyāsa).

3. Identity of the Physical Body with Suffering (*Dukkha*)

Drawing upon the verse from Lō Väḍa Saňgarāva, Ven. Nanananda highlights that just as a pile of wood continuously ablaze can no longer be called "firewood" but simply "fire," this body cannot truly be called a "body"—it is pure dukkha (dukkha-sacca). Existence and dukkha fit together perfectly like two closing shutter doors (dukkhe loko patiṭṭhito).

4. The Illusory Nature of Health and Youth

The worldly notion of being "healthy" is a foolish delusion. The bodily cage made of the four great elements (mahābhūta) begins decaying from the moment of birth. Hunger and thirst are constant fires that must be fed continually, while sickness (vyādhi) and aging (jarā) represent the ongoing derangement of the elemental humors (vā, pit, sem).

5. The Intoxications (*Mada*) and Grounds for Negligence (*Pamādaṭṭhāna*)

Mindfulness is destroyed by various forms of intoxication (mada): alcohol addiction (surā-meraya), youth (yobbana-mada), health (ārogya-mada), life (jīvita-mada), and wealth (bhoga-mada). Worldly beings use entertainment and alcohol as superficial "plasters" to hide suffering rather than curing the root cause.

6. The Noble Discipline’s Perspective on Worldly Entertainment

Ven. Nanananda points to radical declarations from the Suttas regarding worldly amusements:

- In the Noble Discipline (ariyassa vinaye), singing is considered a form of crying/wailing (rodataṃ).

- Dancing is considered a form of madness (ummattakaṃ).

- Excessive laughing showing one's teeth is considered childish (komārakaṃ).

Worldly "sweet songs" are often built upon subterranean sorrow and loss (piya-vippayoga), deriving gratification (assāda) from recalled saṃsāric pain.

7. The Illusion of Permanence (*Nicca-Saññā*) and Language Constructs

Existence (pavattana) literally means a continuous "rolling-over" or changing (vipariṇāma). Worldly language and conventions (sammuti) are built upon the false perception of permanence (nicca-saññā), creating concepts of "I" and "mine." The Buddha hesitated to teach because human language itself is constructed out of delusional views of selfhood.

8. The Tug-of-War with Māra and the Strategy of Letting Go (*Pahāna*)

Craving for existence (bhava-taṇhā) leads Māra to grant beings a "bodily cage." However, Māra only bestows this cage to drag it back through decay, illness, and death—rejoicing in the resulting torment (santāpa). The only way to win this tug-of-war is not to pull back, but to completely let go (pahāna) of the body and craving.

9. The Progression of Insight Knowledges (*Vipassanā-Ñāṇa*)

True liberation requires cultivating insight through direct contemplation of the three marks (tilakkhaṇa):

- Udayabbaya-ñāṇa: Seeing the rapid arising and passing away of phenomena.

- Bhaṅga-ñāṇa: Observing continuous dissolution and destruction.

- Ādīnava-ñāṇa: Recognizing the inherent danger and defectiveness of all aggregates.

- Nibbidā-ñāṇa: Developing profound disenchantment/weariness with saṃsāra, which acts as the "iron door" separating saṃsāra from the deathless element (amata-dhātu).

10. Cultivation of the Five Faculties (*Indriya*) and Powers (*Bala*)

Attentive listening to the Dhamma with a concentrated mind is not a passive act. It systematically cultivates the five spiritual faculties and powers: faith (saddhā), energy (viriya), mindfulness (sati), concentration (samādhi), and wisdom (paññā), transforming conceptual knowledge into experiential realization.
