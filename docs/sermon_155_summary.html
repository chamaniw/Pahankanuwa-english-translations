Sermon 155 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #155 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #155 (Audio Sermon No. 114)

Primary Theme: The Invulnerability and Power of a Developed Mind (Bhāvita-Citta), the Deconstruction of Feeling (Vedanā), and Liberation through Insight into the Six Sense-Bases (Saḷāyatana).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Venerable Sāriputta, Venerable Mahā Moggallāna, and two yakkhas (demons).

The Interaction: While residing at Kapota Kandara (Pigeon Glen) near Rājagaha, Venerable Sāriputta, having just shaved his head and beard, sat in deep meditative concentration under the open moonlight. Two yakkhas flying from north to south spotted him. One yakkha expressed a desire to strike the meditating elder.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Despite three explicit warnings from his companion about Venerable Sāriputta’s great spiritual and psychic powers, the foolish yakkha delivered a devastating blow directly to Venerable Sāriputta's head—a blow powerful enough to fell an elephant or split a mountain peak.

The Result / Resolution: Resting in the concentration of the fruition of Arahantship (arahatta-phala-samādhi), Venerable Sāriputta was completely unharmed, later remarking only that his head hurt "just a little." Ven. Mahā Moggallāna, using his divine eye (dibba-cakkhu), witnessed the strike and praised Sāriputta's immense strength, while Sāriputta humbly praised Moggallāna's divine vision. The aggressive yakkha instantly crashed into the Great Hell (Mahā Niraya), screaming "I am burning!" Upon overhearing the two chief disciples' mutual praise, the Buddha uttered a solemn verse (Udāna 4.4) on the unshakable, rock-like mind.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Unshakable Rock-Like Mind (Selūpamaṃ Cittaṃ):

A mind thoroughly cultured through meditation (bhāvita-citta) stands firm like a solid rock. It remains unperturbed by the eight worldly conditions (aṭṭha-loka-dhamma), free from attraction towards enticing objects (rajanīyesu virattaṃ) and free from irritation towards provoking conditions (kopaneyye na kuppati).

2. The Danger of Insulting Noble Ones (Āryopavāda) and Law of Kamma (Kamma-niyāma):

Striking or insulting an Arahant constitutes a severe unwholesome act (akusala). The malicious volition (cetanā) itself acts like striking one's own head against a mountain peak; it instantly produces severe fruition (kamma-vipāka), causing the offender to fall into hell. This natural order (dhamma-niyāma) applies even when harming inanimate representations of noble ideals, such as Buddha statues.

3. Protection through Supramundane Concentration (Arahatta-Phala-Samādhi):

Venerable Sāriputta’s invulnerability stemmed from dwelling in the fruition of Arahantship, a state where the mind is stilled in Nibbāna and completely detached from the sensory world. At such high levels of concentration—similar to the attainment of cessation (nirodha-samāpatti)—the mind and physical body are rendered immune to external physical harm.

4. True Bodily vs. Mental Development (Kāya-bhāvanā & Citta-bhāvanā):

Rebuffing Saccaka the Nigantha's view of physical mortification, the Buddha defined bodily development (kāya-bhāvanā) in the Noble Discipline (ariya-vinaya) not as physical exercise or ascetic self-torment, but as sense-restraint (indriya-saṁvara), preventing pleasant feelings (vedanā) from giving rise to lust (rāga). Mental development (citta-bhāvanā) prevents painful feelings from giving rise to aversion (paṭigha) or mental distress.

5. The Simile of the Two Arrows (Salla Sutta):

When an untaught worldling (puthujjana) experiences physical pain, they weep and lament, thereby striking themselves with a "second arrow" of mental pain. The noble disciple (ariya-sāvaka) experiences only the first arrow (physical feeling) but refrains from generating mental grief, bringing the mind directly to equanimity (upekkha).

6. Physical Pain as the True Abyss (Pātāla):

The true bottomless pit (pātāla) mentioned in the world is physical painful feeling. Untaught worldlings, fearing this abyss, frantically chase after sensual pleasure (kāma-sukha) to cover up pain. The noble disciple, however, escapes pain not through sensory indulgence, but by anchoring the mind in peaceful neutrality (upasama).

7. Equanimity (Upekkha) as the Foundation for Bliss and Realization:

The bliss of equanimity (upekkha-sukha), realized fully in the fourth absorption (jhāna) and perfected in Arahantship, provides supreme mental stability. The Middle Path (majjhimā paṭipadā) utilizes neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling (adukkham-asukha-vedanā) as a secure, unshakable basis for developing insight.

8. Specific Meditation Countermeasures (Kammaṭṭhāna) for Mental Defilements:

Meditation on Foulness (Asubha-bhāvanā) / Perception of Repulsiveness (Paṭikūla-saññā): Subdues extreme lust (rāga) and taste-craving (rasa-taṇhā).

Attention to the Elements (Dhātu-manasikāra): Analyzing internal and external forms as earth, water, fire, and wind (mahā-bhūta) neutralizes attachment and anger.

Perception of Impermanence (Anicca-saññā): Cuts down grasping (grahana) by dismantling the sign (nimitta) of permanence.

9. The Three Similes of the Nandakovāda Sutta:

The Oil Lamp: Just as light depends on an impermanent flame, wick, and oil, feeling (vedanā) depends on impermanent conditions and cannot be regarded as a permanent self (attā).

The Tree's Shadow: Just as a shadow depends on impermanent roots, trunk, and leaves, secondary mental states are impermanent.

The Butcher and the Cow Hide: The internal flesh represents the 6 internal bases (ajjhattika-āyatana); the outer hide represents the 6 external objects (bāhira-āyatana); the connecting tendons represent delight and lust (nandī-rāga). The butcher’s knife represents noble wisdom (ariya-paññā), which cuts the bond of craving connecting sense-organs to sense-objects.

10. Correction of Distortions (Vipallāsa) via Wise Attention (Yoniso Manasikāra):

Worldlings suffer due to perverted views (viparīta-diṭṭhi)—mistaking the impermanent (anicca) for permanent (nicca), the foul (asubha) for beautiful (subha), the painful (dukkha) for pleasant (sukha), and non-self (anattā) for self (attā). Continuous moment-to-moment noting through wise attention serves as the primary tool to loosen the five aggregates of grasping (pañc'upādānakkhandha).

11. Phenomenological Definition of "The World" (Loko):

The Buddha defined the world as arising entirely within the six sense-bases (chasu loko samuppanno). The world and suffering (dukkha) are identical and interdependent like two panels of a folding door. True "globalization" occurs internally; liberating oneself from the world requires relinquishing attachment to contact (phassa) across the six sense-fields.

12. The Mindful Posture for Hearing the Dhamma:

True spiritual transformation requires hearing Dhamma with proper application (atthikaṃ katvā manasikatvā sabba-cetasā ohita-soto): recognizing its practical utility for Nibbāna, reflecting on it word-by-word, and listening with undivided attention. This reflective listening (bhāvanā-manasikāra) enabled the five hundred bhikkhunīs in the Nandakovāda Sutta to attain full spiritual fruits (magga-phala) upon hearing the exact same sermon repeated.
