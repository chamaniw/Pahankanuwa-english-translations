Sermon 087 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #87 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #87 (Audio Sermon No. 36)

Primary Theme: The Mental Environment vs. Physical Environment: How a land becomes truly delightful (rāmaṇeyyaka) through the presence and supramundane concentration of Arahants, and the urgency of escaping the cycle of rebirth (saṁsāra) through right view (sammā-diṭṭhi) and self-establishment (attasammā-paṇidhi).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Seven-year-old Prince Revata (the youngest brother of Ven. Sāriputta) and his Brahmin mother.

The Interaction: Fearing her lineage and family wealth would be lost after all her other children were ordained by Ven. Sāriputta, Revata’s mother arranged a child marriage for seven-year-old Revata. Ven. Sāriputta, anticipating this, instructed the Sangha to ordain Revata immediately without seeking parental permission if he ever approached them.

The Disaster / Turning Point: At the wedding, relatives blessed the young bride to grow old like her 120-year-old grandmother. Upon seeing the grandmother—wrinkled, gray-haired, bent like a roof rafter, and completely weakened by decay (jarā)—Revata realized his young bride would also decay. Grasping the inherent danger (ādīnava) of existence, he resolved to escape that very day. Pretending to have stomach trouble during the chariot ride home, he slipped away into the forest.

The Result / Resolution: Revata fled to a forest monastery, was ordained, and retreated into an Acacia forest (Khadiravana). Within three months, he attained Arahantship with the four analytical knowledges (paṭisambhidā) and psychic powers (iddhi). When the Buddha later visited the thorny forest—where two skeptical monks mistook Revata's psychic construction of lodgings for excessive vanity—the Buddha uttered Dhammapada verse 98, declaring that wherever Arahants dwell, even a thorny wilderness becomes supremely delightful (rāmaṇeyyaka).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Mental Environment vs. Physical Environment (Patirūpadesavāsa)

While worldly science and environmental engineering focus exclusively on physical geography, vegetation, and architecture, the Buddha introduced the concept of the "mental atmosphere." Dwelling in a suitable locality (patirūpadesavāsa) is fundamentally measured by an environment that fosters righteous thoughts, noble association, and spiritual health.

2. The Supramundane Energy of Arahantship (Arahatta-phala-samādhi)

A land becomes delightful not because of physical luxury, but because of the spiritual energy imparted by Arahants entering the concentration of the fruition of Arahantship (arahatta-phala-samādhi). This peace and tranquility imbues sacred lands (suddha-bhūmi) with an attractive power that can persist for centuries or even across an interval between Buddhas (buddhantara).

3. The Three Lessons of Saṁsāra: Gratification, Danger, and Escape

Existence comprises three levels of understanding: gratification (assāda—sensual pleasure), danger (ādīnava—the inherent flaws of birth, aging, sickness, and death), and escape (nissaraṇa—liberation from the cycle). Seven-year-old Revata demonstrated mature wisdom by immediately discerning the danger (ādīnava) in aging (jarā) and making a swift escape (nissaraṇa).

4. Right Establishment of Self (Attasammā-paṇidhi)

A core blessing (maṅgala) in life is directing and establishing one's mind rightly. Without internal self-control through virtue (sīla), concentration (samādhi), and wisdom (paññā), one is easily swept away by emotional impulses, wrong views, and the societal forces of decline into unwholesome states.

5. Activating Past Merits (Pubbe ca kata-puññatā)

Every being possesses a mix of past merits and demerits. Like blowing on sparks under ashes, one must exert energetic effort (vīriya), faith (saddhā), and virtue (sīla) to bring hidden past merits to the surface. Succumbing to greed or immoral economic practices brings past demerits to the surface instead, leading to rebirth in lower realms.

6. Primacy of the Mind (Manopubbaṅgamā Dhammā)

Contrary to material science which treats matter as primary, the Dhamma establishes that mind precedes all phenomena (manopubbaṅgamā dhammā). The physical world acts according to mental directives, and the future "cage" or body one inherits (whether human, deity, hungry ghost, or demon) is constructed entirely by the state of one's mind at death.

7. Rectification of Views (Diṭṭhijukamma) and Right View (Sammā-diṭṭhi)

Ordinary Buddhists often possess crooked views influenced by toxic societal misconceptions. Straightening one's view (diṭṭhijukamma) is a gradual process that is only fully finalized upon reaching Stream-entry (sotāpatti), when the Four Noble Truths are directly penetrated in a supramundane sense.

8. Virtue (Sīla) as a Form of Noble Sacrifice

Just as giving (dāna) involves relinquishing physical possessions, maintaining moral conduct (sīla) requires sacrificing immediate sensual gratification, selfish gains, or easy wealth derived from wrong livelihood (e.g., slaughter, theft, trade in intoxicants or weapons) in order to protect one's long-term spiritual trajectory.

9. The Practical Application of Meditation (Kammaṭṭhāna)

Daily meditation is a necessary battle against mental dispersion and pollution. Practices such as recollection of the Buddha (Buddhānussati), loving-kindness (mettā), contemplation of foulness (asubha-bhāvanā), and mindfulness of death (maraṇasati) settle the mind's turbidity like a water-clearing gem (udaka-prasādaka-māṇikya) and loosen long-standing attachments to worldly bonds.

10. The Urgency of Escape (Nissaraṇa) at the End of an Era

Referring to the societal and spiritual decline at the turn of the century (1999), Ven. Nanananda Thero emphasizes that before the lamp of the Dispensation (sāsana) goes out, it emits a bright light. Practitioners must exercise diligent effort (appamāda) to escape the forces of decline—even if "by ninety-nine"—and prioritize liberation from saṁsāra above short-term worldly goals.
