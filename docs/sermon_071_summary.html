Pahankanuwa Sermon #71 Summary

Sermon #71 Summary

1. Overview

- Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #71

- Primary Theme: Spiritual warfare against inner defilements (Māra-saṅgrāma), self-conquest (ātma-vijaya), the systematic progression of the Thirty-Seven Requisites of Enlightenment (bodhipakkhiyā dhammā), and refuting modern delusions of instant realization by honoring the Threefold Training (tividha-sikkhā).

---

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

- The Protagonist: Bhaddā Kuṇḍalakesā, the sixteen-year-old daughter of a wealthy merchant (seṭṭhi) in Rājagaha, who later became a Jain wanderer (Jambū Paribbājikā) and eventually an Arahant Bhikkhuṇī.

- The Interaction: Seeing a condemned thief being led to execution, she fell deeply in love due to past saṃsāric conditions. Her father bribed the chief executioner with 1,000 Kahāpaṇas to release the thief and gave her to him in marriage. She served him with deep affection and devotion while wearing valuable ornaments.

- The Disaster / Turning Point: Driven by his thievish nature, the husband plotted to kill her for her jewelry. Pretending to fulfill a vow to a deity, he led her to Thieves’ Cliff (Chorapapāta Pabbata). At the cliff edge, he demanded her ornaments and announced his intention to throw her to her death. Utilizing sharp presence of mind, Bhaddā feigned compliance and asked for a final boon: to circumambulate, embrace, and pay respects to her husband one last time. As she circumambulated him, she grabbed him firmly from behind and pushed him over the precipice to his death.

- The Result / Resolution: Afraid to return home, she entered a Jain (Nigaṇṭha) female ascetic hermitage, had her hair plucked out (earning the name Kuṇḍalakesā or "Curly-hair"), and mastered 1,000 systems of debate. She traveled India challenging debaters until she met Venerable Sāriputta in Sāvatthī. After Venerable Sāriputta easily answered all 1,000 of her questions, he stumped her with a single Buddha-question: "Ekaṃ nāma kiṃ?" ("What is one?"). Recognizing her defeat, she took ordination as a Bhikkhuṇī, heard the Dhamma from the Buddha to attain Stream-entry (Sotāpanna), and soon attained Arahantship while contemplating foot-washing water flowing away. She was subsequently declared foremost among nuns in swift intuition (khippābhiññā).

---

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Spiritual Warfare (*Māra-Saṅgrāma*) and Self-Conquest (*Ātma-Vijaya*)

The Buddha appropriated martial language ("fight," "battle," "warfare") to appeal to the energetic nature of humanity. While worldly conquest (dig-vijaya) involves slaughter, bad karma (akusala), and ultimate subjugation to death, conquering oneself (ātma-vijaya) is the supreme victory that subdues internal defilements (kilesa-dhamma) and overcomes Death (Maccu).

2. The Delusion of "Instant Enlightenment" in Modern Times

Modern society often attempts to superimpose technological concepts of "instant gratification" onto spiritual life. True swift intuition (khippābhiññā)—as seen in Bhaddā Kuṇḍalakesā or Bāhiya Dārucīriya—is the result of immense pāramīs accumulated over long cycles of Saṃsāra. Reading short, profound Suttas repeatedly without realizing the Dhamma proves that claiming khippābhiññā status today is generally a form of delusion (moha).

3. The Four Modes of Practice (*Paṭipadā*)

The path of practice is categorized based on character and faculties:

- Painful progress with slow intuition (dukkhā paṭipadā dandhābhiññā)

- Painful progress with swift intuition (dukkhā paṭipadā khippābhiññā)

- Pleasant progress with slow intuition (sukhā paṭipadā dandhābhiññā)

- Pleasant progress with swift intuition (sukhā paṭipadā khippābhiññā)

Dukkhā paṭipadā involves intense contemplation of foulness (asubhānupassī), repulsiveness of food (āhāre paṭikūlasaññā), and death (maraṇasaññā), whereas sukhā paṭipadā utilizes the mastery of jhānic absorptions (jhāna) to easily suppress hindrances (nīvaraṇa).

4. The Four Types of Spiritual Individuals (*Puggala*)

- Ugghaṭitaññū: One who comprehends the Dhamma upon a brief indication or hint.

- Vipañcitaññū: One who realizes the Dhamma after a detailed discourse.

- Neyya: One who requires step-by-step guidance, frequent questioning, radical attention (yoniso manasikāra), and association with noble friends (kalyāṇa-mitta).

- Padaparama: One for whom words are the maximum limit; despite learning and memorization, they cannot attain Path and Fruits in this lifetime, though their efforts build vital support for future lives.

5. The Conceit of Skipping the Basics

Some intellectually conceited individuals view virtue (sīla) and basic faith (saddhā) as mere teachings for "foolish laypersons," attempting to rely exclusively on wisdom (paññā). The Buddha warns that abandoning foundational practices leads to inevitable regret. All practitioners must humbly begin with the Threefold Training (tividha-sikkhā).

6. Defilements as the Real Army of Māra

The Buddha personified internal, unwholesome mental states—such as sensual desires, craving (taṇhā), discontent, doubt (vicikicchā), and sluggishness—as the "Army of Māra." Personifying these forces creates immediate vigilance, prompting the practitioner (yogāvacara) to shatter the army of Death as an elephant crushes a reed hut.

7. The Buddha's Army: The Thirty-Seven Requisites of Enlightenment (*Sattis-Bodhipakkhiya-Dhamma*)

As summarized in the Mahāparinibbāna Sutta, the Buddha’s true spiritual army consists of the 37 Requisites of Enlightenment: the 4 Foundations of Mindfulness, 4 Right Efforts, 4 Bases of Power, 5 Spiritual Faculties, 5 Powers, 7 Enlightenment Factors, and the Noble Eightfold Path.

8. The Four Bases of Power (*Iddhipāda*) as Spiritual Battle Strategies

Achieving success over defilements requires strategic avenues (iddhipāda):

- Zeal/Desire (chanda)

- Energy (viriya)

- Mind/Determination (citta)

- Investigative Wisdom (vīmaṃsā)

Mastering even one of these bases according to one's temperament can lead to ultimate spiritual victory.

9. The Noble Eightfold Path (*Ariyo Aṭṭhaṅgiko Maggo*) as an Integrated Unit

Unlike other groupings that are listed as individual components (e.g., cattāro satipaṭṭhānā), the Noble Eightfold Path is described as a single, organically integrated unit (ariyo aṭṭhaṅgiko maggo). When supramundane path-concentration (magga-samādhi) arises, all eight path factors emerge simultaneously in a single mind-moment to penetrate the Unconditioned (Asaṅkhata).

10. Proper Contextual Understanding of "Ekāyano Maggo"

The phrase ekāyano maggo in the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta does not mean the "only path" in opposition to the Noble Eightfold Path. Rather, ekāyana means an "unbranching path" that proceeds with absolute certainty and merges directly into the Noble Eightfold Path (Ñāya).

11. Primacy of the Threefold Training (*Sīla, Samādhi, Paññā*)

Citing the teachings of Bhikkhuṇī Dhammadinnā and the Buddha's final illness discourses, Ven. Nanananda highlights that the Noble Eightfold Path is subsumed under the Threefold Training, not vice-versa. Virtue (sīla) enriches concentration (samādhi), concentration enriches wisdom (paññā), and wisdom fully liberates the mind from all taints (āsava).
