Sermon 069 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #69 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #69

Primary Theme: The apaṇṇaka practice (the non-erring, safe path)—contrasting wise, empirical investigation with foolish reliance on superficial logic or deceptive appearances, extended from basic moral decision-making to the complete transcendence of all states of becoming (bhava) and the realization of Nibbāna in this very life (diṭṭheva dhamme parinibbāna).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist:

Present context: The Buddha, the householder Anāthapiṇḍika, and five hundred of his friends who were former sectarian followers (tīrthaka).

Past context (Jātaka story): The Bodhisatta (a wise caravan leader / sātthu-nāyaka) and a foolish merchant (the past birth of Devadatta).

The Interaction:

Present context: After initially taking refuge in the Triple Gem upon hearing the Dhamma, the 500 friends relapsed into their old sectarian refuges when the Buddha traveled from Sāvatthi to Rājagaha. Upon the Buddha's return 7–8 months later, Anāthapiṇḍika brought them back to the Buddha, who used the Apaṇṇaka Jātaka to demonstrate the danger of taking non-refuges as refuges.

Past context: Both merchants prepared 500 carts each to cross a dangerous, waterless desert (nirudaka) infested by non-humans (yakshas). The Bodhisatta allowed the foolish merchant to go first after evaluating the options: the foolish merchant saw five superficial advantages to going first (unmuddy roads, fresh grass, edible leaves, clean water, fixing prices), while the Bodhisatta saw five wise advantages to going second (leveled roads, tender young grass, pre-dug wells, avoiding haggling over prices).

The Disaster / Turning Point:

The Disaster: Out in the middle of the desert, a demon (yaksha) disguised as a high lord dripping with water, wearing lotus flowers, and claiming heavy rains lay ahead convinced the foolish merchant to dump his caravan's water pots. Left exhausted without water, the foolish merchant and his men were devoured by demons during the night.

The Turning Point: The Bodhisatta, traveling a month and a half later, met the same demon. Recognizing him as a non-human due to his red eyes and absence of a shadow, the Bodhisatta refused to throw away his water supply ("without seeing other water, we will not throw away what we have"). He questioned his men to verify that there were no empirical signs of rain (no cold rain-wind, thunder, or lightning) and urged his caravan forward based on sound reasoning.

The Result / Resolution:

The Bodhisatta and his caravan discovered the untouched carts and piles of bones of the foolish merchant's group. Taking strong security measures, they safely navigated the desert, exchanged their damaged carts for superior ones, reached their destination, sold their goods at a high profit, and returned home safely. The 500 relapsed followers were brought back to the true path by hearing this sermon.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Definition of Apaṇṇaka Practice versus Logical Speculation (Takkikā):

The central verse (Apaṇṇakaṃ ṭhānameke, dutiyaṃ āhu takkikā...) distinguishes between wise people (medhāvī) who follow the safe, non-erring path (apaṇṇaka) and those who become slaves to mere logic (takkikā). The foolish rely on superficial, plausible-sounding arguments, whereas the wise act on principles that safeguard them against ruin regardless of outcome.

2. Reasoned Faith (Ākāravatī Saddhā):

Referencing the Apaṇṇaka Sutta (Majjhima Nikāya), Ven. Nanananda points out the Buddha's advice to the Brahmins of Sālā: when one has not yet established reasoned faith (ākāravatī saddhā) through deep investigation of facts, one must adopt the apaṇṇaka practice as a reliable guideline for long-term welfare and happiness.

3. Analysis of Nihilism (Nātthika-diṭṭhi) vs. Ownership of Action (Kammassakatā Sammādiṭṭhi):

The apaṇṇaka method evaluates conflicting philosophical positions on pragmatic and moral grounds:

Nihilism (Nātthika-vāda): Leads to bad conduct (dussīla) because it rejects moral consequences. If there is no world beyond, they gain nothing special; if there is a world beyond, they fall into hell. Thus, they experience failure on both sides (ubhayatha kaliggaho).

Right View of Kamma (Kammassakatā Sammādiṭṭhi): Leads to good conduct (susīla). Even if there were no world beyond, they earn the praise of the wise in this life; if there is a world beyond, they secure a happy destination. Thus, they experience success on both sides (ubhayatha jayaggaho).

4. Progression from Moral Views to Transcendence of Bhava (Becoming):

The apaṇṇaka investigation moves beyond basic morality to evaluate the fine-material (rūpa) and immaterial (arūpa) realms. The wise recognize that all physical disputes, violence, and falsehood stem from form (rūpa). Therefore, even without fully experiencing the formless realms, a wise person rationally inclines toward abandoning form for the immaterial because it carries lesser danger.

5. Realization of Parinibbāna in This Very Life (Diṭṭheva Dhamme Parinibbāna):

Transcending both the fine-material and immaterial states of becoming (bhava), the ultimate apaṇṇaka choice is the total cessation of bhava (bhavanirodha). Ven. Nanananda highlights the sutta phrase "diṭṭheva dhamme parinibbāyati"—demonstrating that Nibbāna is not a post-mortem state of existence in some future realm, but the absolute calming of defilements, exhaustion of craving (taṇhakkhaya), and relinquishment of all assets (upadhi) in this present life.

6. The Structural Formula of Dependent Arising (Upādāna-paccayā Bhavo):

The sermon clarifies the deep link within the Four Noble Truths: the five aggregates of grasping (pañcupādānakkhandha) constitute Suffering (dukkha), which exists solely due to Grasping (upādāna), rooted in Craving (taṇhā). Bhava (becoming) is generated per individual through upādāna. Eradicating taṇhā extinguishes upādāna, which immediately causes the cessation of bhava (Nibbāna).

7. The Simile of the Apaṇṇako Maṇi (The Cubic Gem):

Drawing from the Aṅguttara Nikāya commentary, Ven. Nanananda explains the etymology and imagery of apaṇṇaka through the apaṇṇako maṇi—a four-sided or cubic gem. Whichever way it is thrown up into the air, it always lands completely flat and secure on one of its sides. Similarly, standard bad actions inevitably land a person in states of deprivation, while standard wholesome actions unfailingly land a person in a happy destination.

8. Avoidance of Dogmatic Attachment (Idameva Saccaṃ Moghamaññaṃ):

The apaṇṇaka approach avoids dogmatic declarations such as "this alone is truth, all else is foolishness" (idameva saccaṃ moghamaññaṃ). Instead of blindly asserting claims beyond one's personal knowledge, the wise person weighs dangers against benefits and chooses the practice that guarantees spiritual safety and progress.

9. Proper Contextual Re-interpretation of the Kālāma Sutta:

Ven. Nanananda clarifies a common modern misunderstanding of the Kālāma Sutta. The Buddha's instruction to set aside the ten criteria (such as tradition, hearsay, logic, inference, or authority) is not a license to reject Dhamma principles arbitrarily. Rather, it demands that one bring the Dhamma directly into one's own lived experience—observing internally whether greed (lobha), hatred (dosa), and delusion (moha) lead to harm or benefit.

10. The Experiential Qualities of the Dhamma (Sandiṭṭhika & Paccattaṃ Veditabboti Viññūhi):

The apaṇṇaka practice reflects the inherent qualities of the Dhamma: it is directly visible (sandiṭṭhika), timeless (akālika), inviting one to "come and see" (ehipassiko), and to be personally experienced by the wise (paccattaṃ veditabbo viññūhi). Practical spiritual progress relies on direct internal confirmation rather than abstract, bookish intellectualism.
