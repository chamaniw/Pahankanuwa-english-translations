Sermon 186 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #186 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #186 (Paṭicca Samuppāda Sermon 4: The Law of Dependent Arising)

Primary Theme: Disentangling the internal and external "tangle" (jaṭā) through understanding the radical, mutual dependence (aññamañña paccaya) between Name-and-Form (nāmarūpa) and Consciousness (viññāṇa), correcting commentarial misconceptions of nāma and the fallacy of a monolithic, transmigrating consciousness.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: A devatā (deity) asking a riddle in the Jaṭā Sutta; Ven. Ānanda (in the Mahā Nidāna Sutta); Ven. Mahā Koṭṭhita and Ven. Sāriputta (in the Naḷakaḷāpa Sutta); and the misguided monk Sāti (in the Mahā Taṇhāsaṅkhaya Sutta).

The Interaction:

The deity asks the Buddha who can disentangle the tangle inside and outside (antojaṭā bahijaṭā).

Ven. Ānanda tells the Buddha that despite Paṭicca Samuppāda being deep, it appears "as clear as clear" to him.

Ven. Mahā Koṭṭhita questions Ven. Sāriputta on how decay and death arise, tracing the chain back to the circular relationship between nāmarūpa and viññāṇa.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Worldlings and certain monastics (such as Sāti) fall into personality-view (sakkāyadiṭṭhi), treating consciousness as an enduring soul that jumps from life to life like a leech. Furthermore, post-canonical commentaries (e.g., Buddhaghosa’s Visuddhimagga) obscure the original sutta definition of nāma, misinterpreting the inner and outer tangles as mere external/internal requisites or sense-spheres.

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha refutes Sāti and instructs Ven. Ānanda on the true depth of Dependent Arising. Ven. Sāriputta presents the famous simile of the two sheaves of reeds (naḷakaḷāpa), showing that when the leaning support between nāmarūpa and viññāṇa is severed via non-manifestative, unestablished consciousness (appatiṭṭhita-viññāṇa), the whole tangle is snapped and the entire mass of suffering ceases.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Re-definition of the Tangle Within and Without (Antojaṭā & Bahijaṭā)

Ven. Nanananda contrasts Ven. Buddhaghosa’s commentarial interpretation (which defines the tangles as craving for internal/external requisites or sense-spheres) with the direct sutta exposition: antojaṭā (the inner tangle) is the structural intertwining of Name (nāma) and Form (rūpa), while bahijaṭā (the outer tangle) is the mutual dependency between nāmarūpa and Consciousness (viññāṇa).

2. Strict Sutta Definition of Name (Nāma) vs. Commentarial Misconception

Contrary to the commentarial tradition which equates nāma with all four immaterial aggregates (including consciousness), the suttas strictly define nāma as five specific factors: feeling (vedanā), perception (saññā), intention (cetanā), contact (phassa), and attention (manasikāra). Consciousness (viññāṇa) is not a component of nāma, but stands in a dynamic, reciprocal relationship with it.

3. Verbal and Resistance Impressions (Adhivacana-samphassa & Paṭigha-samphassa)

Form (rūpa) cannot be identified on its own; it receives a verbal impression (adhivacana-samphassa) through the naming factors (nāma). Conversely, the immaterial naming group experiences a resistance impression (paṭigha-samphassa) through the physical impact offered by form.

4. The Psychological Nature of Contact (Phassa)

True contact (phassa) is unique to the animate world where consciousness is established. Physical impact between inanimate objects (such as two stones colliding or trees rubbing together) is merely attributed contact; it lacks phassa because it lacks feeling, perception, and intention.

5. The Vortical Relationship of Nāmarūpa and *Viññāṇa*

Existence is not a linear progression, but a dynamic vortex: viññāṇapaccayā nāmarūpaṁ, nāmarūpapaccayā viññāṇaṁ. Consciousness gravitates towards nāmarūpa like a stretched rubber band. If consciousness fails to descend or gain a footing (patiṭṭhā) in a mother's womb, nāmarūpa cannot develop or mature into a new existence.

6. Established Consciousness vs. Unestablished Consciousness (Patiṭṭhita-viññāṇa vs. Appatiṭṭhita-viññāṇa)

The consciousness of a worldling is "established" (patiṭṭhita) on nāmarūpa, fueling renewed existence (punabbhava). The Arahant's mind is "unestablished" (appatiṭṭhita), non-continuing (appavatta), objectless (anārammaṇa), and non-manifestative (anidassana-viññāṇa), leaving no foothold for future rebirth.

7. Role of Latent Tendencies in Rebirth (Anusaya and the Cetanā Sutta)

Even when active intention (ceteti) or mental construction (pakappeti) is absent (such as in sudden death), underlying latent tendencies (anusaya) suffice as an object (ārammaṇa) for the stationing and growth of consciousness, driving the continuity of Saṁsāra.

8. Refutation of the "Leech Simile" and Soul-Consciousness (The Mahā Taṇhāsaṅkhaya Sutta)

The Thero refutes the Vedāntic idea—erroneously held by the monk Sāti—that consciousness is an unchanging entity wandering through Saṁsāra like a leech moving from leaf to leaf. Consciousness is strictly dependently arisen (paṭiccasamuppanna); just as fire is named after its fuel (log-fire, grass-fire), consciousness is designated purely by its specific condition (eye-consciousness, mind-consciousness).

9. The Rapid Turning Round of Mind (Lahuparivattaṁ Cittaṁ)

The famous teaching that mind "turns round quickly" (lahuparivattaṁ) does not refer to a moving firebrand, but to the inconceivably rapid process of grasping and releasing nāmarūpa objects moment-by-moment, as illustrated by a monkey grabbing and letting go of forest branches.

10. The Simile of the Two Sheaves of Reeds (Naḷakaḷāpa-upamā)

Drawing from the Naḷakaḷāpa Sutta, Ven. Sāriputta illustrates that nāmarūpa and viññāṇa stand leaning against each other like two sheaves of reeds. If one sheaf is pulled away, the other collapses. Removing consciousness from nāmarūpa causes the six sense-spheres, contact, feeling, craving, and the entire mass of suffering (dukkhandha) to collapse completely.

11. Inherent Voidness and Pithlessness (Suññatā)

By selecting reeds (tacasāra—plants with bark but no solid heartwood), Ven. Sāriputta insinuated the self-less, hollow nature (suñña) of both consciousness and nāmarūpa. Neither aggregate contains a monolithic core or self (attā).

12. Pragmatic Realization of Nibbāna via *Paṭicca Samuppāda*

Ven. Mahā Koṭṭhita’s 36-fold eulogy highlights the practical purpose of the Dhamma: preaching, practicing, and achieving liberation through revulsion (nibbidā), fading away (virāga), and cessation (nirodha) of each link. Seeing this conditional process directly is equivalent to seeing the Dhamma and seeing the Buddha himself ("Yo paṭiccasamuppādaṁ passati so dhammaṁ passati").
