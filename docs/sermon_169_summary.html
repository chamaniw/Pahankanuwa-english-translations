Sermon 169 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #169 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #169

Primary Theme: The subtle nature of home-centered attachments, the necessity of eradicating underlying latent defilements (anusaya) beyond mere external renunciation, and the practical application of radical attention (yoniso manasikāra) and supreme sense-restraint (indriya-saṁvara) to attain Nibbāna in this very life.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: A group of elderly householders in Sāvatthi who ordained late in life, and Madhurapācikā ("the sweet-cook"), the former wife (purāṇa-dutiyikā) of one of these monks.

The Interaction: Because they ordained in their old age, these monks lacked the capacity for rigorous study and meditation (bhāvanā). They built huts behind the Jetavana monastery and, during their alms-rounds, regularly visited the house of Madhurapācikā. They assembled at her home daily to consume their meals because she prepared delicious, flavor-enhanced food for them.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Madhurapācikā suddenly fell ill and passed away. Deeply grieving her loss, the elderly monks gathered in the hut of her former husband, weeping, wailing, and clasping each other's necks, lamenting that they had lost the laywoman who provided them with delicious food.

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha brought this incident to the Dhamma assembly, relating the Kāka Jātaka (Crow Birth Story) to show how this same group had previously acted as a flock of crows foolishly trying to drink the ocean dry with their beaks when the female crow was swept away by a wave. The Buddha then uttered Dhammapada Verses 283 and 284, instructing the monks that true renunciation (nekkhamma) requires cutting down the entire forest (vana) and underbrush (vanatha) of internal defilements, rather than merely abandoning lay life externally.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. True Renunciation (Nekkhamma) vs. External Monasticism:

Abandoning the household life and putting on robes is not genuine renunciation if the mind remains bound by home-centered attachments, lay thoughts, and desires. One must cut down both the forest (vana) of major defilements and the underbrush (vanatha) of subtle desires to attain Nibbāna.

2. The Hazard of the Monastic Householder (Samaṇa-Kuṭumbika):

Monks who ordain in old age often fall into the obstacle of relatives (ñāti-palibodha). The commentaries term such a monk a "monastic householder" (samaṇa-kuṭumbika), comparing him to a tree on a riverbank whose roots remain anchored on land while its leaves touch the water. Engaging in lay affairs (gihi-saṁsagga) impedes spiritual progress.

3. The River Ganges Log Simile and the 8 Spiritual Hazards:

A log sweeping down the river will reach the ocean if it avoids eight hazards. The Buddha maps these to the spiritual path leading to Nibbāna:

1. This bank: Eye/internal bases (ajjhattika-āyatana).

2. Outer bank: Form/external bases (bāhira-āyatana).

3. Sinking in mid-stream: Delight and lust (nandīrāga).

4. Running aground: Conceit "I am" (asmi-māna).

5. Taken by human beings (manussaggāha): Entanglement in lay affairs.

6. Taken by non-human beings (amanussaggāha): Practicing holy life for a heavenly rebirth.

7. Whirlpool (āvaṭṭagāho): The fivefold sensual pleasures (pañca-kāma-sampatti).

8. Rotting from within (antopūti): Living an immoral, defiled life while pretending to be a monk.

Hearing this discourse, Nanda the cowherd immediately sought and later achieved Arahatship.

4. Three Levels of Mind Restraint (Ven. Piṇḍola Bhāradvāja's Discussion with King Udeni):

To help young monks maintain the holy life (brahmacariya), the Buddha provided three progressive instructions:

Perception of family: Arousing the perception of mother, sister, or daughter towards women based on their age.

Meditation on foulness (asubha-bhāvanā / paṭikūlasaññā): Contemplating the body's 32 repulsive parts.

Sense-restraint (indriya-saṁvara): Guarding the sense-doors (guttadvāra) without grasping signs (nimitta) or secondary details (anuvyañjana) to prevent the influx of taints (āsava). King Udeni validated this third point through his own experience in his harem and took refuge in the Triple Gem.

5. The Three Stages of Defilement:

Defilements operate across three distinct stages:

1. Anusaya: Underlying tendencies lying dormant beneath consciousness.

2. Pariyuṭṭhāna: Active arousal of defilements in the mind.

3. Vītikkama: Physical or verbal transgression (breaking precepts).

The central mind-faculty (manindriya) consumes all sensory data gathered by the five peripheral senses and holds these deep saṁsāric accumulations.

6. Supreme Faculty Development (Anuttarā Indriya-Bhāvanā) vs. Physical Restraint:

Rebutting the brahmin teacher Pārāsarī (via his student Uttara), the Buddha clarified that simply closing or blocking the physical eyes and ears is not true faculty development—otherwise, the blind and deaf would be fully developed. True development (anuttarā indriya-bhāvanā) consists of recognizing sensory contact as prepared (saṅkhata), coarse (oḷārika), and dependently arisen (paṭicca-samuppanna), and instantly replacing agreeable (manāpa) and disagreeable (amanāpa) feelings with equanimity (upekkha).

7. Six Rapid Similes for High-Level Sense Restraint:

The Buddha illustrated the rapid speed required to return the mind to equanimity during sensory contact:

Eye: As fast as a blink of an eye.

Ear: As quick as a finger-snap.

Nose: Like water droplets sliding instantly off a lotus leaf (udabindu yathāpi padumapatte).

Tongue: Like spitting out saliva from the tip of the tongue without effort.

Body: As fast as extending a bent arm or bending an extended arm.

Mind: Like drops of water falling onto an iron pan heated all day, evaporating instantly upon contact.

8. Radical Attention (Yoniso Manasikāra) vs. Conceptual Proliferation (Papañca):

Unwise attention (ayoniso manasikāra) acts like a deer chasing a mirage, leading to long streams of daydreaming and mental proliferation (papañca). Radical attention (yoniso manasikāra) goes directly to the origin point (yoni) of contact (phassa), recognizing that flavor or sensation exists only at the moment of collision between the internal and external bases, thereby dissolving the illusion of a self (attā).

9. Latency of Defilements (Anusaya) in Newborn Infants:

In response to Mālunkyaputta, the Buddha pointed out that even a newborn infant lying on its back—who lacks conscious concepts of body, self, rules, or others—possesses dormant underlying tendencies (anusaya) for:

Dogma of self-illusion (sakkāya-diṭṭhi-anusaya)

Doubt (vicikicchā-anusaya)

Adherence to rules and vows (sīlabbata-parāmāsa-anusaya)

Sensual lust (kāmarāga-anusaya)

Ill-will (vyāpāda-anusaya)

This proves that defilements cannot be eradicated by superficial, modern short-cut methods that ignore saṁsāric latency.

10. Underlying Tendencies Rooted in Feelings (Vedanā) and Grasping Attitudes:

Grasping (upādāna) occurs through three ego-attitudes: "this is mine" (craving, taṇhā), "this I am" (conceit, māna), and "this is my self" (views, diṭṭhi). Beneath feelings lie specific latent tendencies:

Pleasant feeling (sukha-vedanā): Underlaid by the tendency to lust (rāgānusaya).

Painful feeling (dukkha-vedanā): Underlaid by the tendency to aversion (paṭighānusaya).

Neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling (adukkhamasukha-vedanā): Underlaid by the tendency to ignorance (avijjā-anusaya).

11. Dissolution (Bhaṅga), Dispelling Compactness (Ghana-Saññā), and Stilling of Preparations:

Vipassanā meditation breaks down the perceived compactness of existence (ghana-saññā). The practitioner shifts focus from arising (udaya) to dissolution (bhaṅga), developing equanimity towards preparations (saṅkhārūpekkhā). Ultimate liberation requires turning away from all prepared (saṅkhata) states to the Unprepared (asaṅkhata)—the stilling of all preparations, the relinquishment of all acquisitions, the destruction of craving, dispassion, and cessation (sabba-saṅkhāra-samatha sabbūpadhi-paṭinissagga taṇhakkhaya virāga nirodha).

12. The Luminous Mind (Pabhassara-Citta) and Realizing Nibbāna in This Life:

The mind is intrinsically luminous (pabhassara), but defiled by adventitious taints. By removing these defilements, one realizes the cessation of the six sense-bases (saḷāyatana-nirodha) and the cessation of existence (bhava-nirodha). Nibbāna is not a post-mortem destination in a distant world, but the Deathless element (amata-dhātu) to be directly experienced right here in this life within one's own mind.
