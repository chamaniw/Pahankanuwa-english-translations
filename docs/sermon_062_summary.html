Sermon 062 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #62 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #62 (Sinhala Audio Dhamma Sermon No. 130)

Primary Theme: The delusion of perverted perceptions (vipallāsa) that mistake suffering (dukkha) for happiness (sukha), and the practice of internal scrutiny (antara-sammasana) via Dependent Arising (paṭicca-samuppāda) to overcome heedlessness (pamāda) and attain the cessation of becoming (bhava-nirodha).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Suppavāsā Koliyadhītā (mother of Ven. Sīvalī), her infant son (the future Great Arahant Sīvalī), the Supreme Buddha, Venerable Mahā Moggallāna, Venerable Sāriputta, and a wise lay disciple (attendant of Ven. Mahā Moggallāna).

The Interaction: Suppavāsā endures an extraordinary seven-year pregnancy followed by seven days of agonizing, obstructed labor. She sustains her mind through three noble reflections on the Buddha, Sangha, and Nibbāna. Upon receiving a word of blessing from the Buddha (“Sukhini hotu Suppavāsā...”), she safely gives birth to Sīvalī and wishes to offer alms to the Buddha and Sangha for seven days. Ven. Mahā Moggallāna persuades another scheduled lay donor to yield his turn; the lay disciple agrees on the condition of three guarantees (preservation of wealth, life, and faith). Ven. Moggallāna guarantees wealth and life through his psychic powers but emphasizes that personal faith (saddhā) is entirely one's own responsibility.

The Disaster / Turning Point: The underlying cause for this immense physical suffering is revealed through the Asātarūpa Jātaka: in a past life, Suppavāsā (then a queen mother) advised her son (the prince) to blockade and starve a besieged city for seven days to capture it. The kamma of that blockade ripened as seven years in the womb and seven days of birth agony. When the infant Sīvalī speaks to Ven. Sāriputta, describing his time in the womb as living "inside a jar of blood," Suppavāsā—blinded by maternal affection and perverted perception—tells the Buddha she would gladly have seven more such sons despite her immense suffering.

The Result / Resolution: Seeing Suppavāsā fall into heedlessness through perverted perception, the Buddha utters the famous Udāna verse (Asātaṃ sātarūpena...): "What is unpleasant in the guise of the pleasant... overwhelms the heedless person." The young child Sīvalī uses the deep recollection of the suffering of birth (jāti-dukkha) as his meditation subject (kammaṭṭhāna) and eventually attains full Arahantship.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Perverted Perceptions (Vipallāsa) and Heedlessness (Pamāda):

Sensual existence is dominated by four perversions of perception: viewing the impermanent (anicca) as permanent, the painful (dukkha) as pleasant, the unlovely (asubha) as beautiful, and non-self (anattā) as self. Ignorance and craving (taṇhā) cause beings to forget mountains of past agony, mistaking the unpleasant in the guise of the pleasant (asātaṃ sātarūpena) and falling back into saṃsāric heedlessness.

2. Personal Responsibility for Faith (Saddhā):

As demonstrated by Ven. Mahā Moggallāna's interaction with the lay disciple, even an Arahant of supreme psychic power cannot guarantee or control another person's faith. Genuine Buddhist faith is not blind devotion (amūlikā saddhā) or subservience; it must be self-directed, rooted in personal responsibility, and established through wise investigation (anuviccākāro), as advised in the Vīmaṃsaka Sutta and Upāli Sutta.

3. Kamma and its Inevitable Ripening (Kamma-vipāka):

The severe pain experienced by both Suppavāsā and Ven. Sīvalī was not accidental or uncaused, but the precise fruit of unwholesome kamma committed in a past birth (advising the blockade and starvation of a city for seven days, as detailed in the Asātarūpa Jātaka).

4. The Suffering of Birth (Jāti-Dukkha) as a Meditation Subject:

While worldly society celebrates births with merrymaking and annual birthday parties, the Dhamma reveals birth as a perilous disaster for both mother and child. Ven. Sīvalī utilized his direct recollection of the horrific confinement in the womb ("a jar of blood") as his primary insight subject to achieve total liberation.

5. Conditional Defilement and Purification (Paṭicca-samuppāda):

Refuting the fatalistic view of Pūraṇa Kassapa (who claimed beings are defiled or purified without cause), the Buddha explained to Mahāli Licchavi that defilement and purification occur strictly due to causes and conditions. Because the five aggregates (pañcakkhandha) contain a subtle aspect of gratification (assāda), attachment forms; because they contain danger (ādīnava) and suffering (dukkha), disenchantment (nibbidā) and liberation (nissaraṇa) become possible.

6. Internal Scrutiny (Antara-Sammasana):

A deep analytical contemplation highlighted by the Buddha where the practitioner investigates within: Aging and Death (jarāmaraṇa) arise from assets/acquisitions (upadhi, the five aggregates of grasping); assets arise from craving (taṇhā); craving arises and establishes itself wherever there are lovely and agreeable forms (piya-rūpa / sāta-rūpa), namely the six internal and external sense-bases.

7. The Simile of the Fragrant Poisoned Cup:

Worldly attachment to sense-objects is like a thirsty traveler drinking a delicious, colorful, and highly fragrant beverage that is secretly laced with lethal poison. The heedless drink it for temporary gratification and face death or severe agony; the wise practitioner recognizes the poison and rejects the drink altogether.

8. Modes of Wise Scrutiny (Sammasana-Vidhi) vs. Dogmatic Grasping (Parāmāsa):

The sermon contrasts dogmatic attachment (parāmāsa—clinging stubbornly to views) with insight scrutiny (sammasana—gentle, analytical examination). Practitioners must contemplate mental and physical phenomena through eleven specific insight modes: as impermanent (aniccatō), suffering (dukkhatō), a disease (rōgatō), a boil (gaṇḍatō), a dart (sallatō), a calamity (aghatō), an affliction (ābādhatō), alien/other (paratō), disintegrating (palōkatō), empty (suññatō), and non-self (anattatō).

9. The Banana Tree Simile for Volitional Formations (Saṅkhāra-sammasana):

The aggregate of volitional formations (saṅkhārakkhandha) is like a plantain tree trunk. If one tightly shoulders it home (parāmāsa), one gains nothing; but if one peels back layer after layer right where it stands (sammasana), one realizes it completely lacks heartwood or inner essence.

10. The Fire Simile and Disenchantment (Nibbidā):

All sense-bases, objects, sense-contacts (phassa), and sensations (vedanā) are actively burning with the fires of lust, hatred, and delusion (referencing the Āditta-pariyāya Sutta). Liberation requires developing nibbidā (disenchantment with saṃsāric phenomena), which naturally leads to virāga (dispassion) and ultimate purification.

11. The Dependent Arising and Cessation of Consciousness (Viññāṇa):

Consciousness is not a static soul or solid entity, but a rapidly flowing stream dependent on causes (e.g., eye + form + attention = eye-consciousness, like striking a matchstick to produce flame). Because consciousness relies entirely on volitional formations (saṅkhāra), the stilling of formations leads directly to the cessation of consciousness and the cessation of becoming (bhava-nirodho nibbānaṃ).
