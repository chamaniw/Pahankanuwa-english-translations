Sermon 077 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #77 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #77

Primary Theme: The rare opportunity of listening to the True Dhamma (saddhamma-savana), the overcoming of saṃsāric habits (vāsanā), the necessity of supreme respect for the Dhamma (dhammagārava), and the causal chain (uppāda-krama) leading from hearing to total liberation (vijjā-vimutti).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Five lay devotees visiting Jetavana monastery, Venerable Ānanda, and the Supreme Buddha.

The Interaction: Five laymen arrive to hear the Buddha preach. As the sermon proceeds, Venerable Ānanda (standing behind the Buddha, fanning him) notices their bizarrely contrasting behaviors:

1. The first falls into a deep sleep.

2. The second scratches the ground with his finger.

3. The third continuously shakes a nearby tree.

4. The fourth sits staring vacantly at the sky.

5. The fifth listens with rapt, undistracted attention.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Puzzled as to why four of these men behave disrespectfully while the Buddha preaches the sweet Dhamma, Ven. Ānanda asks the Buddha for the cause. The Buddha exercises his Extraordinary Knowledge of Inclinations and Underlying Tendency (āsayānusaya-ñāṇa) to reveal that each man is bound by deep-seated saṃsāric habits (vāsanā) formed over 500 consecutive lifetimes (jāti):

The sleeper was born as a snake/cobra for 500 births (now coiling up in sleep).

The ground-scratcher was an earthworm for 500 births.

The tree-shaker was a monkey for 500 births.

The sky-gazer was an astrologer for 500 births.

The attentive listener was a learned Vedic Brahmin for 500 births.

The Buddha explains that for most beings swept away in saṃsāra, useless "animal talk" and sensory distractions are pleasant, whereas hearing the Dhamma is rare and unpleasant due to the inner fires of lust (rāga), hatred (dosa), delusion (moha), and craving (taṇhā). He then utters the canonical verse (Dhammapada 251):

> Natthi rāgasamo aggi, natthi dosasamo gaho,

> natthi mohasamaṃ jālaṃ, natthi taṇhāsamo nadī.

> (There is no fire like lust, no grip like hatred, no net like delusion, no river like craving.)

The Result / Resolution: Understanding the grave danger of saṃsāra through the Buddha's structural breakdown of defilements and saṃsāric habituation, the fifth devotee—who possessed the requisite wholesome inclinations—attains the fruit of Stream-entry (sotāpatti-phala) on the spot.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Rarity of Listening to the True Dhamma (Dullabha-saddhammasavana) & Saṃsāric Obstacles

Hearing the Dhamma is exceptionally rare, not merely because the birth of a Buddha is rare, but because beings with the inner disposition (adhimutti) to listen attentively are few.

Deep-seated saṃsāric weaknesses (vāsanā) block the "inner ear." Even when the acoustic sound of a sermon strikes the eardrum, the Dhamma fails to penetrate the mind of a heavily conditioned person. Hearing the words "Buddha," "Dhamma," and "Saṅgha" is an opportunity obtained only after millions of aeons (kalpas).

2. Knowledge of Inclinations and Underlying Tendencies (Āsayānusaya-Ñāṇa)

The Buddha possesses six extraordinary knowledges (asādhāraṇa-ñāṇa) not shared by general disciples. Among them is āsayānusaya-ñāṇa, through which he instantly evaluates a person's complete spiritual profile via three factors:

Temperament (Carita): Past wholesome and unwholesome actions (kamma) shaping present habits.

Inclination (Adhimutti): The present flexibility and direction of the mind toward wholesome or unwholesome bents.

Capability vs. Incapability (Bhavya-abhavya-tā): Whether an individual has the potential to realize noble paths and fruits (ariya-magga-phala) in the current lifetime.

Even when preaching to a mixed crowd where four out of five cannot benefit immediately, the Buddha continues to preach if even a single individual (bhavya-puggala) can attain spiritual fruit.

3. The Four Benefits of Listening to Dhamma (Dhammasavana-Ānisaṃsa)

When approached with wisdom and full attention, listening to Dhamma provides four classic benefits:

1. Assutaṃ suṇāti: Hearing what has not been heard before.

2. Sutaṃ pariyodapeti: Purifying and clarifying what has been previously heard.

3. Kaṅkhaṃ vitarati: Dispelling remaining doubts.

4. Diṭṭhiṃ ujuṃ karoti: Straightening out and rectifying one's Right View (sammā-diṭṭhi).

4. Five Factors Hindering Fruitful Hearing

A listener fails to reap any spiritual fruit from a Dhamma sermon if they:

1. Disparage or mock the Dhamma content.

2. Insult or blame the speaker/expounder.

3. Blame themselves or fall into self-absorbed remorse and regret over past shortcomings.

4. Listen with a distracted, un-concentrated mind (vikkhiptacitta).

5. Reflect upon the heard Dhamma in an incorrect or improper manner (ayoniso manasikāra).

5. Supreme Respect for the Dhamma (Dhammagārava)

Respect for the Dhamma takes priority over respect for individuals. In the fifth week post-Enlightenment under the Ajapāla banyan tree, the Buddha searched the cosmos for a superior to revere, but finding none superior in virtue (sīla), concentration (samādhi), or wisdom (paññā), he determined to live honoring and respecting the Dhamma he realized—a decision ratified by Sahampati Brahmā.

Vinaya Rules (Sekhiya): Sixteen Vinaya rules govern Dhamma instruction, prohibiting monks from preaching to anyone displaying disrespect—such as non-sick individuals wearing footwear/turbans, holding umbrellas/staffs/weapons, lying down while the speaker sits, sitting on higher seats, sitting with knees clasped (binding hands), or walking ahead of/on a main road while the preacher is behind/on a side path.

6. The Mindset for Listening (Atthikatvā Manasikatvā)

Proper listening requires a specific active mental orientation summarized as: atthikatvā, manasikatvā, sabbacetaso samannāharitvā ohitasoto dhammaṃ suṇāti.

Atthikatvā: Recognizing the sermon as personally beneficial for one's own Nibbāna.

Manasikatvā: Reflecting word-by-word on the meaning.

Sabbacetaso samannāharitvā: Gathering the total mind with unbroken focus.

Ohitasoto: "Inclining the ear" attentively, rather than letting the sound play passively in the background while performing domestic chores.

7. Three Types of Listeners / Types of Wisdom

The Buddha classifies Dhamma listeners into three distinct categories:

1. Upside-Down Wisdom (Avakkujja-pañña): Like a pot placed upside down; no matter how much water (Dhamma) is poured over it, nothing is retained during or after the sermon.

2. Lap-Wisdom (Ucchaṅga-pañña): Like a person holding sweetmeats in their lap who suddenly stands up without mindfulness, scattering everything. They reflect well while sitting, but forget everything the moment they rise.

3. Broad/Upright Wisdom (Puthu-pañña): Like an upright pot that holds water. The listener reflects clearly during the sermon and retains, examines, and applies the Dhamma after rising.

8. Efficacy of Dhamma Sound Even for Non-Human Beings

Even without intellectual comprehension of Pali words, listening with pure faith to the acoustic sound of Dhamma (sadda-rasa) yields immense karmic benefit:

Maṇḍūka Devaputta (Frog-Deity): A frog listening attentively to the Buddha's "Brahmā-voice" (brahmassara) on the banks of Gaggarā lake was accidentally pierced by a cowherd's staff. Dying with mind settled on the Buddha's voice, he was instantly reborn as a deity in Tāvatiṃsa heaven.

The Hen/Sow Story: A young hen near an alms hall listened to a monk chanting an insight meditation subject (vipassanā-kammaṭṭhāna). Killed by a hawk, she was reborn as Princess Ubbirī, who later developed the perception of a worm-infested corpse (puḷuvaka-saññā) in a toilet, attaining the first absorption (paṭhama-jhāna). After various existences, she eventually attained Arahantship as a nun in ancient Sri Lanka.

9. Process of Realization During Dhamma Hearing

Realization of supramundane paths and fruits (lokuttara-magga-phala) can occur during five specific occasions (listening, preaching, chanting, reflecting, or concentrating on a meditation sign). The sequential inner mental process during listening is:

Experiencing Meaning & Dhamma (atthappaṭisaṃvedī / dhammappaṭisaṃvedī) ➔

Joyful Delight (pāmojja) ➔

Rapture (pīti) ➔

Tranquility (passaddhi) ➔

Unworldly Happiness (nirāmisa-sukha) ➔

Concentration (samādhi) ➔

Insight Contemplation (anicca, dukkha, anattā) ➔

Path Attainment ➔

10. The Five Attributes of the Learned Person (Bahussuta) vs. Technology

A true bahussuta is defined by five inner mental qualities: sutā (heard much), dhatā (retained/borne in mind), vacasā paricitā (practiced/recited verbally), manasānupakkhitā (examined/contemplated with the mind), and diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā (well-penetrated with Right View).

Devices and computers can store textual data, but they cannot perform manasānupakkhitā or realize Nibbāna. Over-reliance on external technology reduces listening to a passive habit ("the sermon is playing"), causing people to lose the Knowledge of Drawbacks (Ādīnava-ñāṇa)—the highest level of mundane wisdom, which discerns the hidden harms within modern technological conveniences.

11. The Causal Chain of Liberation (Sotāpattiyanga and Downpour Simile)

The journey from casual association to full liberation flows through an unbroken causal chain (uppāda-krama):

Association with Virtuous Persons (sappurisasaṃseva) ➔

Hearing the True Dhamma (saddhammasavana) ➔

Radical Attention (yoniso manasikāra) ➔

Mindfulness &amp; Clear Comprehension (sati-sampajañña) ➔

Restraint of Senses (indriya-saṃvara) ➔

Threefold Good Conduct (sucharita) ➔

Four Establishments of Mindfulness (satipaṭṭhāna) ➔

Seven Factors of Enlightenment (bojjhaṅga) ➔

Knowledge and Liberation (vijjā-vimutti)

The Downpour Simile: Just as heavy rain on a mountain peak flows down to fill rock clefts and gullies, which fill small rivers, which fill large rivers, which ultimately fill the great ocean—so does authentic association with a virtuous person (sappurisa) sequentially fill every stage of the path up to Nibbāna.

12. The Counter-Current Nature of Dhamma (Paṭisotagāmī) & Becoming the Dhamma (Dhammabhūta)

While craving (taṇhā) flows downward like a non-stop river, the Dhamma moves against the current (paṭisotagāmī).

The six qualities of the Dhamma (Svākkhāto, Sandiṭṭhiko, Akāliko, Ehipassiko, Opanayiko, Paccattaṃ Veditabbo Viññūhi) emphasize that the Dhamma operates causally: ehipassiko ("come and see") draws the practitioner inward, while opanayiko ("leading onwards") mechanically pushes one step of practice to the next.

By applying the three trainings—Virtue (sīla to tame raw animal wildness), Concentration (samādhi to still distraction), and Wisdom (paññā to penetrate impermanence)—the practitioner directly realizes the Dhamma and becomes "become the Dhamma" (dhammabhūta), transforming into a living embodiment of liberation.
