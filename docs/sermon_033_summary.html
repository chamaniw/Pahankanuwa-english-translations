Sermon 033 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #33 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #33

Primary Theme: Deconstructing the illusion of physical beauty (subha-saññā) through the perception of foulness (asubha-saññā) and charnel-ground contemplations, leading to the insight into the conditioned nature (saṅkhata-lakkhaṇa) of stability (ṭhiti), the abandonment of grasping (upādāna), and the realization of emptiness (suññatā).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Sirimā (the famously beautiful courtesan of Rājagaha and younger sister of the physician Jīvaka) and a young monk who became infatuated with her physical form purely through hearing her beauty described.

The Interaction: Sirimā, having attained Stream-entry (Sotāpatti-phala) after hearing the Dhamma from the Buddha, arranged to offer daily ration-meals (salāka-bhatta) to eight monks. A monk who ate at her house traveled to a distant temple and passionately described her supreme physical beauty to the Saṅgha. Overcome by lustful curiosity, the young monk traveled overnight to secure a turn for the ration-meal at Sirimā's house the next morning.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Upon his arrival, Sirimā fell gravely ill, appearing weak, trembling, and supported by maidservants. The young monk was so overwhelmed by agitation and disappointment (saṃvega) that he could not eat; he returned to his monastery and lay down starving for three days. That evening, Sirimā died. The Buddha instructed King Bimbisāra not to cremate her corpse but to let it rot in the charnel ground for three days. On the fourth day—when her body was bloated, oozing purulent matter, and infested with worms—the Buddha, monks, and citizens gathered. The Buddha had the King auction Sirimā’s corpse, reducing the price from 1,000 kahāpaṇas down to a single kākaṇika, and finally for free. No one would take her corpse even for free.

The Result / Resolution: Demonstrating the stark contrast between her former value and her present decayed state, the Buddha uttered Dhammapada Verse 147 (Passa cittakataṃ bimbaṃ...). Upon witnessing the stark reality of the body and hearing the verse, the young monk's conceptualized image shattered, and he attained Stream-entry (Sotāpatti-phala), alongside a vast assembly of people who attained Dhamma-realization.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Perception of Beauty (Subha-Saññā) vs. Perception of Foulness (Asubha-Saññā)

The perception of physical beauty (subha-saññā) acts as a primary fetter binding beings to saṃsāra by feeding lust (rāga). The Buddha teaches the perception of foulness (asubha-saññā) not out of cynicism, but as a crucial analytical tool to induce disillusionment (nibbidā), shatter sensual delusion, and facilitate liberation.

2. Exegesis of Dhammapada Verse 147

Passa cittakataṃ bimbaṃ: Look at this embellished image or statue-like body, artificially held together by bones and sinews.

Arukāyaṃ samussitaṃ: A body that is essentially a heap of sores, held upright.

Āturaṃ bahusaṅkappaṃ: Diseased (ātura), yet a subject of prolific mental fabrications and poetic imaginations (bahusaṅkappa).

Yassa natthi dhuvaṃ ṭhiti: In which there is no permanence, stability, or enduring duration (ṭhiti).

The body is inherently sick, constantly afflicted by basic biological drives (cold, heat, hunger, thirst, defecation, urination) and forced into constant postural changes.

3. The Triad of Gratification (Assāda), Danger (Ādīnava), and Escape (Nissaraṇa)

Drawing from the Mahā-dukkhakkhandha Sutta, Ven. Nanananda highlights the Buddha's systematic framework:

Gratification (Assāda): The temporary pleasure derived from physical form (e.g., a maiden in her prime at 15–16 years old).

Danger (Ādīnava): The inevitable alteration of that form through aging (80–100 years old), disease (lying in one's own excrement), death, and progressive stages of charnel-ground decay.

Escape (Nissaraṇa): The complete removal and abandonment of desire and lust (chanda-rāga-vinaya / chanda-rāga-pahāna) for forms.

4. Redefining Sensual Pleasure (Kāma) as Conceptualized Lust

Citing the canonical verse Na te kāmā yāni citrāni loke..., the sermon emphasizes that external, beautiful objects (citrāni) in the world are not themselves sensual pleasures (kāma). True kāma is the lust of conceptualized intentions (saṅkapparāga) within the mind. The wise do not destroy external objects; they eradicate their own desire (chandaṃ vinayanti) toward them.

5. Internal (Ajjhatta) and External (Bahiddhā) Contemplation in Satipaṭṭhāna

Using the analogy of honing a knife on a whetstone (stroking upward, downward, and then fluidly in both directions), the sermon details the insight methodology of Satipaṭṭhāna:

Observing decay externally (bahiddhā) in another's corpse.

Applying that vision internally (ajjhatta) to one's own body using the formula: 'Upasaṃharati'—"This body too is of such a nature, will become like this, it is not exempt from this fate."

Merging internal and external (ajjhatta-bahiddhā) to erase the artificial, delusional distinction of self vs. other (attānu-diṭṭhi / asmi-māna), recognizing that both consist merely of the four primary elements (mahābhūta).

6. The Perception of Bones (Aṭṭhika-Saññā) and Direct Insight

Illustrated through the story of Ven. Tissa—who saw a woman laugh, caught sight of her teeth, and immediately perceived a walking skeleton (aṭṭhika-saññā)—the sermon explains how a honed eye of insight (vipassanā-ñāṇa) sees through conventional appearance directly into the fundamental foulness and emptiness of the physical frame.

7. The Analytical Honing of Arising (Samudaya) and Vanishing (Vaya)

Contemplating samudayadhamma (arising) and vayadhamma (vanishing) individually sharpens the mind, but observing them simultaneously (samudaya-vayadhammānupassī) yields penetrative wisdom (nibbedhika-paññā). At this juncture, the illusion of temporal continuity dissolves, exposing emptiness (suññatā).

8. Deconstruction of Conditioned Stability (Saṅkhata-Lakkhaṇa)

The sermon analyzes the three characteristics of the conditioned (saṅkhata):

Uppādo paññāyati (Arising is manifest)

Vayo paññāyati (Vanishing is manifest)

Ṭhitassa aññathattaṃ paññāyati (Alteration while existing is manifest)

Ven. Nanananda highlights that "duration" or "stability" (ṭhiti) is an illusion created by grasping (upādāna). There is no static point of existence; ṭhiti is merely a continuous process of rolling-over (aññathābhāva). Realizing that stability is precariously trapped between arising and vanishing causes grasping to collapse (upādāna-paccayā bhavo → bhava-nirodha).

9. The Unconditioned Element (Asaṅkhata-Dhātu)

The unconditioned (asaṅkhata) is characterized by na uppādo paññāyati, na vayo paññāyati, na ṭhitassa aññathattaṃ paññāyati (no arising, no vanishing, no alteration of duration). Nibbāna is not a physical realm to inhabit, but the complete cessation of mental fabrications and graspings generated through ignorance (avijjā) and craving (taṇhā).

10. Udāna Analysis of Becoming (Bhava) and Alteration

Reflecting on the Udāna verse Ayaṃ loko santāpajāto phassapareto..., the Thero explains:

The world suffers from contact (phassa) and mistakenly identifies a aggregate of disease as "self" (rogaṃ vadati attato).

Whatever the world conceptualizes, it alters into something else (yena yena hi maññati, tato taṃ hoti aññathā).

Beings delight in becoming (bhava), but the very becoming they delight in is a source of fear/danger (yadabhinandati taṃ bhayaṃ), and the alteration they fear is suffering (yassa bhāyati taṃ dukkhaṃ).

The holy life (brahmacariya) is lived precisely for the abandoning of becoming (bhava-vippahāna).

11. Steering Clear of the Two Extremes via Relinquishment

The Buddha avoids both Eternity View (bhava-diṭṭhi / sassata-diṭṭhi) and Annihilation View (vibhava-diṭṭhi / uccheda-diṭṭhi). Liberation is attained neither by eternal existence nor by post-mortem annihilation, but through the destruction of all grasping (sabbupādānakkhaya) and the relinquishment of all acquisitions (sabbūpadhipatinissagga).

12. Viewing the World as Empty (Suññata) to Overcome Death

Citing the Mogharāja Sutta (Suññato lokaṃ avekkhassu...), the sermon concludes that viewing the world as empty and uprooting the conceit of self (attānudiṭṭhi) renders one invisible to Māra (death). Māra can only cling to what is prepared and grasped (saṅkhata); by abandoning all acquisitions (upadhi), one realizes the true liberation of mind (citta-vimutti) in this very life.
