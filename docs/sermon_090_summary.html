Sermon 090 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #90 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #90 (Sinhala Audio Sermon No. 105)

Primary Theme: Preserving the Buddha-Dispensation (Buddha-Sāsana) through personal exertion (vīriya), moral example (sīla), and the inner purification of one's mind (sacittapariyodapanaṃ), illustrated through the simile of the elephant stuck in the mud and canonical/historical exemplars.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The royal elephant Bhaddaka (belonging to King Kosala) and his wise elephant trainer.

The Interaction: Bhaddaka, once an immensely powerful war elephant in his youth, had grown weak due to decay and aging (jarā). One day, he descended into a large lake and became trapped in a deep mud heap, unable to extract himself.

The Disaster / Turning Point: As the elephant lay helpless in the swamp, onlookers lamented his downfall. Hearing this, King Kosala dispatched the royal elephant trainer to rescue him. Rather than using physical force, the trainer creatively arranged a mock battle scene within sight of the elephant and ordered the beating of war drums.

The Result / Resolution: Motivated by the sounds of war and a sudden recall of his former heroic capability, Bhaddaka gathered his strength, hastily arose from the mud, and freed himself. Observing this, the Buddha compared the elephant's self-rescue to the spiritual path, admonishing the monks to cultivate diligence (appamāda), arouse energy (vīriya), and pull themselves out of the swamp of defilements (kilesa-paṅka).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The True Essence of Diligence (Appamāda):

Diligence in the Buddha-Dispensation is not merely ordinary alertness; it is defined as the constant guarding of one's own mind from inner defilements (sacittam anurakkhatha). It forms the fundamental starting point of the path to Nibbāna.

2. The "Sāsana Elephant" and True Preservation:

The Buddha-Dispensation (Sāsana) is metaphorically depicted as a majestic elephant stuck in a mud heap of defilements. Preserving the Sāsana does not mean merely building temples, stupas, or statues, which are only external symbols; true preservation consists of establishing the internal virtues of virtue (sīla), concentration (samādhi), and wisdom (paññā) within individual minds.

3. The Threefold Elements of Energy (Dhātu):

Internal effort requires bringing forth latent powers categorized in the Dhamma as:

Ārambha-dhātu: The element of initiation (the power to begin a righteous course of action).

Nikkama-dhātu: The element of launching (the power to execute and sustain the effort).

Parakkama-dhātu: The element of exertion or persistence (the power to push forward and overcome obstacles).

4. The Core Mandate of All Buddhas:

The famous stanza "Sabbapāpassa akaraṇaṁ..." outlines the complete threefold training (tikkhā):

Sabbapāpassa akaraṇaṁ (Avoiding all evil) = Virtue (sīla).

Kusalassa upasampadā (Cultivating the wholesome) = Concentration (samādhi) and the absorptions (jhāna).

Sacittapariyodapanaṁ (Purifying one's own mind) = Wisdom (paññā / vipassanā-paññā).

5. Exemplary Character as a Silent Sermon:

Emperor Ashoka (Dharmashoka) serves as a supreme historical example of state-level social reform brought about through an internal revolution of the mind—transitioning from Candashoka (Ashoka the Fierce) to Dharmashoka. By abandoning warfare, promoting universal compassion (karuṇā), establishing the granting of safety (abhaya-dāna), and leading by personal example, he demonstrated that "example is better than precept."

6. Cultivating the Perception of Capability:

Just as the weak elephant extracted himself upon remembering his battle heritage, practitioners must overcome spiritual despair by focusing on undefeated, heroic characters from Buddhist history (noble theras, therīs, and lay disciples) rather than defeated fictional characters, thereby generating faith (saddhā) and mindfulness (sati).

7. The Four Right Efforts (Sammappadhāna):

The systemic application of energy requires four functions:

Saṁvara: Restraining unarisen unwholesome thoughts.

Pahāṇa: Abandoning arisen unwholesome thoughts.

Bhāvanā: Cultivating unarisen wholesome states.

Anurakkhana: Preserving and stabilizing already arisen wholesome states.

8. The Voluntarily Undertaken Precept (Sīla-Samādāna) and its Fragrance:

The word "samādiyāmi" highlights self-exertion and voluntary determination, not external compulsion. Abstaining from killing acts as a gift of infinite safety (abhaya-dāna). Pure morality generates the "fragrance of virtue" (sīla-gandha), which—unlike physical scents—spreads both with and against the wind, granting an unshakeable blameless happiness (anavajja-sukha).

9. Ven. Angulimāla's Transformation and the Noble Birth (Ariyā Jāti):

Ven. Angulimāla’s story illustrates how establishing a firm spiritual boundary through ordination—marking his life anew from his "Noble Birth" (ariyā jāti)—cut off past remorse over prior evil acts. This determination allowed his mind to achieve concentration, perform a miraculous Act of Truth (sacca-kiriyā) for a suffering mother, and swiftly attain Arahantship.

10. Eradication of Ignorance as the Ultimate Stain (Avijjā Paramaṁ Malaṁ):

While sīla restrains bodily and verbal misdeeds and samādhi temporarily suppresses the mental hindrances (nīvaraṇa), ignorance (avijjā) remains the fundamental rust of all actions. It can only be cleansed through insight wisdom (vipassanā-paññā) contemplating the three characteristics: impermanence (anicca), unsatisfactoriness/stress (dukkha), and non-self (anattā).

11. Individual Responsibility and the Spiritual Friend (Kalyāṇa-Mitta):

One who is stuck in a swamp cannot pull another out (asamboṭho samboṭhaṃ uddharissatīti netaṃ ṭhānaṃ vijjati). Preserving the Sāsana cannot be delegated to others. One must first liberate oneself to become a genuine spiritual friend (kalyāṇa-mitta) capable of offering others a true "change of heart" (hita-māruvak) and guiding them toward ending repeated existence (punabbhava).
