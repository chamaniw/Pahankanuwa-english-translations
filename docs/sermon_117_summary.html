Sermon 117 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #117 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #117

Primary Theme: The practice of self-exhortation (attanā codayattānaṃ), self-examination, and utilizing authentic internal mementos of mindfulness (satipaṭṭhāna / sihivaṭan) and objects of spiritual urgency (saṃvega-vatthu) to achieve complete self-mastery and liberation.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: A destitute manual laborer and farmer in Sāvatthī who, after entering the Sangha, came to be known as Venerable Nangalakula ("of the plow family").

The Interaction: A compassionate monk saw the poor man going to work with a plow on his shoulder and wearing a dirty, ragged loincloth. The monk offered to ordain him, brought him to Jetavana monastery, bathed him, and conferred ordination. The monk placed the laborer's old plow and ragged cloth on a tree branch within the ordination hall premises (Sīma Mālakaya) as a keepsake.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Enjoying the plentiful four requisites of the monastic community, Venerable Nangalakula eventually became disillusioned with the monastic life and felt a strong urge to disrobe. However, whenever he went to the tree to retrieve his old rags, the sight of them triggered intense spiritual urgency (saṃvega). He fiercely admonished himself ("You shameless man! Do you expect to wear this and do hired labor again?"), driving his mind back to the practice. He repeatedly visited this "teacher" (ācariya) whenever unwholesome states arose.

The Result / Resolution: By constantly using his past symbols of poverty as a meditation subject (kammaṭṭhāna) and an anchor for self-admonition, he eliminated all defilements and attained Arahantship. When questioned by other monks why he no longer visited the tree, he revealed that his spiritual blockages were completely severed. The Buddha verified his declaration of Arahantship and uttered Dhammapada verses 379–380.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Self-Exhortation and Self-Measurement (Attanā codayattānaṃ, paṭimāse attamattanā):

The Pali term codanā implies more than external accusation; it signifies driving or urging oneself forward (like driving cattle out of mud). Paṭimāse attamattanā involves measuring oneself against ideal spiritual standards to spur moral reformation and mental discipline.

2. The Three Principles of Governance/Authority (Adhipateyya):

To reform character and abstain from unwholesome deeds, one relies on three guiding authorities:

Attādhipateyya (Self-sovereignty): Refraining from bad deeds out of self-respect and personal authority.

Lokādhipateyya (World-sovereignty): Refraining from bad deeds out of respect for the world, avoiding censure from the wise (lokāpavāda / parānuvāda).

Dhammādhipateyya (Dhamma-sovereignty): Aligning conduct strictly with the principles of the Truth taught by the Buddha.

3. Objects of Spiritual Urgency (Saṃvega-vatthu):

The force driving the journey to Nibbāna is saṃvega (spiritual urgency). The Buddha outlines eight objects of reflection:

Jāti (Birth/Arising)

Jarā (Decay/Aging)

Vyādhi (Sickness)

Maraṇa (Death)

Apāya (Woeful states)

Past Dukkha endured in saṃsāra

Future Dukkha to be endured if unliberated

Dukkha endured in searching for food/livelihood in daily life.

4. True Mementos vs. Worldly Souvenirs (Sihivaṭan):

Worldly mementos often increase defilements and prolong saṃsāra. True mementos are internal anchors of mindfulness (sihivaṭan / satipaṭṭhāna) that purify the mind, renew Right Effort (Samyak Vyāyāma), and assist in abandoning unwholesome states while cultivating wholesome ones.

5. Self-Discipline and Ultimate Self-Reliance (Attā hi attano nātho):

Liberation cannot be granted by an external deity or savior. The individual must tame themselves (attā hi sudantena), just as a merchant meticulously tames a wild horse (assaṃ bhadraṃva vāṇijo). Taking precepts (samādiyāmi) is a personal vow of self-discipline rather than an external formality.

6. The Four Types of Fear (Bhaya):

Constructive spiritual progress requires recognizing and fearing what ought to be feared (bhaye bhayadassino):

Attānuvāda-bhaya: Fear of self-reproach (the internal guilt and distress caused by one's own unwholesome actions).

Parānuvāda-bhaya: Fear of reproach by others/the wise.

Daṇḍa-bhaya: Fear of legal or physical punishment.

Duggati-bhaya: Fear of rebirth in woeful realms (apāya).

7. The Threefold Training (Sikkhā) as a Sequential Process:

Sīla (Virtue): Tames body and speech, providing lightness of mind, leisure, and freedom from remorse (avippatisāra).

Samādhi (Concentration): Calms the five hindrances (nīvaraṇa) through focused meditation (kammaṭṭhāna), producing spiritual joy (nirāmisa-pīti).

Paññā (Wisdom): Penetrates reality through insight (vipassanā), eradicating ignorance (avijjā).

8. Direct Contemplation through the Four Establishments of Mindfulness (Satipaṭṭhāna):

Wisdom develops by dynamically observing reality via four bases:

Kāyānupassanā: Seeing the body as a collection of elements (dhātu) and 32 impurities.

Vedanānupassanā: Observing pleasant, painful, and neutral feelings arising and ceasing.

Cittānupassanā: Monitoring the fluctuating states of mind (greed, anger, delusion).

Dhammānupassanā: Scrutinizing mental phenomena and cognized objects.

9. Penetration of the Three Marks of Existence (Tilakkhaṇa):

Insight reveals that all compounded things (saṅkhāra) are Inconstant/Impermanent (anicca), inherently oppressive and full of Suffering (dukkha), and devoid of any core, essence, or self (Not-self/Non-self / anattā), resembling a hollow banana trunk (kadalikkhandha).

10. Immediate Efficacy and Empirical Nature of the Dhamma:

The Buddha's teaching is directly visible (sandiṭṭhika), timeless/immediate (akālika), inviting empirical verification (ehipassika), leading onward to higher stages (opanayika), and personally realized by the wise (paccattaṃ veditabbo viññūhi).

11. Cultivation of the Five Spiritual Faculties (Indriya):

Listening to and practicing the Dhamma balances and strengthens the five spiritual faculties: Faith (saddhā), Energy (viriya), Mindfulness (sati), Concentration (samādhi), and Wisdom (paññā), which collectively serve as the supportive condition (upanissaya) for realizing the stages of awakening (Sotāpatti, Sakadāgāmi, Anāgāmi, Arahatta).
