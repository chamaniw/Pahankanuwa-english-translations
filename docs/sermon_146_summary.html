Sermon 146 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #146 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #146

Primary Theme: Eradicating the root causes of saṃsāric suffering—specifically anger (kodha), jealousy (issā), and pride (māna)—by applying the medicine of Dhamma, cultivating boundless loving-kindness (mettā), and relinquishing attachment to Name-and-Form (nāmarūpa).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Princess Rohiṇī (sister of Ven. Anuruddha Thero; former chief queen of Bārāṇasī), Ven. Anuruddha Thero, and the Supreme Buddha.

The Interaction: Ven. Anuruddha returned to his birthplace, Kapilavatthu, with five hundred bhikkhus. While his relatives came to see him at Nigrodhārāma, his sister Rohiṇī hid at home due to a disfiguring, leprosy-like skin disease. Ven. Anuruddha summoned her and instructed her to sell her personal jewelry to finance a two-story assembly hall (Uposathāgāra) for the Saṅgha, while personally performing duties such as sweeping, cleaning, and preparing seats and water.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Rohiṇī dutifully executed the instructions, and her physical condition partially improved through her dedicated service (vatta-paṭipatti). At the dedication alms-offering, the Buddha summoned Rohiṇī and revealed the saṃsāric origin (kamma-nidāna) of her affliction: in a past life as the chief queen of Bārāṇasī, out of intense jealousy and anger toward a court dancing girl favored by the king, she had sprinkled powdered stinging pods over the girl’s bed and body, causing painful itching and boils.

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha delivered a sermon based on Dhammapada verse 221. Upon penetrating the Dhamma taught in the stanza, Rohiṇī’s skin disease was completely cured—restoring her skin to a golden complexion—and she attained the fruit of Stream-entry (Sotāpatti-phala).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Mind Defilement as the Root Cause of Kammic Disease (Kamma-Roga):

Ordinary physicians attribute skin diseases and chronic ailments to physical factors like the "impurity of blood." The Buddha reveals that the saṃsāric cause (nidāna) of such diseases often lies in mental defilements (kilesa)—particularly past unwholesome deeds born of anger (kodha), jealousy (issā), and pride (māna).

2. Purification through Duty and Relinquishment (Vatta-Paṭipatti):

Ven. Anuruddha’s advice to Rohiṇī to sell her gold ornaments and personally sweep the assembly hall served as a direct spiritual remedy. Relinquishing wealth wore down her attachment, while engaging in humble physical service to the Saṅgha helped wear away the heavy energy of past unwholesome kamma.

3. Exegesis of Dhammapada Verse 221:

The stanza outlines the path to full liberation:

Kodhaṃ jahe: Abandon anger.

Vippajaheyya mānaṃ: Dispel pride.

Saṃyojanaṃ sabbamatikkameyya: Overcome all ten fetters (saṃyojana), beginning with the elimination of self-illusion (sakkāya-diṭṭhi), doubt (vicikicchā), and adherence to rules and rituals (sīlabbata-parāmāsa) at Stream-entry.

Taṃ nāmarūpasmiṃ asajjamānaṃ: Do not cling to Name-and-Form (nāmarūpa).

Akiñcanaṃ nānupatanti dukkhā: Upon the unattached person who "owns nothing" (akiñcana), saṃsāric sufferings do not fall.

4. The Definition of Nāmarūpa and Akiñcana:

Nāma consists of mental phenomena: feeling (vedanā), perception (saññā), volition (cetanā), contact (phassa), and attention (manasikāra). Rūpa is constructed based on the four great elements (mahābhūta): earth (pathavī), water (āpo), fire (tejo), and wind (vāyo). Through wise attention (yoniso manasikāra), one sees no core or self in this collection. One who abandons greed, hatred, and delusion regarding nāmarūpa owns nothing (akiñcana) and becomes untraceable by suffering.

5. The Dual Simile of the Serpent (Uraga Sutta):

Snake Venom (Sappavisa): Arisen anger (uppatitaṃ kodhaṃ) must be treated immediately with Dhamma medicine like snake venom, before it spreads and turns into deep-seated hostility (dosa), malice (āghāta), or enmity (vaira). Worldly beings seek outward revenge, but the noble practitioner appeases anger from within.

Shedding the Worn-Out Skin (Urago jiṇṇamiva tacaṃ purāṇaṃ): Just as a serpent sheds its old skin against a branch and moves on without attachment or conflict, a practitioner instantly lets go of the object of anger, obliterating the boundary between "self" and "other."

6. The Nature of Anger (Poisoned Root, Honeyed Tip):

Anger possesses a poisoned root (mūla visa) and a honeyed tip (aga rasa). Driven by the immediate "sweetness" of seeking revenge, worldly individuals consume the poison and bring ruin upon themselves across Saṃsāra. True victory lies in slaying anger itself, not the external object of provocation.

7. The Simile of the Saw (Kakacūpama Sutta):

The Buddha taught that even if bandits sever one’s limbs piece by piece with a double-handled saw, allowing a single thought of anger to defile the mind violates his teaching. Recollecting the Kakacūpama (āvajja kakacūpamaṃ) serves as an immediate mindfulness tool to subdue sudden rage.

8. Fivefold Categorization of Beings in the Karaṇīyametta Sutta:

The Buddha categorizes the entire world into five totalities to ensure loving-kindness (mettā) is radiated without exception (anavasesā):

Tasā vā thāvarā vā: The trembling/fearful or the steadfast/unmoved.

Dīghā / Mahantā / Majjhimā / Rassa / Aṇuka / Thūla: Long, large, medium, short, subtle, or stout.

Diṭṭhā vā addiṭṭhā: Seen or unseen.

Dūre vasanti avidūre: Dwelling far or near.

Bhūtā vā sambhavesī vā: Born beings or those seeking birth (e.g., embryos).

9. The Pure Stance of Loving-Kindness (Mettā):

Genuine mettā goes beyond non-violence; it wishes that no one deceives another (na paro paraṃ nikubbetha), despises anyone anywhere (nātimaññetha katthaci naṃ kañci), or desires another’s suffering out of resentment (byārosanā paṭighasaññā nāññamaññassa dukkhamiccheyya).

10. The Mother’s Love Simile and Unbounded Mind (Mettā-Cetovimutti):

Just as a mother protects her only child at the risk of her own life (mātā yathā niyaṃ puttaṃ āyusā ekaputtamanurakkhe), one must expand a boundless heart (mānasaṃ bhāvaye aparimāṇaṃ) toward all living beings. When mettā is maintained across all four postures (tiṭṭhaṃ caraṃ nisinno vā sayāno vā) throughout one's waking hours, it breaks down all mental walls, achieving liberation of mind through loving-kindness (mettā-cetovimutti).

11. Integration of Dhamma into Daily Vocation:

Meditation is not confined to a formal posture. By applying an attitude of mettā to daily work, occupations cease to be a burden. Performing duties conscientiously for the welfare of others generates continuous daily merit (kusala), ensuring spiritual protection against unexpected kammic mishaps.

12. Sympathetic Joy (Muditā) as the Antidote to Jealousy (Issā):

Just as mettā counters anger, sympathetic joy (muditā) neutralizes jealousy. By extending the natural joy one feels for one's own child's success to neighbors and strangers, one dismantles the kammic roots that lead to painful future retribution.
