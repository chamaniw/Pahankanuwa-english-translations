Sermon 133 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #133 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #133

Primary Theme: The essential nature, true spiritual value, and practical mechanics of merit (puñña) and wholesome action (kusala) within the framework of the Middle Path (majjhimā paṭipadā); dispelling scholarly misconceptions regarding kamma responsibility and non-self (anattā) by elucidating the two levels of Right View (sammādiṭṭhi).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Sumana, the youngest daughter of the great banker (seṭṭhi) Anāthapiṇḍika of Sāvatthī.

The Interaction: Anāthapiṇḍika and Visākhā routinely arranged daily alms for two thousand monks. Due to being frequently invited elsewhere to organize major ceremonies, Anāthapiṇḍika delegated the organization of home alms-giving to his daughters in succession: Mahā-Subhaddā (who attained stream-entry, sotāpanna), Cūḷa-Subhaddā (who also attained stream-entry), and finally Sumana, who remained unmarried and attained the higher spiritual stage of a once-returner (sakadāgāmī).

The Disaster / Turning Point: Falling suddenly and severely ill on her deathbed, Sumana sent for her father. When Anāthapiṇḍika arrived and asked what was wrong, Sumana addressed her father as "younger brother" (malli). When he asked if she was talking in delirium or afraid of death, she replied that she was neither delirious nor afraid, and immediately passed away.

The Result / Resolution: Distressed that his virtuous daughter had seemingly died in mental confusion, Anāthapiṇḍika went weeping to the Buddha. The Buddha clarified that Sumana spoke with full awareness: she addressed him as "younger brother" because she was senior to him in terms of path-fruits (magga-phala), being a once-returner (sakadāgāmī) while he was a stream-enterer (sotāpanna). Revealing that she was reborn in the Tusita heaven, the Buddha stilled his grief and uttered Dhammapada verse 18 (Idha nandati pecca nandati...), affirming that the doer of merit rejoices both in this world and the next.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Imperishable Treasure of Merit (Puñña-Nidhāna):

Unlike material wealth—which is limited to this world and vulnerable to destruction by fire, water, kings, or thieves—merit functions as an unfailing follower-treasure (anugāmika-nidhāna) that accompanies a being like a shadow throughout the journey of saṁsāra.

2. The Ten Bases of Meritorious Action (Dasa Puñña-Kiriya-Vatthu):

The complete scope of merit accumulation encompasses ten actions: giving alms (dāna), moral virtue (sīla), meditation (bhāvanā), sharing merit (patti-dāna), rejoicing in others' merit (pattānumodanā), rendering service (veyyāvacca), paying reverence (apacāyana), preaching Dhamma (dhamma-desanā), listening to Dhamma (dhamma-savana), and straightening one's view (diṭṭhijjukamma). The full, proper execution of all ten is uniquely available during the period when a Buddha arises (buddhōtpāda-kāla).

3. Dynamic Functions of Kamma in Present Life:

Merit accumulated in this life interacts dynamically with past kamma: it can reinforce positive past birth-kamma as productive kamma (janaka-kamma), weaken unwholesome kamma as obstructive kamma (upapīḍaka-kamma), or completely nullify harmful forces as destructive kamma (upaghātaka-kamma).

4. Direction of Merit via Resolve (Praṇidhi / Adhiṭṭhāna):

Merit does not merely prolong saṁsāra; when combined with a firm resolution/aspiration (praṇidhi / adhiṭṭhāna), all performed wholesome actions (kusala-kamma) are pulled together as perfections (pāramī) toward noble goals—including Arahantship, Paccekabuddhahood, or Buddhahood—ultimately helping to end saṁsāric suffering (dukkha).

5. Twofold Classification of Right View (Sammādiṭṭhi):

Mundane Right View (sāsavā puññabhāgiyā upadhivepakkā): Accompanied by taints (āsava), yet inclined toward merit; provides resources of existence (upadhi) and accepts the ten-point view of kamma ownership (kammassakatā sammādiṭṭhi).

Supramundane Right View (ariyā anāsavā lokuttarā maggāṅgā): Noble, free from taints, and serving as a factor of the path based on the Four Noble Truths and dependent arising (paṭiccasamuppāda).

6. Transcending Extremes via the Middle Path (Majjhimā Paṭipadā):

The Buddha's path avoids both annihilationism (ucchēda-diṭṭhi / devotion to sensual pleasures, kāmasukhallikānuyoga) and eternalism (sassata-diṭṭhi / self-mortification, attakilamathānuyoga). It operates as a gradual training (anupubba-sikkhā), gradual action (anupubba-kiriyā), and gradual practice (anupubba-paṭipadā).

7. Crossing the Floods Without Halting or Straining (Oghataraṇa Sutta):

Crossing the four saṁsāric floods—sensual desire (kāmōgha), becoming (bhavōgha), views (diṭṭhōgha), and ignorance (avijjōgha)—requires a balanced approach. "Halting" (passively rejecting merit and succumbing to sensuality) causes one to sink, while "straining" (extreme self-mortification out of belief in a permanent soul) causes one to be swept away.

8. Clarification on 'Self' (Atta) and Moral Responsibility:

In texts like the Atta Vagga (e.g., attanā va kataṁ pāpaṁ...), the word atta denotes "oneself" as a locus of moral responsibility, not a permanent soul (attā). Because volitional preparations (saṅkhāra) arise out of ignorance (avijjā-paccayā saṅkhārā) with an implicit sense of self, one remains responsible for experiencing the fruits of kamma. Rejecting merit under the guise of anattā logic is an erroneous overrunning of intellect leading to a nihilistic view (nāstika-diṭṭhi).

9. The Highest Mode of Giving (Dāna):

Among the eight modes of giving alms, the supreme level is giving for the adornment and equipment of the mind (cittālaṅkāraṁ cittaparikkhāraṁ dānaṁ deti). This turns giving into a meditative practice of serenity (samatha) and insight (vipassanā), abandoning greed (alobha), hatred (adosa), and delusion (amoha), allowing one to glimpse Nibbāna through non-attachment.

10. The Hierarchy and Great Power of Meritorious Deeds:

Meritorious actions progress in noble value from external gifts (dāna), to taming body and speech (sīla), to direct mental development (bhāvanā). Citing his own past practice of loving-kindness meditation (mettā cetovimutti) for seven years, the Buddha exhorted, "Do not fear merits" (Mā, bhikkhave, puññānaṁ bhāyittha), defining merit as a true synonym for happiness (sukhassetaṁ adhivacanaṁ).
