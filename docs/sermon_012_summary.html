Sermon 012 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #12 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #12

Primary Theme: The unyielding, natural law of kamma (kamma-niyāma), the terrifying retributions (kamma-vipāka) born of evil deeds committed out of ignorance, and the ultimate transcendence of the wheel of kamma (kammacakka) through the wheel of Dhamma (dhammacakka).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Treasurer Sumangala and a nameless thief (during the time of Kassapa Buddha), whose fate as the python hungry ghost (Ajagara peta) was later observed by Ven. Mahā Moggallāna and explained by the Gautama Buddha.

The Interaction: Sumangala, having spent vast wealth paving the ground with golden bricks to build a perfumed chamber (gandhakuṭi) for Kassapa Buddha, saw a man sleeping near the city gate with muddy feet and casually remarked to his companions that the man must have spent the night wandering about.

The Disaster / Turning Point: The thief overheard the remark, took deep offence, and out of intense malice set fire to Sumangala’s fields seven times, cut the hamstrings of his cattle seven times, set fire to his mansion seven times, and ultimately burnt down Kassapa Buddha’s perfumed chamber. Sumangala felt no grief, but clapped joyfully because his store of merit remained indestructible in the Sāsana. He rebuilt the monastery at equal cost and, upon completing a seven-day alms offering, publicly shared the first portion of his merit with the unknown perpetrator.

The Result / Resolution: Struck with overwhelming spiritual urgency (saṃvega), the thief confessed, sought forgiveness, and offered himself into Sumangala's service. Although Sumangala fully forgave him, the natural order of kamma did not forgive him: after death, the thief suffered in Avīci hell for an aeon and was subsequently reborn as a 25-league-long hungry ghost in the shape of a python, engulfed in sweeping flames, until witnessed by Ven. Mahā Moggallāna descending Gijjhakūṭa mountain.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Inviolable Order of Kamma (Kamma-niyāma)

Kamma operates as a universal, natural law completely independent of personal belief, faith, or ignorance. Just as fire naturally burns anyone who touches it regardless of whether they are a child (bāla) or an adult, unwholesome actions bring severe suffering (vipāka) even if done in ignorance of their consequences.

2. True Definition of the Fool (Bāla)

In the Dhamma, a fool (bāla) is not defined by physical age but by conduct: one who engages in bad thoughts, bad speech, and bad deeds (duccintitacintī, dubbhāsitabhāsī, dukkatakammakārī). Unaware of the gravity of karmic retributions, the fool commits evil and is subsequently tormented by his own deeds like one burning in a forest fire (aggidaḍḍhova tappati).

3. The Law of Seeds (Bīja-niyāma) and Form-Matching Retribution

The retribution of a kamma frequently reflects the exact nature of the deed performed (yādisaṃ vapate bījaṃ – tādisaṃ harate phalaṃ). Because the thief set fire to fields, homes, and monasteries, his ultimate karmic retribution was to burn endlessly in Avīci hell and to exist as a hungry ghost engulfed in flames from head to tail.

4. Intention (Cetanā) as the Essence of Kamma

The Buddha explicitly defined kamma as intention: "Cetanāhaṃ, bhikkhave, kammaṃ vadāmi" (Intention, monks, I say is kamma). Physical actions alone do not create karmic responsibility; responsibility arises because the mind, under the influence of egoistic ignorance (avijjā), claims ownership ('I' and 'mine') over intentions carried out by body, speech, and mind.

5. Volitional Formations (Saṅkhāra) Driven by Ignorance

All unwholesome and wholesome actions are volitional preparations (saṅkhāra) rooted in ignorance (avijjā). Whether meritorious (puññābhisaṅkhāra), demeritorious (apuññābhisaṅkhāra), or imperturbable (āneñjābhisaṅkhāra), these mental formations bind the individual to saṃsāric suffering because of the self-referential delusion that holds oneself responsible for the deed, thereby mandating responsibility for the result.

6. The Unwholesome and Wholesome Roots (Hetu / Nidāna)

All evil actions stem from three unwholesome roots: greed (lobha), hatred (dosa), and delusion (moha). Conversely, wholesome actions arise from non-greed (alobha), non-hatred (adosa), and non-delusion (amoha). Sumangala exemplified non-hatred and non-greed when he responded to the destruction of his monastery with joy and forgiveness, while the thief acted entirely out of hatred and delusion.

7. Constant Reflection on Kamma as a Meditation Subject (Kammaṭṭhāna)

The Buddha taught that one must constantly reflect (abhiṇhaṃ paccavekkhitabbaṃ): "Kammasakohamhi kamma-dāyādo..." ("I am the owner of my kamma, heir to my kamma..."). This reflection is not a license to act casually, but an urgent warning of the terrifying responsibility one carries, aimed at instilling deep disenchantment (nibbidā) toward saṃsāric existence.

8. Virtue (Sīla) as Road Signs on the Saṃsāric Journey

The Five Precepts (pañca-sīla) and virtue centered on livelihood (ājīva-aṭṭhamaka-sīla) act like road signs warning a vehicle driver of dangerous cliffs. Ignoring these precepts causes one to veer off the path (apāya), take the wrong destination (duggati), plunge down an abyss (vinipāta), and be crushed beyond repair in hell (niraya).

9. Empirical Proofs of Kamma in Modern Times

The reality of past lives (jātissara-ñāṇa) and karmic retributions is validated not only by ancient texts but also by modern observations, childhood past-life memories, hypnotic regression, and documented cases such as Edgar Cayce—who accurately diagnosed illnesses (e.g., congenital blindness resulting from ancient acts of blinding enemy captives) rooted in unwholesome past-life kamma.

10. The Wheel of Dhamma (Dhammacakka) vs. The Wheel of Kamma (Kammacakka)

While all unawakened beings are trapped in the terrifying, endless cycle of the wheel of kamma, the Buddha established the wheel of Dhamma to break this cycle. Through the training of virtue (sīla), concentration (samādhi), and wisdom (pañca), one develops insight into impermanence (anicca), suffering (dukkha), and non-self (anattā).

11. Transcendence of Kamma through Stages of Awakening

Attaining Stream-entry (sotāpatti) eliminates the personality view (sakkāya-diṭṭhi), completely prevents the mind from committing deeds that lead to lower realms (apāya), and limits remaining saṃsāric births to a maximum of seven. Ultimately, Arahantship eradicates the generation of new kamma altogether, bringing final cessation in Nibbāna.
