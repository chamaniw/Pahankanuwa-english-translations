Sermon 058 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #58 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #58

Primary Theme: Eradicating craving for existence (bhava-taṇhā) and perverted views of permanence through the contemplation of feeling (vedanānupassanā), insight into conditioned constructs (saṅkhāra), and radical attention (yoniso manasikāra).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Venerable Uttara Thera (a young man born to a wealthy Brahmin family in Magadha who ordained under Ven. Sāriputta), Chief Minister Vassakāra, Ven. Sāriputta, and the Supreme Buddha.

The Interaction: Uttara rejected a marriage proposal from Chief Minister Vassakāra’s young daughter due to his mature spiritual wisdom. He instead entered the order under Ven. Sāriputta, serving as his close attendant.

The Disaster / Turning Point: While seeking medicinal alms food for the ill Ven. Sāriputta, a fleeing thief dropped a stolen bag of royal gems into Uttara’s alms bowl near a city pond. Minister Vassakāra, serving as judge and harbouring personal ill-will over the rejected proposal, summarily sentenced Uttara to be impaled on a stake without holding a trial.

The Result / Resolution: As Uttara was being impaled, the Buddha arrived, placed his hand on his head, and instructed him to endure the intense painful feeling (vedanā) through reflection on past kamma. Sitting on the stake, Uttara practiced contemplation of feeling (vedanānupassanā), subdued the pain, saw the danger in bhava and saṅkhāras, realized Arahantship with higher knowledges (abhiññā), levitated into the sky, and uttered two verses from the Aṅgulimālattheragāthā (Thag 121–122).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Grasping Creates Existence (Upādāna-paccayā bhavo): Existence (bhava) is not a permanent soul (attā) but a conditioned state dependent on grasping/holding (upādāna). Grasping the five aggregates (pañcupādānakkhandha) constructs the artificial illusion of a persistent "person" or self.

2. The Function of Preparations (Saṅkhāra): Saṅkhāra means "preparing" or "constructing" the conditioned (saṅkhataṃ abhisaṅkharonti). Ignorance (avijjā) leads to taking form, feeling, perception, preparations, and consciousness as "mine," establishing limits and artificial boundaries over natural phenomena.

3. The Conceit "I Am" (Asmimāna) and the Mirror Simile: Citing Ven. Puṇṇa Mantāniputta's instruction to Ven. Ānanda (upādāya asmi'ti hoti, no anupādāya), the sermon explains that the conceit "I am" arises only relative to grasping. Just as a person looks at their reflection in a mirror to adjust their appearance, consciousness (viññāṇa) acts as a reflective medium (paccavekkhaṇa); grasping what is reflected generates the notion of self-identity.

4. Three Characteristics of the Conditioned (Saṅkhata-lakkhaṇa): Every constructed thing exhibits three signs: arising (uppādo paññāyati), passing away (vayo paññāyati), and alteration while standing/persisting (ṭhitassa aññathattaṃ paññāyati). True insight (vipassanā) through the knowledge of arising and passing away (udayabbaya-ñāṇa) reveals constant alteration (aññathābhāva) moment-by-moment in the present, puncturing the illusion of self-identity.

5. Radical Attention (Yoniso Manasikāra) vs. Unwise Attention (Ayoniso Manasikāra): Illustrated by the simile of a dog crossing a footbridge deceiving itself over its reflection in the water. Unwise attention forgets the reflective nature of mind/consciousness and gets trapped in delusion. Radical attention (yoniso manasikāra) recognizes the process of reflection, turning attention back to the point of origin to dispel delusion.

6. Mutual Interdependence of Name-and-Form (Nāmarūpa) and Consciousness (Viññāṇa): Mind-objects (nāma: feeling, perception, contact, intention, attention) and material phenomena (rūpa: the four great elements mahābhūta) mutually support each other and draw attention to the reflective nature of consciousness. Liberation occurs when this mutual dependence is penetrated.

7. Contemplation of Feeling (Vedanānupassanā) as a Direct Path: Feeling (vedanā) is the primary spark that awakens self-perception (attā-saññā). By reflecting on feelings conditionally (tajjaṃ tajjaṃ paccayaṃ paṭicca tajjā tajjā vedanā uppajjanti...) rather than taking them personally, one sees them as transient like water bubbles (bubbuḷūpamā) and breaks the grip of sakkāya-diṭṭhi.

8. The Two Arrows: Worldling vs. Noble Disciple: The uninstructed worldling (assutavā puthujjana) hit by physical pain adds mental grief, experiencing two arrow strikes. This breeds the underlying tendency to aversion (paṭighānusaya) and, by chasing sensual pleasure as an escape, the underlying tendency to lust (rāgānusaya). The instructed noble disciple (sutavā ariyasāvaka) experiences only the physical arrow, reacting without mental conflict or aversion.

9. Ven. Sāriputta’s Insight on Physical Pain: Ven. Sāriputta noted that if someone strikes the body with a stick, the fault lies in the vulnerable nature of the body itself—which is inherently a boil, wound, and disease—rather than the person or the stick. Insight meditators view physical pain without personal clash, treating pain as a meditative opportunity.

10. Insight Realization of the Three Feeling Types:

Pleasant feelings must be seen as suffering due to their changeability (sukhā ṭhitibhāve, vipariṇāmadukkhā).

Painful feelings must be recognized directly as a stake or spear.

Neither-painful-nor-pleasant (neutral) feelings are peaceful yet impermanent (anicca). Insight requires stepping off the seesaw of pleasure and pain entirely.

11. The Four Distortions (Vipallāsa): The world is trapped in four perversities: regarding the impermanent (anicca) as permanent, suffering (dukkha) as pleasant, non-self (anattā) as self, and the foul (asubha) as beautiful. These distortions operate across three progressive levels: perception (saññā-vipallāsa), thought/mind (citta-vipallāsa), and unshakeable view (diṭṭhi-vipallāsa).

12. The World Within the Fathom-Long Carcass (Byāmamatte Kaḷevare): The Buddha declared that the world, its origin, its cessation, and the path to its cessation are located within this very fathom-long body endowed with perception (sasaññimhi) and mind (samanake). By replacing distorted perceptions with Dhamma-conforming perceptions (such as anicca-saññā, asubha-saññā, and ādīnava-saññā), one turns radical attention inward to attain the deathless Nibbāna.
