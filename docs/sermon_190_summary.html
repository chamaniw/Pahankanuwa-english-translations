Sermon 190 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #190 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #190 (Paṭicca Samuppāda Sermon No. 8)

Primary Theme: The radical opposition between the worldling’s point of view (which clings to the six sense-spheres as real and pleasant, fearing their cessation) and the Ariyan insight (which realizes the cessation of the six sense-spheres, saḷāyatananirodha, as the supreme, immediate bliss of Nibbāna).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The deity Rohitassa (a former psychic seer named Rohitassa, son of Bhoja), the Brahmin Uṇṇābha, and Venerable Ānanda acting as the primary exegete for the Buddha’s concise riddles.

The Interaction:

Deity Rohitassa approaches the Buddha at Jetavana to ask whether one can reach the end of the world—where there is no birth, decay, death, or rebirth—by traveling.

In a separate discourse analyzed in the sermon, Brahmin Uṇṇābha asks the Buddha about the mutual pastures (gocara) and ultimate resort (paṭisaraṇa) of the five physical sense faculties, the mind, mindfulness, deliverance, and Nibbāna.

The Disaster / Turning Point:

Rohitassa recounts his past life as a seer with miraculous speed (able to step from the eastern to western ocean) who walked continuously for 100 years trying to reach the end of the world, only to die on the way without reaching it.

Uṇṇābha pushes his line of questioning too far by asking, "What is the resort of Nibbāna?", revealing the worldling's structural error of seeking an objective existence beyond final release.

Ven. Nanananda illustrates the ultimate worldly plight through the folk tale of Gamarāla (the village elder) and his troupe of devil-dancers, who perform inside a giant gourd-shell blissfully unaware that it has been swallowed by a shark and taken to a market kitchen—symbolizing worldlings holding speculative "dances" entirely inside the gourd-shell of the six sense-spheres.

The Result / Resolution:

The Buddha resolves Rohitassa's dilemma by declaring that the world, the arising of the world, the cessation of the world, and the path to its cessation are all located within "this very fathom-long physical frame with its perceptions and mind."

The Buddha gently corrects Uṇṇābha, explaining that the holy life merges into Nibbāna as its ultimate goal and consummation. Uṇṇābha understands and attains the Fruits of the Path on the spot.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Dualistic Vision of Bliss (Dvayatānupassanā)

Worldlings (puthujjanā) regard the six sense-objects (rūpā, saddā, gandhā, rasā, phoṭṭhabbā, dhammā) as desirable and pleasant, viewing their cessation as woe. Conversely, the Noble Ones (ariyā) see the curb on self-hood bias (sakkāyassuparodhanaṁ) and the total cessation of these sense-fields as the highest bliss (sukhanti diṭṭhamariyehi).

2. Nibbāna Defined as *Saḷāyatananirodha*

Refuting the scholarly misconception of Nibbāna as a post-mortem, non-descript realm of existence ("Simple Simon / Siyadoris Nibbāna"), the sermon demonstrates via the Kāmaguṇa Sutta that Nibbāna is strictly the realization of the total cessation of the six sense-spheres (saḷāyatananirodha) right here and now.

3. Redefinition of "The World" (Loka) in Ariyan Discipline

As explained by Ven. Ānanda in response to the Buddha’s enigmatic utterances, "the world" in the Ariyan discipline refers to that by which one has perception of the world (lokasaññī) and conceit of the world (lokamānī)—namely, the six sense-faculties (eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind) and their respective objects.

4. The End of the World Within the Fathom-Long Body (Byāmamatta Kalevara)

The Rohitassa Sutta establishes that one cannot end suffering without reaching the end of the world, yet spatial travel cannot reach it. The four Noble Truths—the world (loka), its arising (samudaya), its cessation (nirodha), and the path (magga)—are contained entirely within the fathom-long physical frame (byāmamatte kaḷevare).

5. All Speculative Views Are Dependent on Contact (Tadapi Phassa Paccayā)

In the Brahmajāla Sutta, the Buddha dismantles all 62 speculative views of existence by applying the refutation tadapi phassa paccayā ("that too is dependent on contact"). Philosophers and scientists remain trapped within the "gourd-shell" of the senses because their theories rely entirely on sense-contact (phussa phussa paṭisaṁvedenti).

6. The Fivefold Insight into Sense-Spheres

True transcendence over all speculative views is achieved only when a monk understands the five aspects of the six sense-spheres as they really are: their arising (samudaya), their passing away (atthagama), their satisfaction (assāda), their peril (ādīnava), and the escape from them (nissaraṇa).

7. The Hierarchy of Sense Resort (Paṭisaraṇa)

In the Uṇṇābha Sutta, the Buddha maps the functional resort of experience: the five physical senses yield their pastures to the mind (mano); the mind’s resort is mindfulness (sati); mindfulness rests on deliverance (vimutti); and deliverance culminates in Nibbāna (Nibbānaṁ), which is the final consummation rather than an object to be further referenced.

8. Mind as Forerunner and the Illusion of Mind-Objects (Manomaya Dhammā)

Reversing the worldly assumption that objects precede mind (Dhammapubbaṅgamo mano), the Dhamma establishes that mind is the forerunner (Manopubbaṅgamā dhammā). Mind-objects (dhammā) are mind-made (manomaya), constructed via attention (manasikāra).

9. The Culprit of Non-Radical Attention (Ayoniso Manasikāra)

Using the courtroom metaphor, manasikāra (attention) acts as the prime culprit fabricating the illusion of external "things." Under insight practice, non-radical attention is converted into radical attention (yoniso manasikāra) within the Four Foundations of Mindfulness (satipaṭṭhāna), exposing the fabrication and causing the perception of mind-objects to fade (dhammasaññā ca virajjati).

10. The Unestablished, Objectless Sphere (Tadāyatana)

The much-debated Udāna passage describing tadāyatana does not refer to a physical after-death realm without sun or moon, but to the experiential state of nirodha where physical elements and immaterial spheres cease, characterized as unestablished (appatiṭṭhaṁ), non-continuing (appavattaṁ), and objectless (anārammaṇaṁ).

11. The Trap of the "Arising" Side (Samudaya-Pakka)

Beings swept by the current of becoming (bhavasotānusārihi) continuously jump from one arising to another (samudaya), dreading cessation. Deliverance is only attained by turning back toward total cessation—the stilling of all preparations (sabbasaṁkhārasamatho) and the relinquishment of all assets (sabbupadhipaṭinissaggo).
