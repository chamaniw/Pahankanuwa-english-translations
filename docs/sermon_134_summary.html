Sermon 134 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #134 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #134

Primary Theme: The ultimate goal of the Buddha’s Dispensation (Sāsana) is Nibbāna, achieved through progressive teaching (anupubbikathā) and detachment from material gains (lābhasakkāra); reviling or hindering this deep practice out of clannish stinginess (kula-macchariya) leads to self-destruction.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Elder Kāla (Kāla Thera) and a faithful, devoted female lay disciple (upāsikā) in Sāvatthi who treated him with maternal care.

The Interaction: The lay disciple regularly supported Elder Kāla with alms. Hearing neighbors praise the Buddha’s sweet Dhamma preaching, she repeatedly requested permission from Elder Kāla to go and listen to the Blessed One, but he constantly forbade her out of fear that her material support and devotion to him would diminish.

The Disaster / Turning Point: On an Uposatha day, the lay disciple left early to listen to the Buddha preach at Jetavana monastery. When Elder Kāla discovered this, jealousy and a burning sensation arose in him. He rushed to Jetavana, approached the Buddha while He was preaching to her, and rudely interrupted, insisting that the laywoman was too simple to understand deep Dhamma like the five aggregates (pañcakkhandha) and should only be taught basic almsgiving (dāna) and morality (sīla).

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha rebuked Elder Kāla as a "foolish monk" (dummedho) for obstructing the lay disciple's spiritual progress out of clannish stinginess (kula-macchariya). Uttering Dhammapada stanza 164, the Blessed One declared that anyone who reviles the Noble Dispensation due to evil views (pāpikā diṭṭhi) prospers only to bring about their own self-destruction—just as the bamboo, reed, or banana tree bears fruit only to die, or a she-mule conceives only to perish during birth.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Clannish Stinginess (Kula-Macchariya) and Evil Views (Pāpikā Diṭṭhi):

Monks who attempt to keep lay followers confined to basic merit-making out of fear of losing their material patronage suffer from kula-macchariya. Holding views that actively hinder practitioners from approaching Nibbāna constitutes an "evil view" (pāpikā diṭṭhi), which destroys the perpetrator's own spiritual life.

2. Five Factors of a Qualified Dhamma Preacher:

Referencing the discourse to Ven. Ānanda regarding Ven. Udāyī, the Buddha outlined five prerequisite factors for preaching Dhamma:

Delivering a gradual, step-by-step discourse (anupubbikathā).

Providing reasoned causes and fitting examples (pariyāyadassāvī).

Preaching out of genuine compassion and loving-kindness (anukampaṃ upādāya).

Preaching without expecting material gain, requisites, or offerings (na āmisantaro).

Preaching without self-exaltation (attukkaṃsana) or disparaging others (paravambhana), thus preserving all virtues (anupahacca attānañca parañca).

3. Pure vs. Impure Dhamma Preaching:

Drawing from the Kassapa Saṃyutta, pure preaching is motivated solely by veneration for the self-evident, timeless, and onward-leading qualities of the Dhamma (sandiṭṭhiko, akāliko, ehipassiko, opaneyyiko, paccattaṃ veditabbo viññūhi) and a compassionate wish for others to realize it. Preaching designed to elicit gifts and personal praise is impure.

4. Detachment in Lay Relations (Kulūpaga Monks):

A monk visiting lay households must remain completely unattached, moving like a hand waved through the air without getting caught. He must be equanimous (yathālābha-santuṭṭhi) regarding whether he receives alms or not, without demanding expectations regarding quantity, quality, speed, or deference.

5. The Dreadful Dangers of Gains, Offerings, and Fame (Lābhasakkārasiloka):

Material gains and praise are described as bitter, harsh, and hazardous to Nibbāna (dāruṇo, kaṭuko, pharuso, antarāyiko). The sermon details key canonical similes:

The Fisherman’s Hook: Gains act as Māra's bait to hook unmindful monks.

The Thorny Thicket: An unrestrained monk gets entangled in household attachment like a long-haired goat in thorns.

The Horsehair Rope: Seeking fame cuts through skin, flesh, tendons, and bone down to the marrow, destroying spiritual virtue.

Devadatta’s Downfall: Material prosperity given to a wicked person leads solely to self-ruin (attaghaññāya phallati).

6. Restraint by Wisdom (Ñāṇa-Saṃvara) and Use of the Requisites:

The four requisites (catupaccaya) are not the goal of ordination. Through proper reflection (paccayasannissita-sīla), a practitioner sees material goods merely as tools to sustain the body for crossing Saṃsāra. This insight is termed "the wisdom of seeing danger and seeing escape" (ādīnavadassāvī-nissaraṇa-paññā).

7. The Noble Lineage (Ariyavaṃsa Sutta):

The Buddha established four ancient, unblemished traditions for ascetics:

Contentment with any available robes (itarītara-cīvara-santuṭṭhi).

Contentment with any available alms-food (itarītara-piṇḍapāta-santuṭṭhi).

Contentment with any available lodging (itarītara-senāsana-santuṭṭhi).

Delighting in meditation (bhāvanārāmatā) and delighting in the abandonment of defilements (pahānārāmatā).

8. Structure and Purpose of the Gradual Discourse (Anupubbikathā):

Teaching must lead the mind progressively higher (uttaruttariṃ paṇītapaṇītaṃ): starting with giving (dāna) and morality (sīla), advancing to heavenly rewards (sagga), exposing the danger and baseness of sensual pleasures (kāmānaṃ ādīnavaṃ, okāraṃ saṅkilesaṃ), praising renunciation (nekkhamma), and culminating in the Four Noble Truths (caturāriya-sacca).

9. Refutation of World-Expansion (Lokavaḍḍhana):

The sermon highlights Dhammapada stanza 167 (na siyā lokavaḍḍhano), warning against expanding the suffering of Saṃsāra. Obstructing people from pursuing Nibbāna out of concern for maintaining worldly populations or social systems is likened to keeping patients permanently sick in a hospital just to keep the medical staff employed.

10. The Essence of Suffering (Dukkha-Sacca) and Nibbanic Liberation:

Suffering is summarized as the five aggregates of grasping (saṅkhittena pañcupādānakkhandhā dukkhā). Nibbāna is not a physical place or a conceptual element, but the immediate, personal realization (paccattaṃ) of the bliss of stilling (vūpasama-sukha) that arises when craving (taṇhā) and ignorance (avijjā) are uprooted.
