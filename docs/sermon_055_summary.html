Sermon 055 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #55 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #55 (Sinhala Audio Sermon No. 208)

Primary Theme: The doctrinal breakdown of the Ajita Māṇava Pucchā (Sutta Nipāta), charting the spiritual transition from the darkness of ignorance (avijjā) to the light of wisdom (vijjā); specifically detailing how mindfulness (sati) acts as the dam for the thirty-six streams of craving (taṇhā) while penetrative wisdom (paññā) completely shuts them off, culminating in non-manifestative consciousness (anidassana-viññāṇa) and Nibbāna.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The elderly Brahmin teacher Bāvarī (120 years old, master of the Vedas), residing at a hermitage on the banks of the Godāvarī River in Dakkhiṇāpatha (Southern India), and his chief disciple, the youth Ajita, along with fifteen other Brahmin disciples.

The Interaction: After utilizing village revenue granted by the King of Assaka to conduct a lavish sacrificial ritual, Bāvarī was approached by a deceitful visiting Brahmin who demanded five hundred coins (kahāpaṇas). Bāvarī explained that all his wealth earmarked for charity had been completely exhausted.

The Disaster / Turning Point: The deceitful Brahmin uttered a severe curse: if Bāvarī did not grant the money within seven days, his head would split into seven pieces. Terrified and overcome with grief, Bāvarī could neither eat nor enter meditative absorption (jhāna). A benevolent deity intervened, reassuring Bāvarī that the cheat knew nothing of "the head" or its "splitting," and revealed that only the newly arisen Fully Enlightened Buddha possessed this knowledge. Bāvarī then dispatched his sixteen disciples to locate the Buddha and test Him by asking questions mentally regarding Bāvarī's age, clan, physical marks, disciples, and the true meaning of the head and its splitting.

The Result / Resolution: Arriving at the Pāsāṇaka Shrine in Magadha, Ajita posed the questions in his mind. The Buddha answered each mentally asked question aloud, explicitly declaring: "Ignorance (avijjā) is the head; wisdom (vijjā) is the splitting of the head." Convinced of the Buddha's omniscience, Ajita then asked his famous canonical verses regarding the nature of the world, its sticky smear, and the damming of its streams. Upon hearing the Buddha’s answers, Ajita and the disciples attained Arahantship on the spot.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Head and the Splitting of the Head (Avijjā & Vijjā)

Ignorance (avijjā) is identified as the "head" that envelops all beings in darkness, while wisdom (vijjā)—supported by faith (saddhā), mindfulness (sati), concentration (samādhi), desire (chanda), and energy (vīriya)—is the "splitting of the head" (the complete destruction of ignorance).

2. The Definition of "The World" (Loko)

In the Noble Discipline (ariyassa vinaye), "the world" (loko) is not an objective external physical realm, but that which is perceived and conceived through the sixfold sense base (saḷāyatana): eye/forms, ear/sounds, nose/smells, tongue/tastes, body/tactile objects, and mind/mental phenomena. Where the six sense bases operate, there the designation of the world (lokapaññatti) exists.

3. The Simile of the Eggshell (Apagabbho)

All worldlings and scientists are trapped inside the "eggshell" of the sixfold sense base (saḷāyatana). The Buddha is termed apagabbha (devoid of embryonic growth/re-birth) and the "eldest in the world" (jeṭṭho) because He was the first being to break through this eggshell of ignorance through the cessation of the six sense bases (saḷāyatana-nirodha), attaining the world-transcending (lokuttara) state.

4. Craving as the Sticky Smear (Taṇhābhilepana / Jappābhilepana)

Craving (taṇhā) is described as a sticky pitch or smear (visattikā / lepa) that binds beings to the five cords of sensual pleasure (kāmaguṇa). This is illustrated by the monkey-trap simile, where a greedy monkey touches sticky pitch and becomes trapped by all four limbs and its mouth, rendering it helpless. Avijjā veils reality while taṇhā adheres beings to saṁsāric suffering (dukkha).

5. The 36 and 108 Streams of Craving (Sotā)

The streams (sotā) of craving flow outward through the six sense doors toward pleasant objects. When the six sense faculties are multiplied by the three types of craving—sensual craving (kāma-taṇhā), craving for becoming (bhava-taṇhā), and craving for non-becoming (vibhava-taṇhā)—they yield 18 streams, which double across internal and external domains to become 36 streams. Multiplied across the three periods of time (past, present, future), these expand into 108 flowing streams of craving.

6. Mindfulness as the Dam (Sati Nivāraṇaṁ)

Mindfulness (sati) serves as the essential dam (nivāraṇa) or restraint (saṁvara) that temporarily checks and prevents the outward flow of these streams of craving. This explains why the Buddha designates the Four Establishments of Mindfulness (cattāro satipaṭṭhāna) as the direct/sole path (ekāyana maggo).

7. Mindfulness as the Resort of the Mind (Sati-Paṭisaraṇa)

Referencing the Uṇṇābha Brahmin Sutta, the five physical sense faculties have distinct domains, but all their resort (paṭisaraṇa) gathers in the mind (manas). The resort of the mind is mindfulness (sati), the resort of mindfulness is liberation (vimutti), and the resort of liberation is Nibbāna. Here, sati acts as a protective gatekeeper (dvārapāla) at the doors of the faculties (indriyesu guttadvāratā).

8. Wisdom as the Ultimate Closure (Paññāy'ete Pidhiyyare)

While mindfulness (sati) dams the streams of craving temporarily, only penetrative wisdom (paññā) completely shuts them off (pidhiyyare) and eradicates the underlying influxes (āsavakkhaya). True wisdom is noble and penetrative (ariyā nibbedhikā), seeing the immediate rise and fall (udayatthagāminī) of all prepared phenomena (saṅkhāra).

9. The Supreme Splendor of Wisdom (Paññāpabhā)

Among the four splendors—the sun, moon, fire, and wisdom—the splendor of wisdom (paññāpabhā) is supreme. The internal light of wisdom renders all external light-giving celestial bodies lusterless. In the state of unmanifestative consciousness (anidassana-viññāṇa), external physical elements (mahābhūta) find no footing, and conventional light sources cease to shine.

10. Radical Attention (Yoniso Manasikāra) as the Internal Witness

The perceptual conflict between consciousness (viññāṇa) and Name-and-Form (nāmarūpa) is likened to a court case without a witness. By converting improper attention (ayonisomanasikāra) into radical attention (yoniso manasikāra), attention is made an internal witness that reveals the impermanence (aniccatā), suffering (dukkha), and unsubstantiality (anattā) of all Name-and-Form preparations (nāmarūpa-saṅkhāra).

11. Non-Manifestative Consciousness (Anidassana-Viññāṇa)

When prepared consciousness (saṅkhata-viññāṇa) dependent on Name-and-Form ceases (nāmarūpa-nirodha), there emerges non-manifestative consciousness (viññāṇaṁ anidassanaṁ)—a state that is infinite (anantaṁ) and radiant all-round (sabbato pabhaṁ). In this state, Name-and-Form ceases without remainder (asesaṁ uparujjhati).

12. The World and Its Cessation Within the Fathom-Long Body

The Buddha’s definitive lion's roar states that the world (loko), the origin of the world (lokasamudaya), the cessation of the world (lokanirodha), and the path to its cessation (lokanirodhagāminī paṭipadā) are declared within this very fathom-long carcass (byāmamatte kaḷevare) endowed with perception and mind. Liberation is the present-life realization (sandiṭṭhiko) of the cessation of existence (bhava-nirodho nibbānaṁ), marked by the stilling of all preparations (sabbasaṅkhārasamatho).
