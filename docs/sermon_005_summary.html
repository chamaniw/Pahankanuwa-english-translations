Sermon 005 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #5 Summary

Sermon #5 Summary

---

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #5

Primary Theme: The spiritual symbiosis between the clergy and laity, the four levels of consuming requisites (paribhoga), the true nature of monastic popularity exemplified by Venerable Mahā Kassapa, and transforming cultural pauses (nonakatha) into internal mental renewal through insight meditation (vipassanā).

---

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The Supreme Buddha, Venerable Mahā Kassapa, a retinue of 500 monks, and a group of 500 children from Rājagaha carrying baskets of oil cakes (kāvum).

The Interaction: On a festival morning in Rājagaha, the Buddha and 500 monks met 500 children carrying baskets of oil cakes to a park. The children paid homage to the Buddha and Saṅgha but bypassed them without offering cakes. Walking behind the main group was Ven. Mahā Kassapa, practicing solitary composure. Upon seeing him, the children felt spontaneous faith, prostrated, and offered all their baskets of cakes to him. Ven. Mahā Kassapa instructed them to take the cakes to the Buddha and the Saṅgha.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Seeing the children offer all the food to Ven. Mahā Kassapa, some monks whispered critically, claiming the children distributed alms based on personal favoritism rather than offering first to the Buddha and chief disciples. The Buddha corrected the monks, explaining that Ven. Mahā Kassapa was the true "owner" of the oil cakes because his pristine virtue made him beloved to both humans and devas.

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha uttered Verse 217 of the Dhammapada (Sīladassanasampannaṃ...), praising the qualities that make a monk dear to the world. Upon hearing the verse and understanding the supramundane value of their sacrifice, all 500 children realized the Fruit of Stream-entry (sotāpatti-phala).

---

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Practice (*Paṭipatti*) as the Life of the Dispensation:

Without the systematic practice of virtue, concentration, and wisdom (sīla, samādhi, paññā), realization (paṭivedha)—the direct experience of the Paths and Fruits—cannot be attained. The relationship between laity and clergy exists fundamentally to foster supramundane growth.

2. The Fourfold Purification of Virtue (*Catupārisuddhi-Sīla*):

Pātimokkha-saṃvara-sīla: Restraint according to the monastic disciplinary code.

Indriya-saṃvara-sīla: Guarding the sense faculties against defilements.

Ājīva-pārisuddhi-sīla: Purity of livelihood, acquiring requisites righteously.

Paccaya-sannissita-sīla: Utilizing requisites through wise reflection (paccavekkhaṇa), transforming usage into meditation.

3. Analysis of Dhammapada Verse 217:

Sīla-dassana-sampannaṃ: Fully endowed with pristine virtue and Right View (sammā-diṭṭhi).

Dhammaṭṭhaṃ: Firmly established in the supramundane Dhamma.

Saccavedinaṃ: Directly comprehending the Four Noble Truths.

Attano kamma kubbānaṃ: Doing one's "own work" (fulfillment of sīla, samādhi, paññā).

Taṃ jano kurute piyaṃ: Such a monk is loved by gods and humans alike.

4. Monastic Seclusion and the Similes for Ven. Mahā Kassapa:

The Moon Simile (Candūpamā): Like the moon that is always "new" (nicca-navakā) and unobtrusive (appagabbho), a monk interacts with lay households without excessive familiarity or intimacy.

The Abandoned Well Simile (Jarūdapāna): Walking among laypeople like someone looking down into an old, dry well—with complete composure, detachment, and balance of mind.

5. The Four Modes of Utilizing the Four Requisites (*Paribhoga*):

Theyya-paribhoga (Use by Theft): Utilizing requisites without virtue (sīla); the user becomes a heavy debtor in saṃsāra.

Iṇa-paribhoga (Use as a Debt): Utilizing requisites with reflection (paccavekkhaṇa) and paying "interest" by meditating on the four elements (dhātu-manasikāra) and repulsiveness (asubha-manasikāra).

Dāyajja-paribhoga (Use as an Inheritance): Utilization by trainees (sekha—Stream-enterers, Once-returners, Non-returners) who act as true children of the Buddha.

Sāmi-paribhoga (Use as an Owner): Utilization by Arahants who are entirely debt-free (anaṇā) and act out of compassion for the world (lokānukampā).

6. The Threefold Excellence of an Almsgiving (*Sampatti*):

An offering yields great fruit and benefit (mahapphala mahānisaṃsa) when three conditions are met:

Dāyaka-sampatti: Moral purity, faith (saddhā), and generosity of the giver.

Deyyadhamma-sampatti: Righteousness and care with which the gift was acquired and prepared.

Paṭigāhaka-sampatti: Spiritual purity and attainment of the recipient.

7. Transmuting Cultural Customs (*Nonakatha*) into Spiritual Audits:

The "non-astrological time" (nonakatha) during New Year celebrations should not be spent in heedless worldly amusement. Instead, it must be used as a "meritorious period" (puṇya-kālaya)—a deliberate pause to audit past physical, verbal, and mental conduct, clear mental "rust," and realign life toward Nibbāna.

8. Right Livelihood (*Sammā-Ājīva*) and Individual Responsibility (*Kammassakatā*):

A warning against resorting to wrong livelihood (micchā-ājīva) for quick financial gain. Beings are the owners and heirs of their actions (kammassakā... kammadāyādā). Since everyone is born alone and dies alone, one must take full responsibility for one's kamma.

9. The Fallacy of "Accidents" and the Law of *Kamma-Vipāka*:

There are no true "accidents" in nature. Seemingly sudden tragedies or unexpected events are the natural unfolding of past causes and conditions (kamma-vipāka). The Buddhas view these events with quiet understanding, knowing they arise from long-forgotten unwholesome deeds.

10. Practical Insight Meditation (*Vipassanā*) in Daily Life:

Spiritual potentials from past lives remain stored in the consciousness continuum (viññāṇa). Lay practitioners can activate these latent potentials by contemplating the three characteristics (tilakkhaṇa)—impermanence (anicca), suffering (dukkha), and non-self (anattā)—through ordinary household activities and the decay of material possessions.
