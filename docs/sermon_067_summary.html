Sermon 067 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #67 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #67 (Sinhala Audio Sermon No. 71)

Primary Theme: The contrasting behaviors of the virtuous (satpuruṣa) and unvirtuous (asatpuruṣa) during adversity; the conquest of the fear of death through the total eradication of craving (taṇhā) and self-illusion (sakkāya-diṭṭhi); and the practical deconstruction of the five aggregates (pañcakkhandha) through insight into non-self (anattā).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Novice (sāmaṇera) Saṅkicca—a child born under miraculous circumstances who survived his mother's funeral pyre as an infant due to being a pacchimabhavika (one in their final birth). Named after an eye injury caused by a cremation hook (saṅku), he ordained at age seven under Ven. Sāriputta and attained Arahanthood while his head was being shaved. The narrative also involves thirty forest monks and a chief of thieves leading a band of 500 outlaws.

The Interaction: Thirty elder forest monks go to spend the rainy season retreat (vassa) in a remote village, bringing the seven-year-old Arahant novice Saṅkicca with them at Ven. Sāriputta's behest. During their stay, a destitute man is given food by the monks and stays as their attendant (kappiyakāra).

The Disaster / Turning Point: After two months, the poor attendant leaves unannounced and is captured by 500 thieves seeking a human sacrifice for a forest deity. To save his own life, the unvirtuous man betrayingly directs the thieves to sacrifice one of the noble monks instead. When the thieves demand a monk, all thirty monks sequentially volunteer to sacrifice their lives for one another out of supreme virtue. Finally, Novice Saṅkicca steps forward, revealing he was sent precisely for this occasion. As the thief chief repeatedly swings his sword at the meditating novice, the blade miraculously bends and curls up like a dry palmyra leaf due to Saṅkicca's virtue and jhānic power.

The Result / Resolution: Astounded by the miracle and Saṅkicca's total lack of fear, the chief of thieves prostrates himself and requests guidance. Saṅkicca preaches the Dhamma on how an Arahant fears no death. Inspired, all 500 thieves request ordination. Saṅkicca ordains them on the spot, brings them back to reassure the thirty monks, and leads them to Ven. Sāriputta and the Buddha. Upon hearing the Buddha recite Dhammapada verse 110, all 500 newly ordained monks attain Arahanthood.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Adversity as the Touchstone of Virtue (Satpuruṣa vs. Asatpuruṣa): Adversity brings out an individual's true inner nature. The unvirtuous person (asatpuruṣa) willingly sacrifices his benefactors to save himself, as demonstrated by the poor attendant. Conversely, the noble person (satpuruṣa) willingly sacrifices his own life for the sake of others, as shown by the thirty monks and Novice Saṅkicca.

2. The Inviolable Law of the Final Existence (Pacchimabhavika-Dhammatā): A person possessing the merit to attain Arahanthood in their current life (pacchimabhavika) cannot pass away before realizing that goal. This natural lawfulness (dhammatā) explains how infant Saṅkicca survived unburned inside his deceased mother's womb atop a funeral pyre.

3. Immediate Awakening via the Pentad of Skin (Taca-Pañcaka): The contemplation of the five basic body parts—hair of the head (kesā), hair of the body (lomā), nails (nakhā), teeth (dantā), and skin (taco)—is a powerful meditation subject. By reflecting on these standard components of bodily foulness (asubha) while his head was being shaved, seven-year-old Saṅkicca developed deep insight (vipassanā-ñāṇa) and attained Arahanthood alongside the four analytical knowledges (paṭisambhidā).

4. Conquest of the Fear of Death (Abhaya): Fear of death is rooted in craving (taṇhā), ignorance (avijjā), and expectations. An Arahant (a khīṇāsava) experiences no mental suffering (dukkha) regarding death because all fetters (saṁyojana) and attachments to existence have been severed. For an Arahant, death is simply laying down a heavy burden (ohitabhāra).

5. The Five Aggregates as Accumulated Heaps (Pañcakkhandha / Upadhi): The five aggregates—form (rūpa), feeling (vedanā), perception (saññā), preparations/volitions (saṅkhāra), and consciousness (viññāṇa)—are not solid, singular structures. Khandha explicitly means a "heap" or "pile." Worldly life is a continuous, sorrowful effort to avoid dying by incessantly accumulating these dynamic, fleeting processes.

6. The Triple Mechanism of Self-Piling (Etaṁ Mama, Eso Hamasmi, Eso Me Attā): Ignorant worldlings continually pile up saṁsāric existence across the six sense-bases (āyatana) using three conceits:

Craving (taṇhā): "Etaṁ mama" ("This is mine")

Conceit (māna): "Eso hamasmi" ("This I am")

Dogma/View (diṭṭhi): "Eso me attā" ("This is my self")

This process creates the illusion of compactness (ghana-saññā) and reinforces the dogma of self-illusion (sakkāya-diṭṭhi).

7. The Deconstructive Vipassanā Formula (Na Etaṁ Mama...): Insight practice systematically unravels the compact self-illusion by applying wise attention (yoniso manasikāra) to all mental and physical experiences:

"Na etaṁ mama" ("This is not mine")

"Na eso hamasmi" ("This I am not")

"Na eso me attā" ("This is not my self")

This direct analytical vision dismantles the grasping (upādāna) that fuels becoming (bhava).

8. The Simile of the Bag Open at Both Ends: The Buddha compares the body to a bag open at both ends (found in the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta). Ordinary perception treats the body as a single, continuous vessel, but insight reveals it to be a dynamic throughput system of rapidly arising and ceasing elements (e.g., inbreath/outbreath, ingestion/excretion) without any abiding core entity.

9. "Dying While Still Alive" (Liberation of Mind / Arahatta-Phala): True Nibbāna is experienced in this very life (diṭṭhadhamme) when the mind completely detaches from and surrenders all five aggregates. When physical death ultimately arrives, an Arahant naturally enters the attainment of the fruit (phala-samāpatti), having already abandoned all acquisitions (upadhi).

10. The Function of Emptiness (Suññatā) and the Four Elements (Cattāro Mahābhūtā): What worldlings take to be a personal self is merely an unstable play of the four primary elements: earth (pathavī), water (āpo), fire (tejo), and air (vāyo). Seeing the essential emptiness (suññatā) and meaninglessness of constant bodily maintenance frees the practitioner from saṁsāric anxiety.

11. Cultivation of the Five Spiritual Faculties (Indriya / Bala): Progress toward liberation requires the active development of five key mental faculties: faith (saddhā), energy (viriya), mindfulness (sati), concentration (samādhi), and wisdom (paññā). These faculties act as spiritual powers that enable one to penetrate the Four Noble Truths and realize Nibbāna.
