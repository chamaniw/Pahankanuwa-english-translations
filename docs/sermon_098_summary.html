Pahankanuwa Sermon #98 Summary

Sermon #98 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #98

Primary Theme: The hidden, mass of suffering (dukkha) underlying lay happiness versus the sublime, peaceful happiness of renunciation (nekkhamma-sukha) and monastic seclusion, culminating in Nibbāna through the Noble Eightfold Path.

---

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: A Vajjiputtaka monk (a former prince of the Vajjian confederacy who had renounced his turn at kingship to ordain).

The Interaction: Residing in a forest grove near the Gabled Hall in the Great Wood at Vesālī, devoted to night-time walking meditation (caṅkamana).

The Disaster / Turning Point: While walking back and forth, he heard the music, singing, dancing, and drumming of an annual city festival. Remembering his past royal luxuries, sadness overcame him, and he lamented: "We live alone in this forest like a piece of wood cast away in the jungle. On a night like this, who is more unfortunate than us?" A forest deity, seeking to arouse spiritual urgency (saṁvega), admonished him by reversing the sentiment: "Indeed, you live alone... Yet, laypeople look up to you with great respect... just as those destined for hell look longingly up to those who have gone to heaven."

The Result / Resolution: The monk’s lingering attachment to lay life vanished. The following day, when he visited the Buddha, the Blessed One uttered Dhammapada Stanza 302 (“Duppabbajjaṁ durabhiramaṁ...”) to permanently eradicate any remaining subtle attachment to household pleasures.

---

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Exegesis of Dhammapada 302:

Duppabbajjaṁ: Renunciation/Going forth is difficult due to the complex web of domestic and social attachments.

Durabhiramaṁ: Finding delight (abhirati) in the ascetic life is hard due to deeply ingrained lay habits; failure to do so leads to discontent (anabhirati) and weariness (ukaṭalī).

Durāvāsā gharā dukhā: Household life is a heavy mountain of suffering (dukkha), hard to manage and impossible to fully satisfy.

Dukkho 'samānasaṁvāso (or asamanasaṁvāso): Living among discordant, competitive, and incompatible people in society is inherently stressful.

Dukkhānupatito addhagū / Na caddhagū siyā: Wandering through saṁsāra means dragging a weight of kamma-driven suffering behind oneself; one must cease being such a traveler.

2. The Triad of Ascetic Happiness:

Happiness of Renunciation (Nekkhamma-sukha): The relief of breaking lay entanglements, comparable to being released from prison.

Happiness of Seclusion (Paviveka-sukha): The tranquility of dwelling alone in nature/forests, overcoming fear through loving-kindness (mettā) and mindfulness of death (maraṇasati).

Happiness of Peace (Upasama-sukha): The supreme bliss derived from the complete stilling and eradication of lust (rāga), hatred (dosa), and delusion (moha).

3. Internal Defilements vs. External Comfort (Hatthaka Āḷavaka Sutta):

The Buddha, sleeping on a thin bed of aṭṭēriya leaves on rough cattle ground during freezing winter frost, declared he slept in total comfort.

A householder living in a luxurious, heated mansion with soft couches and pleasing attendants sleeps in discomfort if tormented by the internal fires of lust, hatred, and delusion. True comfort belongs to the Tathāgata, who has cut off all defilements at the root.

4. Freedom from Anxiety and Fear (Story of Ven. Bhaddiya):

Ven. Bhaddiya constantly exclaimed "Aho sukhaṁ!" ("Oh, what happiness!") in the forest.

As a king, despite heavy internal/external security forces, he lived trapped in constant fear and terror. As a secluded monk, he lived completely fearless, peaceful, and unburdened like a wild deer.

5. Simile of the Peacock and the Swan (*Muni Sutta*):

The layperson is compared to the blue-necked peacock: outwardly beautiful with its train of feathers, yet unable to fly swiftly or far due to the immense physical weight it drags behind.

The forest-dwelling sage (muni) is compared to the swan: unattached, light, and capable of taking flight from one lake to another at a moment's notice without encumbrance.

6. The Mass of Lay Suffering (*Mahādukkhakkhandha Sutta*):

The danger (ādīnava) of sensual pleasures (kāma) involves severe physical toil (enduring heat, cold, hunger), anxiety when labor yields no profit, constant fear of wealth being confiscated or destroyed, and the grief of loss (yampi me ahu tampi me natthi—"what was mine is no longer mine").

Wealth and sensual attachment inevitably breed disputes, violence, domestic conflict, and warfare.

7. The True Concept of the Noble Uposatha (*Ariya-uposatha*):

Observing the Eight Precepts is not merely avoiding wrong deeds; it is a temporary practice of living like the Arahants (arahantappavedita).

Refraining from high beds, unseemly entertainment, cosmetics, and untimely food trains the mind to break saṁsāric attachments, serving as a stepping stone to full renunciation.

8. Etymology and Intent of Going Forth (*Pabbajjā*):

Derived from the root concept of escaping an enclosure/cowpen (vaja/braja), like cattle bursting out of a stable at dawn seeking freedom.

Illustrated by King Mahākappina, who abandoned his kingdom the moment he heard "A Buddha has arisen," prompting his ministers and subjects to go forth in imitation (pabbajitaṁ anupabbajiṁsu). Returning to lay life after ordaining is likened in ancient texts to eating one's own vomit.

9. The Universal Reach of Dukkha and the Lion's Roar:

True happiness does not exist anywhere in saṁsāra, even in the Brahma realms.

When the Buddha proclaims the Three Characteristics—impermanence (anicca), suffering (dukkha), and non-self (anattā)—even high Brahma gods are struck with spiritual urgency (saṁvega) and terror, realizing their subtle existence is impermanent, akin to wild animals scattering at the lion's roar.

10. Systematic Development of the Noble Eightfold Path:

Liberation cannot be achieved merely by wishing (na kho pana etaṁ icchāya pattabbaṁ); it requires practical cultivation (paṭipadā).

Begins with Right View (sammā-diṭṭhi)—from basic karma comprehension to deep insight into the Four Noble Truths and Five Aggregates—progressing through virtue (sīla), concentration (samādhi), and wisdom (paññā), culminating in Right Knowledge (sammā-ñāṇa) and Right Liberation (sammā-vimutti).
