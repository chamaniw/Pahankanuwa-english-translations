Sermon 119 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #119 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #119

Primary Theme: Reframing worldly labor through the metaphor of supramundane spiritual cultivation (amata-phala kasī); contrasting the repetitive, impermanent nature of saṃsāric endeavor (punappunaṃ) with the internal purification of the mind-stream (citta-santāna) via the Noble Eightfold Path.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The Supreme Buddha and the farming brahmin Kasībhāradvāja (a member of the priestly caste engaged in agricultural production).

The Interaction: While residing in the brahmin village of Ekanālaka in Magadha, the Buddha stands quietly for alms near Kasībhāradvāja, who is supervising a massive sowing festival involving five hundred yoked plows.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Seeing the Buddha standing with an empty bowl, Kasībhāradvāja critically challenges him, viewing monastic life as idle dependency: "Monk, I plow and I sow. Having plowed and sown, I eat. You too should plow and sow; having plowed and sown, eat." When the Buddha responds that he indeed plows and sows, the brahmin demands to see his agricultural implements—his oxen, plow, goad, and seed.

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha delivers the verses of the Kasībhāradvāja Sutta, mapping every element of farming onto the spiritual virtues of the Noble Eightfold Path. Inspired, the brahmin offers a golden bowl of milk-rice. The Buddha rejects it on the grounds of pure livelihood (ājīva-pārisuddhi), as it was offered in response to verse recitation (gāthābhīgītaṃ). When the rejected food is thrown into sterile water, it emits steam and hisses dramatically ("ciṭi-ciṭi"), striking Kasībhāradvāja with deep spiritual urgency (saṃvega). He takes refuge, requests ordination (pabbajjā and upasampadā), and before long attains full liberation as an Arahant.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Mundane vs. Supramundane Farming (Kasī):

Worldly agriculture produces perishable crops to temporarily relieve physical hunger. Spiritual farming in the Buddha’s Dispensation (sāsana) cultivates internal virtues within the mind-stream to yield the harvest of the Deathless (amataphala / Nibbāna), liberating one permanently from the totality of saṃsāric suffering.

2. The Symbology of Spiritual Farming Implements (Kasībhāradvāja Sutta Breakdown):

The Buddha maps the farmer’s tools directly to foundational spiritual faculties:

Seed-Paddy (Bījaṃ): Faith (saddhā)—the necessary germinating factor without which spiritual practice is merely "hollow grain."

Rainwater (Vuṭṭhi): Austerity/Sense-restraint (tapas / indriya-saṃvara)—the moistening factor that nourishes the seed of faith.

Yoke & Plow (Yuganāṅgalaṃ): Wisdom (paññā)—the forerunner (pubbaṅgamā) that digs deep to uproot the roots of defilements (kilesa).

Plow-pole (Īsā): Moral shame (hiri)—the guiding shaft grounded in shrinking away from wrong.

Yoke-strap (Yottaṃ): Mind (mano)—the central tie binding all spiritual faculties together.

Plowshare & Goad (Phālapācanaṃ): Mindfulness (sati)—the double-functioned faculty that protects the plow and directs the oxen along the correct path (ārakkha-sārathī).

Fence of the Field: Physical restraint, verbal restraint, and moderation in eating (kāyagutto vacīgutto āhāre udare yato).

Weeding (Niddānaṃ): Truthfulness (saccaṃ)—uprooting the weeds of falsehood, wrong views (micchā-diṭṭhi), and empty doctrines.

Unyoking the Oxen (Pamocanaṃ): Gentleness/Docility (soracca)—the supreme discipline perfected in Arahantship.

Draft-Ox (Dhuradhorayhaṃ): Energy (viriya)—balanced effort (viriya-samatā) that pulls evenly without falling into restlessness (uddhacca) or laziness (kosajja).

3. The Un-Turning Plow toward Nibbāna (Anivattantaṃ):

Unlike a conventional plow that reaches the edge of a field and turns back, the spiritual plow moves strictly forward without turning back (gacchati anivattantaṃ), drawing the practitioner straight to security from bondage (yogakkhema)—a direct epithet for Nibbāna where sorrow ceases (yattha gantvā na socare).

4. Nature of the Saṅgha: Spiritual Practice vs. Priestly Ritual:

Brahminic society was a priestly caste relying on external rituals, sacrifices, and professional agriculture. In contrast, the Buddha’s Saṅgha is defined entirely by inner practice (paṭipadā): supaṭipanno (entered well), ujupaṭipanno (entered straight), ñāyapaṭipanno (entered methodically), and sāmīcipaṭipanno (entered properly). They are worthy of offerings (āhuneyyo, dakkhiṇeyyo) not due to ritualistic performance, but solely due to their wealth of virtue (sīla-sampadā).

5. Purity of Monastic Livelihood (Ājīva-Pārisuddhi):

The Buddha’s refusal of the milk-rice highlights the strict standard of pure livelihood. Food acquired as a direct reward or trade-off for preaching Dhamma or displaying verses (gāthābhīgītaṃ) is unsuitable for an Enlightened One.

6. The Mind-Stream (Citta-Santāna) as the True Field of the Dispensation (Sasun Keta):

The "field" of the Buddha's Dispensation has no physical, external location; it is the individual mind-stream. Real practice consists of internal reorganization—abstaining from evil, cultivating the wholesome, and purifying one's own mind (sacittapariyodapanaṃ), transforming the mind-stream into an unsurpassed field of merit for the world (anuttaraṃ puññakkhettaṃ lokassa).

7. The Four Similes of Mental Conversion:

The standard text of conversion illustrates the radical shift from delusion to Right View (sammā-diṭṭhi):

Uprighting what was overturned: Reversing conventional, upside-down worldly values.

Revealing what was hidden: Exposing the hidden supramundane truths of virtue.

Guiding the lost: Pointing out the correct path out of saṃsāric confusion.

Holding up a lamp in the dark: Shedding light so those with "eyes" (wisdom) can clearly see reality.

8. The Endless Repetition of Saṃsāra (Punappunaṃ):

Citing a secondary dialogue with Udaya Brahmin, Ven. Nanananda highlights how all worldly activities (sowing, rain, milking, begging, birth, entering the womb, dying) are repeated endlessly (punappunaṃ). Saṃsāra is an exhausting, unceasing cycle of preparing and repairing conditioned formations (saṅkhāra). The Dhamma offers the only exit from this endless repetition.

9. Noble Suffering (Ariya-Dukkha) vs. Ignoble Suffering (Anariya-Dukkha):

Ignoble suffering is the partial, worldly view of pain as opposed to pleasure. Noble suffering, as revealed in the Four Noble Truths (cattāri ariya-saccāni), recognizes that the five aggregates of grasping (pañcupādānakkhandhā) are themselves inherent suffering (dukkha) because they are impermanent, fabricated heaps of preparations.

10. Deconstruction of the Five Aggregates (Pañcupādānakkhandhā):

Form (rūpa), feeling (vedanā), perception (saññā), preparations (saṅkhāra), and consciousness (viññāṇa) are empty of a core self or soul (anattā). Consciousness in particular changes so rapidly (lahuparivatta) that no fit physical analogy can depict its instability.

11. Internal Reorganization via the Noble Eightfold Path:

The Eightfold Path is a complete, systematic restructuring of the mind-stream. Driven by Right View (sammā-diṭṭhi) and guided by Right Intention (sammā-saṅkappa—renunciation, non-ill-will, non-cruelty), it refines conduct and culminates in pure concentration (sammā-samādhi) and deep insight (vipassanā).

12. Shifting Focus from Arising (Samudaya-Pakkha) to Cessation (Nirodha-Pakkha):

Unenlightened beings are trapped on the side of arising (samudaya), constantly hoping for a better future within saṃsāra. The noble disciple directs insight wisdom toward the nature of cessation (nirodha-pakkha), realizing yaṃ kiñci samudayadhammaṃ sabbaṃ taṃ nirodhadhammaṃ ("Whatever is of the nature to arise, all that is of the nature to cease"), which leads directly to Stream-entry (sotāpatti) and ultimate liberation.
