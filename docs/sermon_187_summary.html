Sermon 187 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #187 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #187 (Paticca Samuppada Sermon 5)

Primary Theme: The role of linguistic conventions (akkheyya, saṅkhā, vohāra) and perception (saññā) in perpetuating the Samsāric vortex (vaṭṭa), and how the Law of Dependent Arising (Paṭicca Samuppāda) enables the transcendence of dualistic logic (is vs. is not) leading to the cessation of existence (bhavanirodha).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Venerable Samiddhi (a newly ordained monk) and a radiant deity (yakkha).

The Interaction: At dawn in the Tapoda monastery at Rajagaha, Ven. Samiddhi bathes in the hot springs and stands drying himself in a single robe. An exceedingly beautiful deity illuminates the hot springs, appears in the air, and urges the young monk to enjoy the fivefold sense pleasures during his youth rather than living a monk's life dependent on alms.

The Disaster / Turning Point: The deity attempts to tempt Samiddhi into abandoning what is directly visible (sandiṭṭhika) in favor of what takes time (kālika, i.e., enjoying youth before old age). Ven. Samiddhi decisively refutes the deity, pointing out that sensual pleasures are actually time-bound, full of suffering, and despair, whereas the Dhamma is directly visible (sandiṭṭhika), immediate (akālika), and to be experienced by the wise. Being newly ordained, Samiddhi refers the deity to the Buddha. The deity asks Samiddhi to pose the question to the Buddha on his behalf because the Buddha is surrounded by high-ranking deities.

The Result / Resolution: Ven. Samiddhi approaches the Buddha and relates the entire exchange. In response, the Buddha delivers deep verses explaining how beings are trapped by what can be named (akkheyya) and bound by conceit (māna), whereas the liberated Arahant cuts off craving in nāmarūpa, transcends linguistic reckoning, and escapes the yoke of Death (maccu).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Entrapment in the Nameable (Akkheyyasaññino Sattā): Worldlings take their stand on what can be named (akkheyya) or verbally expressed. Unaware of the limitations imposed by grammar and syntax, they mistake subject-object linguistic relations for ultimate realities, thereby remaining under the yoke of Death (yogamāyanti maccuno).

2. Transcendence of Linguistic Limitations in Arahanthood (Akkheyyaṁ Pariññāya): The Arahant fully understands the nature and limits of language. Consequently, he does not conceive of a fixed "speaker" (akkhātāraṁ na maññati) or subject-object dichotomy, nor does he fall into the three grades of conceit (māna: superior, equal, or inferior).

3. Casting Off Reckoning (Pahāsi Saṅkhaṁ): "Reckoning" (saṅkhā / sankhā) refers to numerals and the basic superstructure of language. An Arahant has relinquished reckoning and cut off craving for name-and-form (nāmarūpa), rendering his mind un-locatable by gods or men across all abodes.

4. The Samsāric Vortex (Vaṭṭa) of Nāmarūpa and Viññāṇa: As detailed in the Mahā Nidāna Sutta, all descriptions of birth, decay, death, terminology (niruttipatho), and designation (paññattipatho) exist only as far as there is a reciprocal whirling round (vaṭṭaṁ vaṭṭati) between consciousness (viññāṇa) and name-and-form (nāmarūpa).

5. Perception Matures into Linguistic Usage (Vohāravepakkā Saññā): The Buddha asserts that perception has linguistic convention as its outcome ("vohāravepakkāhaṁ... saññaṁ vadāmi"). Prolific perceptions (papañcasaññāsaṅkhā) drive beings to project subjective experiences outward through language ("I was of such a perception"), deepening their Samsāric entanglement.

6. Arahattaphala Samādhi as Cessation of Perception: In the concentration of the Fruit of Arahanthood (illustrated in the Sandha Sutta), the mind of the "thoroughbred of a man" (bhadro purisājāniyo) is entirely liberated from objects of perception—including the four great elements (mahābhūta) and the formless realms. This state is established nowhere (appatiṭṭha), unmanifesting (appavatta), and objectless (anārammaṇa).

7. Cutting the Whirlpool (Vaṭṭa-Chinna): Nibbāna is described in the Udāna as the drying up of Samsāric currents and the total cutting off of the whirlpool (chinnaṁ vaṭṭaṁ na vaṭṭati). Once the mutual dependency of consciousness and nāmarūpa is severed, the point of reference for personal existence disappears.

8. Consciousness as Fuel-Dependent Fire (Viññāṇūpasama): Consciousness is not an immutable, independent soul; like a fire that is named after its specific fuel (log-fire, chaff-fire), consciousness arises solely dependent on conditions. Liberation entails the complete appeasement of all six modes of consciousness (viññāṇūpasama), likened to the going out of a lamp (yathāyampadīpo).

9. Beyond Reckoning after Parinibbāna (Na Upeti Saṅkhaṁ): As stated in the Upasīva Māṇavapucchā and Aśvaghoṣa's Saundarānanda, just as a flame blown out by the wind cannot be said to have gone to any direction, an Arahant released from the name-group (nāmakāyā vimutto) cannot be reckoned as existing, non-existing, or persisting in any spatial realm.

10. The Transcendence of Logic via Dependent Arising (Paṭicca Samuppāda): As revealed in the Kaccānagotta Sutta, the world mostly rests on the duality of absolute existence (atthitā) and non-existence (natthitā). Seeing the arising of the world with right wisdom dispels the notion of non-existence; seeing the cessation of the world dispels the notion of existence. The Dhamma is thus beyond the sphere of logic (atakkāvacara).

11. The Fallacy of Personality-View (Sakkāyadiṭṭhi) & The Magic-Kettle Simile: Worldlings mistake the continuous, cyclic process of arising (samudaya) and ceasing (nirodha) for a compact, solid self (sat + kāya). Like a trick "magic-kettle" where water continuously circulates through a hidden tube, existence (bhava) is an uninterrupted process driven by ignorance and craving, which ceases completely upon the realization of Nibbāna.
