Sermon 014 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #14 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #14

Primary Theme: Overcoming hatred (dosa / verina) and social conflict through wise attention (yoniso manasikāra), the systematic cultivation of loving-kindness (mettā-bhāvanā) and the Sublime Abodes (brahmavihāra), and the realization of the unshakeable peace of Nibbāna (nibbāna-sukha).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The Supreme Buddha, along with the Sakya clan of Kapilavastu and the Koliya clan of Koliya.

The Interaction: The Sakya and Koliya clans shared the waters of the Rohini River, controlling its flow via a jointly maintained dam to irrigate their respective agricultural fields.

The Disaster / Turning Point: A severe drought caused the river's water levels to deplete significantly. A fierce dispute broke out between the farmers of both kingdoms over sole water rights. The conflict escalated into bitter insults regarding lineage, eventually drawing in the military commanders, viceroys, and kings of both nations, who mobilized their full armies to wage a devastating war.

The Result / Resolution: Surveying the world at dawn with great compassion, the Buddha saw that the conflict would result in immense bloodshed. He traveled through the air and sat cross-legged above the middle of the Rohini River. The warring kings laid down their weapons upon seeing Him. The Buddha pointed out the foolishness of sacrificing priceless human lives for the sake of low-value river water and explained that hatred was the underlying cause of their strife. He delivered the verses from the Sukhavagga of the Dhammapada (vv. 197–199). The war was immediately averted, and countless beings attained the fruit of Stream-entry (sotāpatti-phala).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Misguided Search for Happiness vs. True Happiness (Nibbāna-sukha)

All living beings desire happiness, yet worldlings routinely fall into suffering (dukkha) because ignorance (avijjā) and craving (taṇhā) drive them to seek happiness through the wrong means. True, supreme happiness (susukhaṃ / nibbāna-sukha) is not found in sensual gratification or worldly victory, but in the complete calming of defilements (kilesa).

2. The Self-Defeating Nature of Victory and Retaliation

Seeking peace by conquering or killing enemies is a delusion. As the Buddha stated: "Victory breeds hatred; the defeated lies down in suffering. The peaceful one lies down happily, abandoning both victory and defeat." Hatred is never appeased by hatred; attempts to forcefully conquer an opponent only sow the seeds of long-term saṃsāric enmity.

3. The Danger of Unchecked Hatred & "The Person Like an Open Sore" (Arukūpamo Puggala)

Hatred originates as a thought seed in the mind, matures into resentment (āghāta), and ultimately burns both self and others. A chronically irritable person who harbours intense hatred is compared to arukūpamo puggala—a person with an open sore (aruka). Just as a wounded man reacts with pain and anger to the slightest physical bump in a crowd, a person steeped in anger collides destructively with people and circumstances everywhere, generating karma that leads to lower realms (niraya, peta).

4. Five Methods for Appeasing Deep-Seated Resentment (Āghātapati-vinaya)

The sermon highlights five practical Dhamma methods to subdue harbored resentment (āghāta):

Practicing loving-kindness (mettā).

Arousing compassion (karuṇā) by viewing the offender as a spiritually sick, suffering person.

Developing equanimity (upekkhā) by recognizing that both oneself and the offender are merely combinations of the four great elements (pathavī, āpo, tejo, vāyo).

Employing non-attention and forgetting (asati amanasikāra) by deliberately refusing to dwell on the offense.

Contemplating the law of karma (kammassakatā / kamma-dāyāda), recognizing that unwholesome doers suffer their own karmic fruits naturally without needing us to act as instruments of punishment.

5. Ven. Sāriputta’s Five Similes for Disciplining Feelings Toward Others

Venerable Sāriputta taught five similes to neutralize resentment based on an individual's character mix:

Bad physical, good verbal conduct: Like a rag-robe monk (paṃsukūla-cīvara) who ignores the soiled part of a cloth and tears off only the clean portion, one should ignore bad physical actions and focus strictly on the person's good verbal conduct.

Good physical, bad verbal conduct: Like a thirsty traveler who pushes aside floating algae to drink clean water from a pond, one should push aside verbal faults to appreciate good physical deeds.

Bad physical and verbal conduct, but rare moments of mental goodness: Like a thirsty traveler who carefully laps water from a cow’s footprint with his tongue so as not to muddy it, one must cherish even the smallest flash of goodness in a largely unwholesome person.

Bad physical, verbal, and mental conduct: Like feeling deep compassion for a destitute, sick traveler stranded in a desert without food, water, or a guide, one must feel pure compassion for a person completely devoid of virtue, as they are heading toward destruction.

Pure physical and pure verbal conduct: Like a traveler effortlessly drinking, bathing, and relaxing by a clear, shaded lake, one should joyfully immerse oneself in the virtues of a pure person.

6. Wise Attention (Yoniso Manasikāra) as the Key to Transforming Attitudes

Attitudes are not fixed entities; they are malleable mental perspectives. Defilements arise due to unwise attention (ayoniso manasikāra), whereas liberation is cultivated by wise attention (yoniso manasikāra). The Buddha permits using any effective simile, image, or wholesome thought construct that successfully suppresses unwholesome roots (lobha, dosa, moha) and expands wholesome states (kusala).

7. Mind-Deliverance of Loving-Kindness (Mettā-Cetovimutti) and Its Eleven Benefits

When loving-kindness is thoroughly digested and universalized beyond narrow personal boundaries, it becomes "mind-deliverance" (mettā-cetovimutti). As outlined in the Mettānisaṃsa Sutta, this grants eleven tangible benefits: peaceful sleep, pleasant awakening, freedom from nightmares, endearment to humans, endearment to non-humans, protection by devas, immunity from harm by weapons or poisons, rapid mental concentration (samādhi), a serene/radiant complexion, an unconfused death, and rebirth in the Brahma worlds if higher attainments are not yet reached.

8. The Simile of the Leather Shoes: Taming the Internal Enemy

Trying to achieve peace by destroying all external enemies is like trying to cover the entire earth with leather to avoid sharp thorns—an impossible task. Wearing a pair of leather shoes instantly protects the feet wherever one walks. Similarly, when the internal enemy of hatred inside one's own mind is slain through meditation, all external enemies are effectively conquered.

9. The Fourfold Dangers (Bhaya) and the Happiness of Blamelessness (Anavajja-sukha)

Misguided pursuit of wealth through wrong livelihood (micchā-ājīva) ignores the internal psychological cost of sin. The Buddha outlined four great fears/dangers:

Fear of self-reproach (attānuvāda-bhaya)

Fear of others' reproach (parānuvāda-bhaya)

Fear of worldly punishment (daṇḍa-bhaya)

Fear of evil destinations/woeful rebirths (duggati-bhaya)

True daily happiness is the "happiness of blamelessness" (anavajja-sukha), experienced when one's conscience is completely free from guilt.

10. The Four Sublime Abodes (Brahmavihāra) for Social and Mental Equilibrium

Emotional balance requires cultivating all four divine abodes:

Mettā (loving-kindness toward all beings).

Karuṇā (compassion toward those who suffer).

Muditā (appreciative joy, celebrating others' success to eliminate envy).

Upekkhā (equanimity, accepting things that cannot be changed through the lens of karma).

11. Establishing Daily Meditative Discipline (Kammaṭṭhāna)

Meditation is not an academic exercise or a pastime reserved for old age; it is the essential daily "workplace" of the mind. By practicing repetitive mental cultivation (āsevitā, bhāvitā, bahulīkatā) at the beginning and end of each day using core subjects—such as mettā, recollection of the Buddha (buddhānussati), bodily foulness (asubha), or mindfulness of death (maraṇasati)—one develops mental clarity, unshakeable decision-making skills, and continuous mindfulness across all four postures.

12. Extinguishing the World's Fires through Insight (Vipassanā)

The entire world is burning (sabbam āditthaṃ) with the fires of greed (lobha), hatred (dosa), and delusion (moha) operating through the six sense bases. Realizing Nibbāna is the actual cooling down (nibbāna) of these fires. By applying insight meditation (vipassanā-bhāvanā) to see the three characteristics (tilakkhaṇa)—impermanence (anicca), suffering (dukkha), and non-self (anattā)—a practitioner develops the spiritual faculties (indriya) and powers (bala) to progress through the stages of awakening (Stream-entry, Once-returning, Non-returning, and Arahantship).
