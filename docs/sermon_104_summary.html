Sermon 104 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #104 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #104 (Pahankaṇuwa Sinhala Audio Sermon No. 121)

Primary Theme: The Middle Way of clearing away karma and food-craving, the three liberations (suññata, animitta, appaṇihita), and treating the root cause of suffering (yoniso manasikāra) through the untraceable mental path of an Arahant.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Venerable Belaṭṭhisīsa, an Arahant who was formerly one of the one thousand matted-hair ascetics under Uruvela Kassapa (attaining liberation via the Ādittapariyāya Sutta) and preceptor to Venerable Ānanda.

The Interaction: Living in the forest and devoted to solitude, Venerable Belaṭṭhisīsa spent long periods absorbed in meditative attainments (dhyāna), the happiness of fruition (phala-samāpatti), and the attainment of cessation (nirodha-samāpatti).

The Disaster / Turning Point: Finding daily alms-round a disturbance to his spiritual happiness (nirāmisa-sukha), Venerable Belaṭṭhisīsa collected plain rice on alms-round, dried it in the sun, and stored it in his hut. He would remain in deep absorption for days, and upon emerging, merely moisten the dried rice with water to appease his physical hunger. Seeing this, other monks criticized him for hoarding food and reported the matter to the Buddha.

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha laid down a training rule (sikkhāpada) of Pācittiya (prohibiting monks from storing food for more than a day) to safeguard the future Dispensation from laxity. However, the Buddha refrained from rebuking Venerable Belaṭṭhisīsa as a "foolish man" (mogha-purisa). Instead, he uttered Dhammapada Stanza 92, revealing that the Elder’s action stemmed not from greed, but from fewness of wishes (appicchatā) and fondness for deep spiritual absorption. His true resort (gocara) was Nibbāna, rendering his conduct pure and his mental path untraceable.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Twofold Hoarding (Sannicayo) and the Method of Clearing Away:

Hoarding occurs in two ways: material requisites (storing food/property) and spiritual accumulation (gathering karma/volition across Saṃsāra). Worldly beings continuously gather karma, but the Buddha discovered the unique Middle Path for clearing away karma rather than accumulating it.

2. The Fourfold Classification of Karma:

Dark karma with dark results (vipāka) (leading to hellish realms).

Bright karma with bright results (leading to Brahma realms).

Mixed dark-and-bright karma (leading to human/deva existences).

Neither-dark-nor-bright karma with neither-dark-nor-bright results: The volition/intention (saṅkhāra) of the Noble Eightfold Path that directly destroys the accumulation of the previous three types of karma.

3. Threefold Full Comprehension of Food (Pariññātabhojanā):

Superficial Knowledge (Ñāta-pariññā): Basic recognition of food items (e.g., distinguishing rice from gruel).

Analytical Evaluation (Tīraṇa-pariññā): Contemplating food through wise reflection (paccavekkhaṇā), discerning it as the four great elements (dhātu-saññā), repulsive (paṭikūla-saññā), impermanent (anicca), and not-self (anattā).

Abandonment (Pahāna-pariññā): Consuming food solely to sustain the body for the holy life (brahmacariya), thereby dispelling desire and lustful attachment (chanda-rāga) for taste.

4. The Three Liberations / Gateways to Nibbāna (Vimokkha):

Signless Liberation (Animitta-vimokkha): Realized at the culmination of contemplating impermanence (anicca), where the mind ceases to grasp any permanent sign (nicca-nimitta).

Desireless Liberation (Appaṇihita-vimokkha): Realized at the culmination of contemplating suffering (dukkha), where craving (taṇhā) is eradicated and no targets or future expectations are projected.

Empty Liberation (Suññata-vimokkha): Realized at the culmination of contemplating non-self (anattā), where the illusion of "I" and "mine" collapses into absolute voidness.

5. The Untraceable Path of the Liberated Mind (Gati tesaṃ durannayā):

Like birds flying through the sky leaving no footprints (ākāseva sakuntānaṃ), the mental process (gati) of an Arahant dwelling in fruition attainment (phala-samāpatti) cannot be traced or located. In non-manifestative consciousness (anidassana-viññāṇa), dualities (long/short, beautiful/ugly) and the four elements (mahābhūta) find no footing.

6. The Redefinition of "Resort/Pasture" (Gocara):

The word gocara historically refers to a pasture village where cattle graze or monks collect alms. For ordinary beings, gocara means sensual stimuli and material happiness (āmisa-sukha). For an Arahant like Belaṭṭhisīsa, the true gocara is the unworldly happiness (nirāmisa-sukha) of fruition attainment (phala-samāpatti) and cessation (nirodha-samāpatti).

7. The Middle Way Regarding Food and Meditative Aids:

The Buddha taught: "Dependent on food, food is to be abandoned" (āhāraṃ nissāya āhāro pahātabbo). Neither self-mortification (attakilamathānuyoga) nor sensual indulgence (kāmasukhallikānuyoga) leads to release. Physical food and dhyānic happiness are utilized as tools/aids (nissāya) without clinging, turning consumption itself into insight meditation (vipassanā).

8. Treating the Cause vs. Treating Symptoms (Yoniso Manasikāra):

The Dog Simile: Nigaṇṭhas (Jains) and self-mortifiers bite the stick (inflicting suffering on the body to exhaust past karma), treating symptoms rather than the root cause.

The Lion Simile: The Buddha, the Supreme Surgeon (bhisakko sallakatto), leaps at the archer (the root cause), using radical/wise attention (yoniso manasikāra) to eradicate craving (taṇhā) within the mind.

9. The Forward-Leaning Nature of Craving (Taṇhā):

Craving is ponobhavikā (leading to renewed existence). It acts like a carrot dangling in front of a donkey, constantly leaning forward toward grasping (upādāna) and birth (jāti). Even Wheel-turning Kings (cakkavatti) like Mahāmandhātu died with unquenched craving because sensual desire has no point of satisfaction.

10. Institutional Rules for Requisites vs. Inner Purity:

To protect the monastic order from drifting into lay habits of hoarding, the Buddha codified four strictly defined time-frames for consumables:

Yāvakālika (temporary food, consumed before noon).

Yāmakālika (allowable fruit juices/beverages, kept until dawn of the next day).

Sattāhakālika (medicinal tonics like ghee, honey, sugar, kept for up to 7 days).

Yāvajīvika (lifelong medicines like dried roots and myrobalans, kept as needed).
