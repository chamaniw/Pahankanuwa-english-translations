Sermon 160 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #160 Summary

Sermon #160 Summary

1. Overview

- Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #160

- Primary Theme: The contrast between external Brahminical rites of water purification (udaka-suddhi) and the Buddha’s internal mental purification (snāto antarena snānena); the non-clinging, causal nature of virtue (sīla) and spiritual practices within the framework of dependent arising (paṭiccasamuppāda); and the implementation of the Middle Path (majjhimā paṭipadā) via the Four Establishments of Mindfulness (satipaṭṭhāna).

---

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

- The Protagonist: The servant maid Puṇṇā (later Puṇṇā Therī), who served in the household of Anāthapiṇḍika the Treasurer, and the Brahmin Sotthiya.

- The Interaction: Out of fear of verbal abuse and punishment from her mistresses, Puṇṇā was forced to fetch water from the river during the freezing winter season. At the river ford, she observed the Brahmin Sotthiya repeatedly plunging into the ice-cold water to perform the ritual bath of purification (udaka-suddhi).

- The Disaster / Turning Point: Seeing Sotthiya trembling uncontrollably from the extreme cold, Puṇṇā challenged the logic of his practice. She pointed out that if plunging in water could wash away evil deeds, aquatic creatures (frogs, turtles, fish) and violent criminals (butchers, executioners, thieves) would automatically attain heaven (sugati). Furthermore, she argued that if water could wash away sin, it would wash away accumulated merit as well. She advised him that true safety comes not from bathing, but from refraining from evil actions and taking refuge in the Triple Gem.

- The Result / Resolution: Sotthiya recognized his wrong view (micchā-diṭṭhi). Refusing his offer of robes as a teaching fee, Puṇṇā directed him to embrace morality (sīla) and the Noble Eightfold Path. Sotthiya subsequently sought ordination under the Buddha, realized the Threefold Knowledge (tivijjā), and became an Arahant—declaring himself a true Brahmin (saccabrāhmaṇo) who is truly bathed (nahātaka). Hearing of this interaction, Anāthapiṇḍika freed Puṇṇā from servitude. She later entered the Bhikkhunī Order and also attained Arahantship with the four analytical knowledges (paṭisambhidā).

---

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Critique of External Purification (*Udaka-Suddhi* vs. *Snāto Antarena Snānena*):

The Buddha rejects ritualistic water-cleansing (udaka-suddhi). True purity is an internal affair (snāto antarena snānena—"bathed with an internal bath"), achieved by removing defilements (upakkilesa) from the mind rather than washing the physical body.

2. The Pure Lake of Dhamma and Ford of Virtue (*Dhammo Rahado Sīlatittho*):

Citing the dialogue with Brahmin Saṅgārava, the sermon emphasizes that Dhamma is the unpolluted lake and virtue (sīla) is its bathing ford. One who bathes in this ford realizes the Threefold Knowledge (tivijjā) and crosses over to the shore of Nibbāna dry or un-moistened (anallagattova tarati pāraṃ), untouched by defilements.

3. Mind Purification as a Prerequisite for Higher Attainments (*Vatthūpama Sutta* Simile):

Just as a soiled cloth cannot take dye properly and becomes discolored, a defiled mind cannot absorb spiritual development and leads to a bad destination (duggati). Removing mental defilements such as greed (abhijjhā), ill-will (byāpāda), and anger (kodha) enables concentration (samādhi), the sublime abodes (brahma-vihāra), and liberating insight (vipassanā).

4. Internal Redefinition of Impurity (*Asoceyya*) and Purification (*Soceyya*):

Referring to the discourse with Cunda the Smith's Son (Cunda Kammāraputta), the sermon contrasts Brahminical ritual actions (touching ground, cow dung, green grass, fire-worship, sun-salutation, and thrice-daily bathing) with the Buddha's criteria:

- Impurity (*asoceyya*): The 10 unwholesome courses of action (dasa akusala kamma-patha)—3 bodily, 4 verbal, and 3 mental (covetousness/unrighteous greed (abhijjhā), ill-will (byāpāda), and wrong view (micchā-diṭṭhi)).

- Purification (*soceyya*): Abstinence (veramaṇī / virati) from these unwholesome paths and the cultivation of the 10 wholesome courses of action (dasa kusala kamma-patha), including mundane right view (dasa-vatthuka sammā-diṭṭhi).

5. Sequential Support of the Seven Purifications (*Visuddhi*) and the Relay Chariot Simile:

Drawing from the Rathavinīta Sutta, the sermon explains that spiritual progress occurs sequentially: purification of virtue (sīla-visuddhi) exists solely for purification of mind (citta-visuddhi), which leads to purification of view (diṭṭhi-visuddhi), and so forth, culminating in unclinging liberation (anupādā-vimokkha). Like King Pasenadi traveling by seven relay chariots, each stage serves as a functional vehicle to the next, not an end in itself.

6. Avoidance of Clinging to Views (*Diṭṭhi-Parāmāsa* / *Aparāmaṭṭha*):

Virtue in the Buddha's Dispensation is described as un-grasped (aparāmaṭṭha). Citing the Māgandiya Sutta, the Thero highlights that liberation is neither attained merely by views, learning, knowledge, or rites/vows (sīlabbatena), nor is it attained without them. Rites and virtues are functional tools utilized without dogmatic attachment (parāmāsa).

7. The Active Effort Required for Mental Cleansing (*Upakkameṇa Cittassa Pariyodapanā*):

Citing the Buddha's explanation of the Noble Sabbath (ariya-uposatha) to Visākhā, mental purification requires conscious exertion (upakkameṇa cittassa pariyodapanā). Just as cleaning a head, washing a cloth, polishing a mirror, or refining raw gold demands human endeavor (tajjā vāyāma), mental cleansing demands active spiritual striving.

8. Virtue as Volition (*Cetanā-Sīla*) and Mental Abstinence (*Virati-Cetasika*):

Sīla is fundamentally internal, defined as volition (cetanā) and mental abstinences (ārati, virati, paṭivirati). Undertaking virtue involves formal mental resolution (adhiṭṭhāna), which stops past regrets, followed by reflective joy (āvarjana / pāmojja). This joy gladdens the mind, serving as the essential foundation for concentration (samādhi).

9. The Three Practices (*Paṭipadā*) and the Middle Path:

The sermon contrasts three global practices:

- Indulgence (*āgāḷhā paṭipadā* / *kāmasukhallikānuyoga*): Complete immersion in sensual pleasures.

- Self-mortification (*nijjhāmā paṭipadā* / *attakilamathānuyoga*): Scorching and tormenting the body through rituals and ascetic pain.

- The Middle Path (*majjhimā paṭipadā*): Practically executed through the Four Establishments of Mindfulness (satipaṭṭhāna), focusing on internal mental transformation rather than physical torture.

10. Reinterpreting Asceticism (*Tapas*) as the Burning of Defilements (*Ātāpī*):

True ascetic heat (tapo / ātāpī) in the Dhamma does not mean inflicting pain on the physical body, but employing supreme energy (padhāna-viriya, pahitatta) to burn away defilements (kilesa) while dwelling mindful (satimā) and fully aware (sampajāno).
