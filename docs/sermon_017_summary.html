Sermon 017 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #17 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #17

Primary Theme: Discriminating the true essence (sāra) from the essenceless (asāra) through right thought (sammā-saṅkappa); abandoning worldly superficiality, wrong views (micchā-diṭṭhi), and counterfeit Dhamma (saddhamma-patirūpaka) in order to realize the true heartwood of the Dispensation—liberation (Nibbāna)—via the structural law of Dependent Arising (Paṭiccasamuppāda) and heedful practice (appamāda).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Upatissa and Kolita (who later became Venerable Sāriputta and Venerable Moggallāna), the wanderer Sañjaya (their initial teacher), Venerable Assaji, and the Supreme Buddha.

The Interaction: Upatissa and Kolita, two wealthy Brahmin youths of Rajagaha, are lifelong friends. While watching the annual mountain-top festival (Giragga-samajja), their mature saṃsāric wisdom awakens, filling them with disillusionment. Realizing all attendees will be dead within a century, they renounce their worldly wealth, ordain under the wanderer Sañjaya, and quickly master his entire doctrine. Finding his teaching essenceless, they agree to search independently for Nibbāna, promising that whoever finds the Deathless first will inform the other.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Upatissa encounters Venerable Assaji walking for alms. Deeply struck by Assaji's tranquil faculties, Upatissa serves him and requests the essence of his master's teaching. Assaji recites the famous two-line causal formula (Ye dhammā hetuppabhavā...), instantly triggering Upatissa's realization of Stream-entry (Sotāpatti-phala). Upatissa shares the verse with Kolita, who also attains Sotāpatti-phala. When they attempt to take Sañjaya to the Buddha, Sañjaya refuses out of pride, asking whether there are more fools or wise people in the world. He declares: "Let the fools come to me, and let the wise go to the wise Ascetic Gotama," refusing to demote himself from a "water-pot" to a "ladle." As five hundred disciples abandon Sañjaya to follow Upatissa and Kolita to the Buddha, Sañjaya vomits warm blood out of intense grief.

The Result / Resolution: Arriving at Veluvana Monastery, Upatissa and Kolita relate the events to the Buddha. Monks murmur about favoritism when the Buddha confers upon them the titles of Chief Disciples (Aggasāvaka), but the Buddha clarifies that titles are bestowed based on past perfections (pāramīs), not personal bias. He then utters Dhammapada verses 11–12, contrasting Sañjaya’s ruinous attachment to the essenceless with the Chief Disciples' wisdom in discerning and attaining the true essence (sāra).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Distinguishing Essence (Sāra) from Essencelessness (Asāra) (Dhammapada 11–12):

The thesis of the sermon rests on recognizing that true spiritual progress requires inner strength to cast aside worldly futility (asāra) and embrace the ultimate truth (sāra). Those governed by wrong thoughts (micchā-saṅkappa) mistake the hollow for the essential, whereas those governed by right thoughts (sammā-saṅkappa) penetrate to the true core.

2. The Danger of Wrong View (Micchā-diṭṭhi) as the Supreme Fault (Parama-vajja):

The Buddha designates wrong view as the most destructive fault because every bodily, verbal, and mental action conforming to micchā-diṭṭhi yields bitter, painful results (dukkha). Ven. Nanananda highlights the Buddha’s agricultural simile: just as a seed of neem, bitter cucumber, or bitter gourd planted in moist soil extracts nutrients only to yield a bitter fruit, conduct rooted in wrong view converts all experiences into unwholesome saṃsāric suffering. Conversely, right view (sammā-diṭṭhi) acts like a grape or sugarcane seed, turning every input into sweet fruit.

3. "Mung-Bean Soup" (Muggasuppatā) and Counterfeit Dhamma (Saddhamma-Patirūpaka):

A critical danger to the Dispensation is the proliferation of "half-truths," anciently termed muggasuppatā (mung-bean soup containing unboiled, hard wild beans). Ven. Nanananda warns against popular, appealing doctrines that blend Dhamma with worldly compromises. Referencing the Buddha’s declaration to Ven. Mahā Kassapa, he emphasizes that external religions cannot destroy the Dhamma; rather, it disappears from within when counterfeit Dhamma (saddhamma-patirūpaka)—like fake gold replacing raw gold (jātarūpa)—deceives the masses.

4. Dependent Arising (Paṭiccasamuppāda) as the Core Law:

The core truth that transformed Upatissa into a Stream-enterer upon hearing Ven. Assaji's verse is the structural law of causality:

“Imasmiṃ sati idaṃ hoti, imassuppādā idaṃ upajjati...” ("When this exists, this is; with the arising of this, this arises...").

This principle dispels the illusion of a soul or permanent entity, exposing all worldly phenomena as conditionally arisen and subject to cessation (nirodha).

5. The Heartwood Simile (Mahāsāropama Sutta):

The sermon uses the structural breakdown of a tree to map spiritual progress:

Leaves and Twigs: Gains, fame, and offerings (lābha-sakkāra-siloka).

Bark: Moral virtue (sīla).

Softwood: Concentration/serenity (samādhi).

Heartwood (Sāra): The fruit of Arahantship, liberation (Nibbāna).

Ven. Nanananda cautions against mistaking lower stages—or mere temporary relief of daily problems—for the primary heartwood of the Dispensation.

6. The Parable of the "Bundle of Hemp" (Asāra-gāhi):

Using an old Sri Lankan folk story, the Venerable illustrates human stubbornness and attachment to the essenceless. Two men travel in search of wealth: one repeatedly discards raw materials (hemp → thread → cloth → iron → bronze → silver → gold) to bring home pure gold, while the foolish companion carries a heavy, worthless bundle of raw hemp all the way home simply because he tied it tightly at the start. Most beings similarly cling to outdated, essenceless worldly habits and views out of sheer inertia.

7. Three Grades of Saṃsāric Experience:

Life in saṃsāra is categorized into three educational levels:

Gratification (Assāda): Chasing sensual pleasures; where most of the world remains stuck.

Danger (Ādīnava): Disillusionment upon perceiving the impermanent, defective nature of sensual pleasures.

Escape (Nissaraṇa): Renunciation (nekkhamma) and the path to Nibbāna.

8. Heedfulness (Appamāda) and Grounds for Urgency (Saṃvega-vatthu):

Human existence is an exceptionally rare opportunity. Because saṃsāra is fraught with danger—where even a virtuous life can end in rebirth in lower realms (apāya) if past unwholesome karma surfaces—one must cultivate urgency (saṃvega) through mindfulness of death (maraṇasati) and contemplation of aging, sickness, and death. Appamāda (heedfulness) is the single most vital quality for securing Stream-entry (Sotāpatti-phala) and escaping saṃsāric peril.

9. Equal Accessibility of Supramundane Realization (Sammā-Paṭipatti):

Citing the Buddha's response to a Brahmin, Ven. Nanananda reinforces that the path to realization does not depend on being a monk or a layperson. Whoever undertakes the correct practice (sammā-paṭipatti) of Virtue, Concentration, and Wisdom (sīla, samādhi, paññā) can realize the supramundane paths and fruits (magga-phala).

10. Preservative Power of Inner Practice vs. External Show:

The Sambuddha Sāsana is not preserved through physical structures (golden fences, buildings, or tourist attractions), but as a heart-to-heart transmission of internal purification across generations. The Dispensation rests equally upon the Fourfold Assembly (catu-parisā: monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen), demanding that lay practitioners actively cultivate meditation rather than settling for mere ritualistic giving.

11. Total Holy Life as Spiritual Friendship (Kalyāṇa-mittatā):

Correcting Ven. Ānanda’s notion that spiritual friendship is half of the holy life, the Buddha stated that kalyāṇa-mittatā constitutes the entire holy life (sakalam-eva hidaṃ brahmacariyaṃ). True preservation of the Dhamma occurs when individuals purify their own minds and act as spiritual friends to their families and communities.

12. The Timeless Leading-Out Quality of the Dhamma (Niyyānika & Akālika):

The True Dhamma possesses an immediate, leading-out quality (niyyānika) that can be seen directly for oneself (sandiṭṭhika) and yields results without delay (akālika). Meditative practice must move beyond serenity (samatha) to insight (vipassanā-manasikāra), penetrating the three marks of existence—impermanence (anicca), suffering (dukkha), and non-self (anattā)—to experience Nibbāna right here and now.
