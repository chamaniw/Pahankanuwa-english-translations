Sermon 191 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #191 Summary

Sermon #191 Summary

1. Overview

- Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #191 (Paṭicca Samuppāda Sermon 9)

- Primary Theme: Deconstructing craving (taṇhā)—symbolized as the "seamstress" (sibbani)—by penetrating the two ends (ubhante) and the middle (majjhe) through Dependent Arising (paṭicca samuppāda), insight into impermanence (anicca), and the attainment of the seamless, non-grasping mind (bhavanirodha).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

- The Protagonist: A assembly of elder monks (theras) gathered at the assembly hall in Isipatana, Benares, discussing a cryptic verse originally taught by the Buddha to the Brahmin youth Tissa Metteyya in the Metteyyapañha of the Pārāyanavagga (Sutta Nipāta).

- The Interaction: The monks hosted a symposium to analyze the verse: "Yo ubhante viditvāna, majjhe mantā na lippati..." ("He who having known both ends gets not attached at the middle with wisdom..."). Six different elder monks presented six distinct doctrinal interpretations regarding what constitutes the "first end," "second end," "middle," and the "seamstress."

- The Disaster / Turning Point: Recognizing the diversity of their interpretations, the monks faced a predicament regarding which interpretation was definitively well-spoken (subhāsita). They decided to approach the Buddha collectively to present all six explanations and seek his clarification.

- The Result / Resolution: The Buddha declared that all six interpretations were well-spoken from specific angles (representing valid subjects for meditation leading to Arahanthood). However, he confirmed that his original intention in the Metteyyapañha matched the first Thera's interpretation: Contact is one end, Arising of contact is the second end, Cessation of contact is the middle, and Craving is the seamstress.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Craving as the Seamstress (*Sibbani / Taṇhā*)

Craving is personified as a seamstress because it rapidly stitches together two ends while bypassing the middle (cessation), binding beings to repeated existence (ponobhavikā). Like a fast sewing machine shuttle, craving's flirting nature of delighting now-here, now-there (tatratatrābhinandinī) creates the illusion of continuity across births.

2. The First Approved Interpretation: Contact, Arising, and Cessation

Approved directly by the Buddha: Contact (phassa) is one end; the arising of contact (phassa-samudaya) is the second end; the cessation of contact (phassa-nirodha) is the middle. Craving prevents the worldling from seeing the cessation in the middle by stitching contact directly to its arising.

3. The Five Alternative Valid Interpretations of the Two Ends & Middle

- Time: Past (atīta) and Future (anāgata) as the two ends; Present (paccuppanna) as the middle.

- Feeling: Pleasant (sukha) and Unpleasant (dukkha) feelings as the two ends; Neither-unpleasant-nor-pleasant (adukkham-asukha) feeling as the middle.

- Name-and-Form: Name (nāma) and Form (rūpa) as the two ends; Consciousness (viññāṇa) as the middle.

- Sense Spheres: Internal sense spheres (ajjhattikāni āyatanāni) and External sense spheres (bāhirāni āyatanāni) as the two ends; Consciousness (viññāṇa) as the middle.

- Personality: Aggregate-body (sakkāya) and Arising of sakkāya (sakkāya-samudaya) as the two ends; Cessation of sakkāya (sakkāya-nirodha) as the middle.

4. The Conditioned Nature of Consciousness and Contact

Citing the Madhupiṇḍika Sutta, contact is shown to be a conditioned preparation (saṅkhata): depending on eye and forms, eye-consciousness arises (cakkhuñca paṭicca rūpe ca uppajjati cakkhuviññāṇaṁ); the concurrence of the three is contact (tiṇṇaṁ saṅgati phasso). It requires a specific, conditionally arisen focusing (tajjo samannāhāro), refuting the notion of an enduring self (attā) that touches.

5. Rejection of the Traditional Definition of *Nāma-Rūpa

The fourth and fifth interpretations explicitly separate Consciousness (viññāṇa) from Name (nāma), positioning consciousness between name and form. This contradicts the traditional commentary that includes consciousness inside nāma. Nāma comprises feeling, perception, intention, contact, and attention; consciousness acts as the reflective medium (illustrated by the myth of Narcissus mistaking his reflection for an external entity).

6. Finding the Exit at the Middle of Feelings

Worldlings see-saw between painful and pleasant feelings, governed by underlying latencies (anusaya) to hate and lust, while ignoring neutral feeling due to latent ignorance (avijjānusaya). The Buddha shows that the counterpart of neutral feeling is ignorance, whose counterpart is knowledge (vijjā), leading to deliverance (vimutti) and Nibbāna. Penetrating the middle releases one from the see-saw.

7. Insight into Present Preparations (*Tattha Tattha Vipassatī*)

Drawing from the Bhaddekaratta Sutta and the verse given to the acrobat Uggasena (Muñca pure...), the practitioner must relinquish past, future, and even hold on the present middle. Accelerating radical attention (yonisomanasikāra) exposes present preparations (saṅkhārā) as brittle and crumbling, breaking the perception of the compact (ghanasaññā).

8. Knowledge of Breaking Up (*Bhaṅga-Ñāṇa*) and Transcendence of Fear

As insight deepens, the meditator observes rapid dissolution at every touch (phussa phussa vayaṁ passaṁ). The prominent appearance of cessation triggers existential fear (bhaya-ñāṇa). Deliverance occurs by realizing that preparations are not "mine," letting go of personality view (sakkāyadiṭṭhi), as illustrated by Arahant Adhimutta facing execution without dread.

9. The Mechanics of Rebirth: Craving as Grasping at Death

Refuting the theory of an intermediate state (antarābhava) using the Kutūhalasālā Sutta, the Thero explains that when a flame is propelled by wind, wind is its grasping (upādāna). Similarly, when a being abandons the body at death, craving (taṇhā) itself functions as the active grasping that stitches the continuum to a new existence.

10. The Arahant’s "Shuttle-Free Mind" and Seamless Experience

For an Arahant in the attainment to the Fruit of Arahanthood (arahattaphala-samāpatti), residual desire and lust (chandarāga) for the five aggregates have been completely abandoned. Like a sewing machine operating without a thread-loaded shuttle, the needle of experience pierces through without leaving a stitch of grasping, realizing the Deathless (amata) and the absolute cessation of existence (bhavanirodha).
