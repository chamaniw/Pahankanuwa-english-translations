Sermon 078 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #78 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #78 (Pahankaṇuwa Sinhala Audio Dhamma Sermon No.112)

Primary Theme: The inescapable nature of kamma, the dangers of sensuality and pride, and the necessity of crossing the four spiritual floods (cattāro oghā) by awakening from the sleep of negligence (pamāda) through the five spiritual faculties (pañc'indriyāni).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: King Pasenadi of Kosala, Prince Viḍūḍabha, Mahānāma the Sakyan, Vāsabhakhattiyā (daughter of Mahānāma and a slave woman), the Sakyan clan, and the Supreme Buddha.

The Interaction: Seeking to build a close relationship with the Buddha and the Saṅgha, King Pasenadi requested a Sakyan princess in marriage. Driven by clan conceit (māna), the Sakyans deceived the King by giving him Vāsabhakhattiyā—a daughter born to Mahānāma by a slave woman—tricking the royal emissaries into believing she was of pure royal lineage. From this union, Prince Viḍūḍabha was born.

The Disaster / Turning Point: At age sixteen, Prince Viḍūḍabha visited his mother's Sakyan relatives. After his departure, a soldier discovered a slave woman washing the wooden bench where the Prince had sat with milk-water while cursing him as the son of a slave. Realizing he had been insulted, Viḍūḍabha swore a bitter revenge: to wash that bench with Sakyan blood. Years later, after seizing the throne of Kosala, King Viḍūḍabha launched a campaign to exterminate the Sakyan clan. Although the Buddha intercepted the army three times (sitting under a tree with little shade to subtly remind him that "the shade of relatives is cool"), on the fourth occasion the Buddha saw that the Sakyans' past unwholesome kamma—collectively poisoning a river in a past life—had reached fruition and could not be averted. Viḍūḍabha systematically massacred the Sakyans.

The Result / Resolution: While returning, Viḍūḍabha’s army camped at the dry bed and banks of the Aciravatī River. Subterranean insects emerged, causing those who had done evil kamma to move onto the riverbed and those free of that specific evil to move to higher ground. A sudden, massive flash flood swept down the river during the night, carrying Viḍūḍabha and his entire host out to sea where they drowned. The Buddha subsequently uttered Dhammapada verse 47, explaining that just as a sudden flood sweeps away a sleeping village, Death (Māra) sweeps away those who remain infatuated with gathering the "flowers" of sensual pleasures.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Ruinous Nature of Clan Conceit (Māna) and Revenge (Paṭigha/Dosa):

The Sakyans' birth-pride led them to employ deceit, which ignited intense hatred and revenge in Viḍūḍabha. This cycle of pride and aversion ultimately caused the complete physical destruction of the Sakyan lineage and the catastrophic demise of Viḍūḍabha's army.

2. The Inescapable and Incomprehensible Nature of Kamma (Kammassakatā & Acinteyya):

Neither Buddhas nor Arahants can alter the cosmic law of kamma (kamma-niyāmatā) once its fruition becomes unpreventable. The Sakyans suffered mass slaughter due to their past past collective deed of river-poisoning, while Viḍūḍabha experienced immediate results in this very life (diṭṭhadhammavedanīya kamma).

3. Collective Demerit through Mental Intention (Cetanā) and Rejoicing:

Just as merit can be shared by rejoicing (anumodanā), unwholesome kamma can be accumulated collectively. Joining in a conspiracy or inwardly rejoicing in evil acts (e.g., celebrating the death of enemies) entangles individuals in shared karmic guilt (cetanāhaṃ bhikkhave kammaṃ vadāmi).

4. The Four Floods (Cattāro Oghā):

Beyond physical torrents, beings in saṃsāra are swept away by four deep, spiritual floods:

Kāma-ogha: The flood of sensual desires.

Bhava-ogha: The flood of existence and attachment to form/formless states (rūpa/arūpa-rāga).

Diṭṭhi-ogha: The flood of the sixty-two wrong views.

Avijjā-ogha: The flood of ignorance regarding the Four Noble Truths.

5. Abandoning and Cutting the Fetters (Saṃyojana):

To cross the torrent (ogha-taraṇa), one must work through two types of fetters:

Cut five lower fetters (orambhāgiya-saṃyojana): Sakkāya-diṭṭhi, sīlabbata-parāmāsa, vicikicchā, kāma-rāga, and paṭigha (which pull one downward like a bond on a bird's leg).

Abandon five higher fetters (uddhambhāgiya-saṃyojana): Rūpa-rāga, arūpa-rāga, māna, uddhacca, and avijjā (releasing one's grip on higher existence).

6. The Sleep of Defilements (Nīvaraṇa) vs. True Wakefulness (Jāgariya-dhamma):

Worldlings who appear physically awake are actually asleep if dominated by the five hindrances (nīvaraṇa). Conversely, those who actively cultivate spiritual faculties remain spiritually awake (jāgara), even during physical sleep or rest (as demonstrated by the Buddha's mindful lion's posture).

7. Sensual Pleasures as Deceptive "Flowers" (Pupphāni):

The competitive commercial world presents alluring, pleasing objects (subha-ārammaṇa) that trick consumers into gathering temporary "flowers." Intoxicated by sensuality, beings fall into the sleep of negligence (pamāda), completely unaware of impermanence (anicca) and impending death.

8. Cultivation of the Five Spiritual Faculties (Pañc'indriyāni):

While physical sense-faculties are shared with animals, spiritual liberation requires developing internal faculties:

Faith (Saddhā): The inspiring confidence/resolution (adhimokkha) that enables one to step into the flood ("saddhāya taratī oghaṃ").

Energy (Viriya): Fourfold-factored effort (caturanga-samannāgata-viriya) applied without wavering.

Mindfulness (Sati): Continuous awareness guarding energy from becoming agitation.

Concentration (Samādhi): Unification of the mind bringing clarity.

Wisdom (Paññā): Direct insight into reality as it is.

9. Living with the Near-Death Mind (Āsanna-citta):

Because external disasters or internal bodily failures can occur at any second, a wise practitioner lives constantly aware of death, avoiding attachment to worldly achievements that turn out to be mere fleeting dreams at the end of life.

10. Nibbāna as Fearless Extinction (Nivīma):

Nibbāna is not a physical place or divine realm; it is the deathless state (amata) realized when the mind is completely freed from the five aggregates. It is the peace of being able to face decay and death (jarāmaraṇa) without a trace of fear, breaking the cycle of saṃsāra once and for all.
