Sermon 130 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #130 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #130

Primary Theme: The rare quality of noble equanimity (tādī) and letting go (cāga) amidst the eight worldly conditions (aṣṭa loka dhamma), contrasting the unshakable balance of the wise (paṇḍita) with the undisciplined volatility of the ignoble (kāpurisa).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The Buddha, five hundred monks (including Ven. Ānanda and Ven. Mahā Kassapa), Brahmin Verañja, five hundred horse dealers from Uttarāpatha, and five hundred undisciplined lay attendants (vighāsāda / eaters of leftovers).

The Interaction: Early in his ministry, while residing under the Naḷeru margosa tree in Verañjā, the Buddha converted Brahmin Verañja after a deep Dhamma debate. Inspired, Verañja invited the Buddha and five hundred monks to spend the upcoming rainy season (vassa) in Verañjā.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Overwhelmed by domestic duties and household worries, Brahmin Verañja completely forgot his invitation. Simultaneously, a severe famine struck Verañjā, leaving the monks with nothing during their alms-rounds. Facing starvation, the monks accepted a daily measure (pata) of raw barley grain offered by five hundred passing horse dealers, which they pounded in mortars to feed the Buddha and themselves throughout the three months.

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha praised the monks for "winning" (vijita) over greed and famine through fewness of wishes (alpeccatā) and silent endurance without complaining to the donor. Later at Jetavana monastery in Sāvatthi, when five hundred lay attendants ate leftover exquisite food and behaved wildly on the riverbank (mirrored in the Vālodaka Jātaka where low-born donkeys became rowdy after drinking sour grape dregs), the Buddha delivered Dhammapada verse 83. He praised the monastic community's constant equanimity and contrasted it with the undisciplined nature of worldly beings.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Unshakable Equanimity Amidst Dualities (Noccāvacaṃ Dassayanti):

The hallmark of the wise (paṇḍita) or good man (sappurisa) is maintaining perfect mental balance without showing elation (ucca) in pleasure (sukha) or depression (avaca) in pain (dukkha). While worldly people fluctuate wildly between highs and lows, noble ones remain unaffected.

2. Everywhere Letting Go (Sabbattha Ve Sappurisā Cajanti):

Renunciation is not merely an external monastic act; it is an all-encompassing internal practice of letting go (cāga). Monks let go of household life, while laypersons practice letting go through generosity (dāna), observing precepts (sīla), and sacrificing personal pleasures for spiritual growth.

3. Absence of Self-Interested Speech (Na Kāmakāmā Lapayanti Santo):

Noble ones (santo) do not engage in flattering, deceitful, or self-serving talk (lapana) to satisfy sensual desires (kāma). During the famine in Verañjā, not a single monk complained to Brahmin Verañja about his neglected promise or their coarse barley diet.

4. The Concept of Spiritual Victory (Vijita) through Contentment (Alpeccatā):

The Buddha declared that the monks "won" (vijita) the struggle against famine not by demanding better conditions, but by conquering defilements through fewness of wishes (alpeccatā). Complaining to lay donors for personal comfort constitutes a spiritual defeat.

5. The Destructive Nature of Unearned Honor (Sakkāra) on the Base Man (Kāpurisa):

Excessive gain and honor destroy an ignoble person (kāpurisa) through pride, just as fruit destroys the banana tree, bamboo, and reed, or as an embryo destroys a she-mule (sakkāro kāpurisaṃ hanti). This was illustrated through the downfall of Devadatta.

6. The Exemplary Ascetic Character of Ven. Mahā Kassapa:

Ven. Mahā Kassapa was highlighted as the supreme model of monastic contentment (santuṭṭho itarītarena). Going for alms with Nibbāna as his sole goal (nissaraṇapañño), his non-attachment to lay families was likened to the hand moving unhindered through the air or the moon reflected untamed in water (candūpamā).

7. The Discipline of Silent Alms-Gathering (Moneyya-Paṭipadā):

In the practice of sagehood (moneyya-paṭipadā), a monk wanders with bowl in hand, behaving as if mute (amūgo mūgasammatho), refraining from idle talk or flattering speech. He neither despises small or coarse gifts (appaṃ dānaṃ na hīḷeyya) nor looks down on the giver (dātāraṃ nāvajāniya).

8. The Quality of "Suchness" (Tādī) and the Balance-Beam Simile:

The term tādī ("suchness" or "being as such") denotes the unwavering equanimity of an Arahant amidst the eight worldly conditions (aṣṭa loka dhamma: gain/loss, fame/disrepute, praise/blame, pleasure/pain). The mind of a tādī remains perfectly level like a weighing scale balance-beam (tulā-daṇḍa), returning from an unyielding alms-round unperturbed, like someone returning empty-handed from a fruitless tree (rukkhaṃva paṇivattati).

9. Compassionate Alms-Taking versus Economic Logic:

Unlike modern economic logic that seeks abundance, enlightened beings accept offerings from the poor out of compassion, knowing that a small gift given amidst hardship (appasmā dakkhiṇā dinnā) yields a thousandfold spiritual fruit for the donor (e.g., the Buddha accepting the coarse bran-cake from the poor slave-woman Puṇṇā).

10. Correction of Perverted Perceptions (Vipallāsa-Saññā) via Insight:

Worldly beings suffer due to four perverted perceptions (vipallāsa): seeing permanence in the impermanent (anicce nicca), pleasure in suffering (dukkhe sukha), self in non-self (anattani atta), and beauty in the foul (asubhe subha). Liberation requires cultivating Dhamma-conforming perceptions (such as those in the Girimānanda Sutta), particularly the perception of impermanence (anicca-saññā), danger (ādīnava-saññā), and non-self (anattā-saññā).

11. The Ultimate Relinquishment: Cessation of Becoming (Bhava-Nirodha):

True Nibbāna is experienced when all mental formations are stilled (sabbasaṅkhārasamatho), all assets/attachments are relinquished (sabbūpadhipaṭinissaggo), and craving is completely destroyed (taṇhakkhaya). The ultimate achievement of a good man is turning the mind away from the side of origination (samudaya) and realizing the absolute peace of the cessation of becoming (bhava-nirodho nibbānaṃ).
