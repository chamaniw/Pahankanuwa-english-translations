Sermon 105 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #105 Summary

Sermon #105 Summary

---

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #105 (Sinhala Audio Sermon No. 154)

Primary Theme: The perils and insatiable nature of craving (taṇhā), its role as the conduit of becoming (bhava-netti) driving rebirth (ponobhavikā), and the ultimate liberation found in the destruction of craving (taṇhakkhaya) as demonstrated through the life and Dhamma summaries of Venerable Raṭṭhapāla.

---

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The clansman Raṭṭhapāla, son of the chief householder Brahmin of Thullakoṭṭhita in the Kuru country, who became Venerable Raṭṭhapāla—declared by the Buddha to be foremost among monks who went forth out of faith (saddhā-pabbajita). Other key figures include the Buddha, Raṭṭhapāla’s parents, his former wives (purāṇa-dutiyikā), and King Koravya of Kuru.

The Interaction: While the Buddha was wandering in Kuru and preaching at Thullakoṭṭhita, Raṭṭhapāla listened to the Dhamma and realized that the pure holy life (brahmacariya) could not easily be lived within the confines of household life. He approached the Buddha requesting higher ordination (upasampadā), but the Buddha informed him that Tathāgatas do not ordain anyone without parental consent.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Raṭṭhapāla’s parents thrice refused his request to go forth. In response, Raṭṭhapāla initiated an uncompromising hunger strike, lying on the floor with the vow "either ordination or death." Realizing he would starve to death, his parents reluctantly conceded on the condition that he would return to visit them. After ordaining and swiftly attaining Arahantship in Sāvatthī, Venerable Raṭṭhapāla returned to Thullakoṭṭhita. Upon arriving at his family home for alms, his father insulted him without recognizing him, and he received only stale rice from a maidservant. When invited back the next day, his father attempted to entice him back into lay life by piling up immense family gold and having his former wives ornament themselves.

The Result / Resolution: Unmoved by wealth and sensory entice, Venerable Raṭṭhapāla advised his father to throw the gold into the River Ganges to avoid grief, addressed his former wives as "Sisters" (causing them to faint), ate his meal, and departed into the air after uttering verses on the worthlessness of sense-pleasures. He retired to King Koravya's park, where he met the king and reconciled the king's doubts regarding why a young, wealthy, healthy man with living relatives would renounce the world without experiencing any of the four worldly losses (pārijunñatā). Raṭṭhapāla explained the Four Dhamma Summaries (caturo dhammuddesā), bringing the king to deep insight into the impermanence and deficiency of all existence.

---

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Craving as the Cause of Rebirth (*Taṇhā Ponobhavikā* & *Bhava-Netti*)

Taṇhā is defined as the Noble Truth of the Origin of Suffering (dukkha-samudaya-ariya-sacca). It functions as bhava-netti—the conduit or lead-rope of existence that pushes bhava forward. It is described as ponobhavikā (leading to renewed existence) because its inherent nature is to lean forward into new states of becoming due to felt deficiency (ūṇo).

2. The Power of the Thought of Renunciation (*Nekkhamma-Saṅkappa*)

Venerable Raṭṭhapāla’s life showcases the absolute resolve required for genuine renunciation. His refusal to compromise—exemplified by his hunger strike—demonstrates that true faith (saddhā) generates an unyielding drive (chanda) to practice monastic observances without lingering attached to household ties.

3. Refutation of Worldly Motivations for Renunciation (*Pārijunñatā*)

King Koravya cited four conventional reasons why people renounce the world:

1. Loss through aging (jarā-pārijunñatā)

2. Loss through sickness (vyādhi-pārijunñatā)

3. Loss of wealth (bhoga-pārijunñatā)

4. Loss of relatives (ñāti-pārijunñatā)

Venerable Raṭṭhapāla clarifies that noble renunciation does not stem from external loss or helplessness, but from penetrating the true nature of existence through the Four Dhamma Summaries (caturo dhammuddesā).

4. The Four Dhamma Summaries (*Caturo Dhammuddesā*)

1st Summary: Upanīyati loko addhuvo — "The world is swept away, it is impermanent." Aging and time constantly lead the body forward; youth and vitality cannot be permanently preserved.

2nd Summary: Attāṇo loko anabhissaro — "The world is without shelter, without a lord." Neither armies nor divine powers can shield a person from physical agony or death; one must bear one's own suffering (kamma) alone.

3rd Summary: Assako loko sabbaṃ pahāya gamanīyaṃ — "The world has nothing of one's own; one has to go leaving everything behind." All hoarded wealth, gold, and possessions must be abandoned at death.

4th Summary: Ūṇo loko atitto taṇhā-dāso — "The world is deficient, insatiable, and enslaved to craving." No amount of domain, conquest, or sensory enjoyment can fulfill a person; craving keeps the mind perpetually lacking.

5. The Simile of the Hunter’s Bait (*Āmiṣa*)

Sense-pleasures (kāma) are like a hunter’s bait set to trap wild animals. Fools embellish the body—a bag of skin, bones, and filth—with jewels and robes, falling into the trap. The liberated Arahant consumes necessary alms (the bait) to sustain the physical frame but departs free, without being caught in the trap of attachment.

6. The Superiority of Wisdom over Wealth (*Tasmā Hi Paññāva Dhanena Seyyo*)

Wealth cannot ward off aging, sickness, or rebirth, nor can it buy liberation. Beings commit evil deeds out of delusion to protect wealth, only to suffer punishment in saṃsāra like a thief caught in the act. Thus, wisdom (paññā) is far superior to wealth, as wisdom alone brings an end to Dukkha.

7. Mechanics of Rebirth: *Taṇhā* as Fuel and the Fire Simile

Addressing the question of what connects one life to the next, Ven. Nanananda highlights the Buddha's response to Vacchagotta: just as a forest fire spreads from tree to tree propelled by the wind, taṇhā acts as the fuel/grasping (upādāna) and wind that propels consciousness (viññāṇa) to grasp a new name-and-form (nāmarūpa) across births without requiring a physical substrate to travel.

8. The Interdependent Rotation of *Viññāṇa* and *Nāmarūpa

Existence is maintained by a continuous mutual feedback loop between consciousness (viññāṇa) and name-and-form (nāmarūpa). At death, governed by kamma, taṇhā firmly grasps the projected mental sign (kamma-nimitta or gati-nimitta). Breaking the conduit of taṇhā stops this rotation and ends bhava.

9. Clarification on Desiring Nibbāna vs. Worldly Craving

The sermon refutes the common philosophical objection: "Is not wishing for Nibbāna also a form of craving?" Worldly craving (taṇhā) is like adding straw to a fire—it continuously demands new objects and perpetuates deficiency. Seeking Nibbāna means aiming directly for taṇhakkhaya (the destruction of craving). Desiring the destruction of craving systematically reduces craving step-by-step until it is completely extinguished.

10. Nibbāna as the Subduing of Thirst (*Pipāsa-Vinaya*) and the Well Simile

Drawing from the Udāna verse spoken at the village of Thūna (Kiṃ kayirā udapānena, āpā ce sabbadā siyuṃ...), the Buddha explains that once thirst is cut off at the root (taṇhāya mūlato chetvā), there is no longer any search or need for a well (sensory objects/existence). Nibbāna is the cool, unagitated state where mental thirst has been permanently appeased.
