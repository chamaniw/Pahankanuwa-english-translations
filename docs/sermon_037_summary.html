Sermon 037 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #37 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #37 (Pahankaṇuwa Sinhala Audio Sermon No. 146)

Primary Theme: The shattering of the illusion of permanence (nicca-saññā) and self-illusion (sakkāya-diṭṭhi) through the Buddha’s "Lion’s Roar" (sīhanāda); the experiential practice of the perception of impermanence (anicca-saññā); and the deep analytical breakdown of how Consciousness (viññāṇa) and Name-and-Form (nāmarūpa) mutually condition the five aggregates of grasping (pañcupādānakkhandha) within Dependent Arising (paṭiccasamuppāda).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The Buddha, long-lived celestial beings (Devas and Brahmas), and specifically Baka Brahma.

The Interaction: Following the Buddha setting in motion the Wheel of Dhamma (Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta) at Isipatana Migadāya, the discourse reverberates across all deva and brahma realms. In a related setup, the Buddha perceives from Jetavana that Baka Brahma has fallen into delusion and uses psychic power to confront him in the Brahma realm.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Celestial beings living for aeons become intoxicated with life (jīvita-mada) and enticed by the perception of permanence (nicca-saññā), mistakenly believing their high mansions and existence to be eternal and unshifting. The Buddha’s introduction of the Sīhanāda (Lion’s Roar) declaring the Four Noble Truths and the impermanence of all five aggregates exposes their fundamental vulnerability to sakkāya-diṭṭhi, triggering profound fear, dismay, and spiritual urgency (saṃvega).

The Result / Resolution: Baka Brahma’s conceit (māna) and wrong views (micchā-diṭṭhi) are dismantled when the Buddha reveals his forgotten past karmic cause; the devas recognize that they have not transcended the existing group (sakkāya), abandoning their delusions of eternity to pursue true release in Nibbāna.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Lion’s Roar (Sīhanāda) and Deva-Saṃvega:

Just as wild forest beasts panic upon hearing the lion's roar, long-lived devas and Brahmas—deluded by their aeon-long lifespans—are overcome with spiritual urgency (saṃvega) and terror when the Buddha proclaims the impermanence (aniccata) of Form, Feeling, Perception, Preparations, and Consciousness. They realize that despite their grand mansions, they remain caught in self-illusion (sakkāya-diṭṭhi).

2. Deconstruction of Sakkāya (The Existing Group):

Sakkāya (sat = existing/being; kāya = group/collective) refers directly to the five aggregates of grasping (pañcupādānakkhandha). It is seized as compact (ghana-saññā) and labeled "I" and "mine" due to ignorance of its constantly disintegrating nature.

3. The Interconnectedness of the Three Characteristics (Tilakkhaṇa):

Based on the principle "yad aniccaṃ taṃ dukkhaṃ, yaṃ dukkhaṃ tad anattā" ("what is impermanent is suffering; what is suffering is not-self"), the text clarifies that grasping impermanent things yields suffering. This is illustrated by the Simile of the River (nadī upamā), where a man swept away by a rapid mountain current grabs leaning rushes and grasses on the bank; because those vegetation links break away, the man falls into ruin.

4. The 20-Fold Dogma of Self-Illusion (Sakkāya-Diṭṭhi):

Unwillingness to let go leads the uninstructed worldling to cling to each aggregate in four ways (e.g., regarding Form as self, self as possessing Form, Form as in self, or self as in Form). Multiplying these four modes across the five aggregates yields the twenty wrong views that are shattered upon attaining Stream-entry (sotāpatti-phala).

5. The Supreme Efficacy of the Perception of Impermanence (Anicca-Saññā):

Systematic cultivation of anicca-saññā completely uproots sensuality-lust (kāma-rāga), form-lust (rūpa-rāga), becoming-lust (bhava-rāga), ignorance (avijjā), and the fundamental conceit "I am" (asmi-māna). The Buddha reinforces this with three key similes:

The Great Plow: Uprooting all creeping roots in a autumn field.

The Severed Mango Stalk: Detaching every attached mango at once.

The Autumn Sun: Dispelling all darkness upon rising into a cloudless sky.

6. Not-Self (Anattā) and Non-Control:

As taught in the Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta, if aggregates were truly "self," they would not lead to affliction/sickness, and one could exercise absolute command ("Let my form be thus..."). Because aggregates are non-self (anattā), they cannot be controlled and must be relinquished by abandoning the threefold graspings: etaṃ mama (this is mine — via craving/ taṇhā), eso ham asmi (this I am — via conceit/ māna), and eso me attā (this is my self — via view/ diṭṭhi).

7. Conditional Origins of the Aggregates & Their Analytical Similes:

Each aggregate arises solely dependent on specific conditions (hetu-paccaya):

Form (Rūpa): Conditioned by the Four Great Elements (cattāro mahābhūtā); deceptive like a lump of foam.

Feeling (Vedanā): Conditioned by Contact (phassa); fleeting like a water bubble.

Perception (Saññā): Conditioned by Contact (phassa); deceptive like a mirage.

Preparations (Saṅkhāra): Conditioned by Contact (phassa); coreless like a banana tree trunk.

Consciousness (Viññāṇa): Conditioned by Name-and-Form (nāmarūpa); deceptive like a magic illusion (māyā).

8. Reciprocal Relationship Between Consciousness (Viññāṇa) and Name-and-Form (Nāmarūpa):

Addressing a common scholarly error, Ven. Nanananda Thero clarifies that Nāmarūpa is not simply a synonym for all four mental aggregates. In Dependent Arising, Viññāṇa and Nāmarūpa exist in a mutual, dyadic conditional loop (viññāṇapaccayā nāmarūpaṃ, nāmarūpapaccayā viññāṇaṃ). Nāma comprises feeling, perception, volition, contact, and attention; Rūpa is the physical perception built around the four great elements. Together, they function like a self-referential whirlpool or reflection.

9. The Deceptive Nature of Consciousness and the Dog on the Footbridge:

Consciousness possesses an internal reflective capacity (paccavekkhaṇā). Unaware of this, worldlings act like a dog walking on a footbridge who growls or wags its tail at its own reflection in the water, mistaking it for another dog. Realizing that the object is merely a projected shadow composed of name-factors and form-factors breaks the magic illusion (māyā) of consciousness.

10. The Factory of the Six Sense-Spheres (Saḷāyatana):

Consciousness does not reside statically inside sense organs; rather, consciousness comes into being dependent on a dyad (dvayaṃ paṭicca)—such as eye and form yielding eye-consciousness. Sense organs become active "spheres" (āyatana) only when conditions align (like a match striking a matchbox). Ignorance within this process leads to accumulation (upacaya) of the aggregates, whereas knowledge of things as they are (yathābhūta-ñāṇa) leads to their wearing away (apacaya).

11. The Instability of Conditions in the Aniccatā Sutta:

The sermon highlights the Aniccatā Sutta, which induces the concentration of impermanence (aniccata-samādhi). It demonstrates that because the underlying conditions (eye and form) are continuously shaking and impermanent, the resulting eye-consciousness, eye-contact, and feeling cannot be permanent. Recognizing the radical instability of the underlying conditions instantly undermines the illusion of a solid consciousness.

12. The Deconstructive Definition of Fabrications (Saṅkhāra):

Saṅkhāra refers to artificial fabrications, make-up, or stage props designed to maintain a theatrical illusion. Expressed through body (kāya-saṅkhāra), speech (vacī-saṅkhāra), and mind (citta-saṅkhāra), these fabrications keep the spinning whirlpool of bhava alive. When wise attention (yoniso manasikāra) exposes the corelessness of nāmarūpa, consciousness ceases to be deluded, stopping the whirlpool and realizing Nibbāna.
