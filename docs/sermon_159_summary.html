Sermon 159 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #159 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #159

Primary Theme: Redefining the true meaning of a "bearer of the Dhamma" (Dhamma-dhara)—moving beyond mere intellectual erudition (bahussuta), superficial recitation, or modern digitized Tipitaka storage to direct, experiential realization (paṭivedha) and practice (paṭipatti) of the Four Noble Truths and Dependent Arising (paṭiccasamuppāda).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Ven. Ekudaniya Thera, an Arahat Elder who lived alone in a forest hermitage and possessed only a single verse/exultation (udāna).

The Interaction: On Uposatha days, Ekudaniya Thera would ascend the Dhamma-seat and deliver his single verse as a sermon to the local forest deities, who would respond with a thunderous roar of appreciation (Sādhu). One Uposatha day, two renowned masters of the Three Pitakas (Tipiṭakadhara) arrived with a retinue of 1,000 monks (500 each). Ekudaniya invited them to preach to the deities.

The Disaster / Turning Point: The two learned Theras gave an extensive, grand sermon, yet not a single deity uttered a word of Sādhu. Perplexed, they asked Ekudaniya to preach. When Ekudaniya recited his single verse, the deities raised a massive roar of Sādhu that echoed through the forest. The visiting monks grew critical, accusing the forest deities of partiality ("looking at faces").

The Result / Resolution: The monks reported the incident to the Buddha at Jetavana. The Buddha corrected them, declaring that one does not become a Dhammadhara merely by speaking much (bahubhāsati), but by touching and realizing the Dhamma directly with the body (kāyena passati) even if one has heard only a little (appampi sutvāna), and remaining non-negligent (nappamajjati).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. True Meaning of Dhamma-dhara vs. Intellectual Erudition (Bahussuta)

The ordinary world equates a Dhamma-dhara with one who has memorized the Tipitaka or holds academic qualifications. In the modern era, carrying a digitized Tipitaka on a computer technically fits this worldly definition.

The Buddha's definition requires internal vision/seeing (kāyena passati) through meditative practice (paṭipatti) and penetration (paṭivedha), emphasizing that direct realization is infinitely superior to mere external knowledge.

2. Exegesis of Ven. Ekudaniya’s Udāna Verse

The verse (Adhicetaso appamajjato munino...) outlines the state of an Arahat: endowed with adhicitta (the highest concentration of Arahatship fruit, arahatta-phala-samādhi), diligent (appamajjato), training in the paths of the sage (monapathesu sikkhato), unshakeable before the eight worldly conditions (tādī), completely peaceful (upasanta), and ever-mindful (sadā satīmato). The deities rejoiced because the verse reflected his direct, internal attainment.

3. The Medical Framework of the Four Noble Truths (Caturāriya-sacca)

The Buddha presents Himself as an incomparable supreme surgeon/physician. The Four Noble Truths parallel clinical medical science: Dukkha (the disease), Samudaya (the cause/diagnosis), Nirodha (health/cure), and Magga (the treatment/medicine). True Dhamma-medicine treats the root cause rather than merely managing symptoms.

4. Essential Definition of Dukkha and Samudaya

While worldly suffering (aging, sickness, death) is obvious, the ultimate summary of suffering is saṅkhittena pañcupādānakkhandhā dukkhā (in short, the five aggregates of grasping are suffering).

The cause (Samudaya) is craving/thirst (taṇhā), which directly conditions grasping (taṇhāpaccayā upādānaṃ) in the chain of Dependent Arising.

5. Nibbāna as Extinction of Craving (Taṇhakkhayo) and Cessation of Becoming (Bhava-nirodha)

Nibbāna is not an alien world or a feeling-bliss (vedanā), but the cooling/extinguishment (taṇhakkhayo) right where craving is destroyed. When craving ceases, grasping (upādāna) naturally slips away, resulting in the cessation of existence/becoming (bhava-nirodha).

6. Experiential Realization of the Stream-enterer (Sotāpanna)

Stream-entry (Sotāpatti-magga) is the direct realization that "whatever is of the nature to arise, all that is of the nature to cease" (yaṃ kiñci samudayadhammaṃ sabbaṃ taṃ nirodhadhammaṃ). The noble method is well-seen and penetrated with wisdom (ariyo ñāyo paññāya sudiṭṭho, suppaṭividdho), dismantling the illusion of a compact entity (ekaghana) or permanent soul.

7. The Eight Titles of a Sotāpanna (The Case of Suppabuddha the Leper)

A Sotāpanna gains eight titles: diṭṭhadhammo (seen the Dhamma), pattadhammo (reached the Dhamma), viditadhammo (understood the Dhamma), pariyogāḷhadhammo (plunged into the Dhamma), tiṇṇavicikiccho (crossed over doubt), vigatakathaṅkatho (freed from perplexity), vesārajjappatto (attained fearlessness), and aparappaccayo satthusāsane (independent of others in the Teacher's dispensation). The Buddha bestowed these prestigious titles upon Suppabuddha, an impoverished leper, proving that spiritual attainment depends on direct insight rather than academic or social status.

8. The Simile of the Cowherd (Gopālaka-upamā)

Quoting the Dhammapada, the Thero highlights the Buddha’s critique of one who recites the Tipitaka without practicing it: such a person is like a hired cowherd who counts another's cows all day but never tastes the five products of the cow (pañca-gorasa—milk, curd, butter, buttermilk, ghee).

9. The Simile of the Man Born Blind (Jātyandha) in the Māgandiya Sutta

A blind man tricked into wearing a dirty, oil-stained rag under the belief that it is a pristine white cloth represents an uninstructed worldling clinging to the five aggregates of grasping due to self-illusion (sakkāya-diṭṭhi). When cured by a surgeon (gaining the Dhamma-eye), he sees the rag's repulsiveness with revulsion. Similarly, gaining the Dhamma-eye brings disenchantment (nibbidā) with the five aggregates, leading to letting go.

10. The Underlying Principle of Dependent Arising (Paṭiccasamuppāda)

The core of Dependent Arising is not merely reciting the 12 linear factors, but grasping the algebraic, reciprocal principle: Imasmiṃ sati idaṃ hoti... ("When this is, this is..."). Illustrated via Ven. Moliya-phagguna’s questions and Ven. Sāriputta's simile of two leaning sheaves of reeds (naḷakalāpī), it demonstrates cause and condition (hetu-paccaya) without an enduring personal agent or soul.

11. The Four Analytical Knowledges (Paṭisambhidā)

Deep preservation and teaching of Dhamma require the four analytical knowledges: Attha (understanding the problem/meaning), Dhamma (understanding the law/cause), Nirutti (precision in linguistic definition), and Paṭibhāna (intuitive analytical perspicuity/acumen).

12. Interdependence of the Threefold Dispensation (Sāsana)

The Dispensation consists of learning (pariyatti), practice (paṭipatti), and penetration/realization (paṭivedha). Pariyatti exists for paṭipatti, and paṭipatti for paṭivedha. Separating them or relying solely on external technological preservation without meditative realization leads to linguistic distortion, intellectualization, and the internal decline of the Sāsana.
