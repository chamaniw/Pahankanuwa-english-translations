Sermon 167 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #167 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #167 (Sinhala Audio No. 108)

Primary Theme: Aligning external religious mobilization (paṭipatti) with internal spiritual purpose: elevating eightfold Uposatha observance from saṃsāric continuation (saṃvaṭṭagāmī kusala) to Arahant-level renunciation (nekkhamma) and liberation (vivaṭṭagāmī kusala) to escape the relentless driving force of decay and death (jarāmaraṇa).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The great lay benefactress Visākhā and a group of five hundred laywomen categorized across four age demographics (elderly, middle-aged, young women, and young girls).

The Interaction: On a Full Moon Uposatha day at Pubbārāma monastery, Visākhā asks each group why they observe the eight Uposatha precepts:

Elderly women: To be reborn in heavenly worlds.

Middle-aged women: To escape living with co-wives (sapatnī vāsa) resulting from polygamy.

Young women: To bear a son from their very first pregnancy.

Young girls: To get married while still young.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Recognizing that all four groups were practicing Uposatha for mundane, saṃsāric goals, Visākhā brings the laywomen before the Buddha and reports their answers. The Buddha highlights the tragedy of worldlings who continuously spin within saṃsāra without seeking an exit, uttering Dhammapada verse 135:

> "Yathā daṇḍena gopālo, gāvo pāleti gocaraṃ; Evaṃ jarā ca maccū ca, āyuṃ pājenti pāṇinaṃ."

> ("Just as a cowherd drives the cows to the pasture with a staff, even so do decay and death drive the lifespan of beings towards the mouth of death.")

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha clarifies the authentic standard of the eightfold Uposatha: it is not a ritual for worldly gain, but a temporary practice of living at the level of the Arahants—a vital stepping stone (nekkhamma) and rehearsal for monastic renunciation and full liberation from saṃsāra.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Saṃvaṭṭagāmī vs. Vivaṭṭagāmī Kusala

The Thero distinguishes between wholesome actions aimed at saṃsāric perpetuation (saṃvaṭṭagāmī kusala), which lead to heavenly rebirths or worldly resolution but keep one bound to suffering, and wholesome actions aimed at ending saṃsāra (vivaṭṭagāmī kusala), which are rooted in non-clinging (aparāmaṭṭha sīla) and directed toward Nibbāna.

2. Uposatha as the Standard of the Arahants

The eight Uposatha precepts are not merely moral constraints for laypeople; they represent a temporary adoption of the lifestyle and mental posture of Arahants. Abstaining from unchastity (abrahmacariya), untimely meals (vikāla-bhojana), entertainment, adornment, and luxurious beds mimics full monastic renunciation (nekkhamma) and serves as a direct rehearsal for leaving household life.

3. Allegory of the Cowherd, the Staff, and the Pasture (Dhammapada 135)

Māra (Death/Maccu) acts as the cowherd, using the staff of decay (jarā) to drive the lifespan (āyu) of all beings across seven life stages (infancy/weaning, toddlerhood/crawling, schooling/examinations, adult pairing/marriage, middle age, old age/walking stick, and death). The final pasture is the cemetery, where the body cage becomes food for worms, dogs, and crows.

4. The Mouse Trap Simile and the Exit of Mindfulness

Lay life is compared to a mouse running frantically inside a trap. As long as it runs hectically, it cannot see the exit. The Uposatha day provides a necessary pause within busy lay life. Pausing allows one to calm the mind and apply it to the Four Foundations of Mindfulness (cattāro satipaṭṭhānā), which is the only true exit from the mouse trap of saṃsāra.

5. The Caterpillar and Butterfly Metaphor for Transformation

Observing Uposatha with high aspirations is like a caterpillar hanging under a leaf before transforming into a beautiful butterfly. Laypersons should use Uposatha to gradually loosen the coarse bonds of household life, eventually breaking free to take up the bowl and robes like a butterfly hovering over flowers without damaging them.

6. The Saṃsāric Merry-Go-Round

Beings in saṃsāra are like children riding a wooden horse on a merry-go-round, intoxicated by fivefold instrumental music (the five cords of sensual pleasure). Although the ride repeatedly spins through birth, decay, sickness, and death (jāti, jarā, vyādhi, maraṇa), deluded worldlings continuously cry out for "another round" instead of jumping off.

7. Perpetual Calamities vs. Temporal Disasters (King Pasenadi's Simile)

Temporal disasters like floods, tsunamis, and famines are temporary crises that ignorance (avijjā) quickly forgets. Drawing from King Pasenadi’s sutta, the Thero emphasizes that decay (jarā) and death (maraṇa) are four giant rolling mountains crushing all beings continuously. Against these true perpetual calamities (sanātana dharmatā), armies, wealth, and strategic conspiracies are useless; the only refuge is righteous and harmonious living (dhamma-cariya, sama-cariya).

8. Ignorance (Avijjā) and Craving (Taṇhā) as Saṃsāric Parents

The root cause of endless wandering is summarized as the hindrance of ignorance (avijjā-nīvaraṇa) and the fetter of craving (taṇhā-saṃyojana). Ignorance is fundamentally the delusion that "there is something" (a real self/substance), while craving is the persistent feeling that "it is not enough." Servicing these two saṃsāric "parents" only increases suffering (dukkha).

9. Deconstructing Compact Perception (Ghana-Saññā) through Insight

The ordinary worldling clings to existence through self-illusion (sakkāya-diṭṭhi) and the perception of permanence (nicca-saññā). By developing insight-wisdom (vipassanā-paññā), one sees beyond logical reasoning (atakkāvacara) to perceive that the five aggregates (pañcakkhandha) are merely an extremely rapid flow of arisings and passings away, revealing the essential emptiness (suññatā) of all preparations (saṅkhāra).

10. Mindfulness of Breathing (Ānāpānasati) and the Asthmatic Simile

Living beings (prāṇīn) survive on in-breaths and out-breaths, continuously grasping external air and expelling it. The Thero notes that while we only perceive breathing as suffering when watching an asthmatic patient, all beings are effectively asthmatic patients struggling for air. Ānāpānasati exposes this basic biological process as a heap of transient, selfless preparations (saṅkhāra-puñja), allowing the meditator to drop the conceit of "I" and "mine."
