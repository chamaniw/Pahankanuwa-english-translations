Sermon 022 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #22 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #22

Primary Theme: The essential necessity of practical application (pakkubbato) over mere intellectual study or passive contemplation, demonstrating how the well-spoken word (subhāsitā vācā) yields its true fragrance and fruit only through balanced, energetic striving (majjhimā paṭipadā) within the Threefold Training.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Chattapāni Upāsaka (an Anāgāmī lay disciple in Sāvatthī), King Pasenadi of Kosala, Queen Mallikā, Queen Vāsabhakhattiyā, Ven. Ānanda, and the Supreme Buddha.

The Interaction: Chattapāni Upāsaka was sitting at the monastery, attentively listening to a Dhamma discussion with the Buddha, when King Pasenadi of Kosala arrived.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Recognizing the Buddha as the Supreme King of Kings, Chattapāni chose not to stand up to honor King Pasenadi, provoking the king's initial resentment. Later, when summoned to the royal palace, Chattapāni respectfully explained that rising before a regional ruler in the presence of the Supreme Teacher would be disrespectful. Reconciled, the king requested Chattapāni to teach the Dhamma to the royal harem. Chattapāni far-sightedly declined due to the political entanglements and moral risks involved for laypeople, suggesting that an ordained monk be assigned instead.

The Result / Resolution: The king approached the Buddha, who assigned Ven. Ānanda to teach the queens. While Queen Mallikā learned and practiced diligently, Queen Vāsabhakhattiyā failed to learn properly. Prompted by this contrast, the Buddha uttered Verses 51–52 of the Dhammapada, declaring that the Dhamma is like a beautiful flower: fruitless and scentless to those who do not practice it, but deeply fragrant and fruitful to those who put it into practice.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Analogy of the Flower (Dhammapada Verses 51–52):

The well-spoken Dhamma (subhāsitā vācā) remains fruitless (aphalā) for one who does not practice it (akubbato), resembling a colorful but scentless flower (vaṇṇavantaṃ agandhakaṃ). Conversely, it becomes highly fruitful (saphalā) for one who puts it into practice (pakkubbato), like a flower that is both vibrant in color and rich in fragrance (vaṇṇavantaṃ sagandhakaṃ).

2. Dhammic Integrity and Far-Sightedness:

Chattapāni Upāsaka exemplifies spiritual strength by prioritizing respect for the Dhamma above deference to royal power. His refusal to accept a royal invitation to teach the harem demonstrates far-sighted wisdom, recognizing the spiritual dangers of seeking worldly status and favors.

3. The Six Virtues of the Dhamma (Dhammaguṇa):

The Dhamma contains an implicit challenge to practice:

Svākkhāta: Well-expounded (providing comfort to the energetic practitioner, vīriyavanta, whereas a poorly expounded teaching, durakkhāta, comforts the lazy, kusīta).

Sandiṭṭhika: Directly visible in this life.

Akālika: Immediate and timeless, leaving no time gap between the Path and its Fruit.

Ehipassika: Inviting one to "come and see."

Opanayika: Leading directly inward to the heart.

Paccattaṃ veditabbo viññūhi: To be personally experienced by the wise.

4. Refutation of Passive "Doing Nothing" in Meditation:

The sermon critiques the modern misconception that meditation consists of sitting idly and merely "watching the mind wander." The Buddha’s Middle Way (Majjhimā Paṭipadā) is not passivity; it requires active, systematic, and balanced energy (vīriya) to direct and dam the dynamic flow of consciousness (citta-santāna).

5. The Four Great Efforts (Sammappadhāna) and the River Analogy:

Managing the flowing stream of the mind is compared to building a dam across a swiftly moving river, requiring four distinct modes of exertion:

Saṃvara: Restraint against the non-arising of unarisen unwholesome states.

Pahāna: Abandonment of arisen unwholesome states.

Bhāvanā: Cultivation and development of unarisen wholesome states.

Anurakkhaṇa: Maintenance, guarding, and maturation of arisen wholesome states.

6. The Ascending Sequence of Striving:

The Buddha describes a precise dynamic progression of energy required for ultimate realization:

Chandaṃ janeti: Generating intent and interest.

Vāyamati: Making a preliminary testing effort.

Viriyaṃ ārabhati: Initiating active work and energy.

Cittaṃ paggaṇhāti: Upholding and firming the mind against obstacles.

Padahati: Applying supreme, ultimate exertion (padhāna) even at the risk of one's life.

7. The Four Bases of Success (Iddhipāda):

Spiritual achievement relies on four bases tailored to individual temperaments:

Chanda: Intention/interest-driven temperament.

Viriya: Energy-driven temperament.

Citta: Mental determination-driven temperament.

Vīmaṃsā: Investigative wisdom-driven temperament.

8. Refutation of Modern Rationalism and Illusion of "Instant Realization":

The sermon cautions against intellectual arrogance and theoretical scholarship. Swift realization (Sukhā paṭipadā khippābhiññā), such as that experienced by Bāhiya Dārucīriya, is rare and depends on vast past merit (pāramī). The standard dispensation operates via gradual training, gradual practice, and gradual progress (anupubbakiriya, anupubbapaṭipadā).

9. Crossing the Floods (Oghataraṇa Sutta):

Crossing the four oceanic floods of sensuality (kāmogha), existence (bhavogha), views (diṭṭhogha), and ignorance (avijjogha) requires navigating between two extremes:

Appatiṭṭhaṃ (without standing still / halting): Avoiding sensual indulgence (kāmasukhallikānuyoga), which causes one to sink.

Anāyūhaṃ (without struggling / straining): Avoiding self-mortification (attakilamathānuyoga), which causes one to be swept away.

10. Restraint through Knowledge (Ñāṇa-Saṃvara) in Daily Requisites:

Unlike extreme ascetics (such as the Nigaṇṭhas, who resorted to nudity or eating filth out of fear of sensuality), the Buddha instituted the Middle Path. Monks use the four requisites (paccayā) with wise reflection (paccavekkhaṇa), employing techniques like element analysis (dhātu-manasikāra) to convert physical consumption (almsfood) into a meditative aid for Nibbāna.

11. The Sequential Necessity of Faith (Saddhā) and Morality (Sīla):

Realization cannot bypass foundational virtues. Faith (Saddhā) is the indispensable seed without which no virtues grow. Upon faith rests Morality (Sīla), which stabilizes Concentration (Samādhi), which in turn yields Insight Wisdom (Paññā) into impermanence (anicca), suffering (dukkha), and non-self (anattā).

12. The Proper Mode of Dhamma-Listening:

Effective listening requires deep personal engagement rather than modern mechanical recording or academic analysis. The noble disciple listens through four steps:

Sutā, dhatā, vacasā paricitā, diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā: Heard, retained, verbally mastered, and thoroughly penetrated with right view.

Aṭṭhikatvā manasikatvā sabbacetaso samannāharitvā ohitasoto dhammaṃ suṇāti: Listening with personal purpose, reflecting word-by-word, dedicating the entire mind, and inclining the ears directly toward internal realization.
