Sermon 068 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #68 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #68

Primary Theme: The essential distinction between wrong view (micchā-diṭṭhi) and right view (sammā-diṭṭhi), the detailed breakdown of mundane versus supramundane right view, and the direct realization of non-self (anattā) and Dependent Arising (paṭicca-samuppāda) through satipaṭṭhāna meditation to transcend saṃsāric suffering.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: A group of children of wrong-view (micchā-diṭṭhi) parents living in Sāvatthī, playing alongside the children of Buddhist lay devotees (Upāsakas); their parents; and the Supreme Buddha.

The Interaction: The non-Buddhist parents strictly forbade their children from bowing down to the Sangha or entering Buddhist monasteries. While playing near the gate of Jetavana Monastery, the children became thirsty and requested a Buddhist child to go inside to fetch water.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Upon entering, the child informed the Buddha, who used His wisdom of skillful means (upāya-kosalla-ñāṇa). He instructed the child not to carry water out, but to invite the other children inside. When they arrived, the Buddha made them drink the "nectar of the Deathless" (amā-pæn) by teaching them Dhamma, establishing them in unshakeable faith (saddhā) and the refuges and precepts (saraṇa-sīla). Upon learning of this, their parents wept and lamented, believing their children had fallen into a dangerous wrong view.

The Result / Resolution: Wise neighbors persuaded the distressed parents to visit the Buddha. Handing the children over to the Ascetic Gotama, the parents also listened to a tailored Dhamma sermon. Their faith was inspired, turning them into devoted lay followers (Upāsakas). By repeatedly returning to hear the Dhamma, all the parents eventually attained the fruit of Stream-entry (Sotāpanna-phala).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Definition and Operational Mechanism of Views (Diṭṭhi):

Wrong view (micchā-diṭṭhi) is characterized by perceiving fault in the faultless (avajje vajjamatino) and faultlessness in the faulty (vajje ca abajjadassino), inevitably leading to states of misery (duggati). Conversely, right view (sammā-diṭṭhi) sees the faulty as faulty and the faultless as faultless, guiding beings to happy states (suggati) and Nibbāna.

2. The Grave Danger of Wrong View (Micchā-diṭṭhi):

The Buddha described wrong view as the single most detrimental condition in the world. Using the simile of a neem (nimba) or bitter-gourd seed, whatever moisture and soil nutrients a person with wrong view absorbs turn entirely bitter; likewise, all bodily, verbal, and mental volitional activities (saṅkhāra), intentions, and preparations under wrong view lead exclusively to harm and suffering.

3. Categorization and Refutation of Historical Wrong Views:

The Brahmajāla Sutta: Serves as a "Supreme Net" (diṭṭhi-jāla) detailing all 62 wrong views that arise in the world.

Proponents of Non-Action (Akiriyavāda): Teachers such as Makkhali Gosāla promoted determinism (niyativāda)—asserting that beings purified or defiled without cause or condition (ahētu-appaccaya), rolling through saṃsāra like a ball of string unwinding until suffering spontaneously ends. The Buddha likened Makkhali Gosāla to a net cast at a river mouth for the destruction of fish.

4. Refutation of Past-Kamma Extremism (Nigaṇṭha Nātaputta):

The opinion that every present experience of pleasure or pain is strictly due to past kamma, and that past kamma must be worn out through self-mortification (attakilamathānuyoga), ultimately degenerates into non-action (akiriyavāda). It denies human capacity, effort (vāyāma), energy (viriya), and the internal desire-to-act (chanda).

5. Karmic Consequence of Extremist Ascetic Vows:

In the encounter with Puṇṇa (who practiced the ox-duty, gova-vatta) and Seṇiya (who practiced the dog-duty, kukkura-vatta), the Buddha revealed that long-term development of animal habits and minds (kukkura-citta / go-citta) results in rebirth as that animal; if coupled with the wrong view that such vows lead to devahood, it leads straight to hell (niraya). Liberation came when they grasped the principle of kamma ownership (kammassakā sattā).

6. Analytical Distinction Between Merit (Puñña) and Wholesomeness (Kusala):

While "merit" (puñña) typically emphasizes pleasant karmic results (vipāka), "wholesomeness" (kusala) signifies profound mental skillfulness and psychological detachment (veramaṇī—abstinence). The Tipitaka speaks of the roots of wholesomeness (kusala-mūla: alobha, adosa, amoha), but never refers to "roots of merit" (puñña-mūla), underscoring kusala as the active reversal of saṃsāric mental habits.

7. The Twofold Classification of Right View (Dvaya-sammā-diṭṭhi):

Mundane Right View (Kammassakatā sammā-diṭṭhi): Accompanied by taints (sāsavā), siding with merit (puññabhāgiyā), and maturing in worldly assets (upadhivepakkā). It prevents downfall into miserable realms and secures favorable saṃsāric rebirths.

Supramundane Right View (Magga sammā-diṭṭhi): Noble (ariyā), taintless (anāsavā), and supramundane (lokuttarā). It is a direct Path factor (maggāṅga) that penetrates non-self (anattā) and the Four Noble Truths.

8. Comprehensive Understanding of the Truth of Suffering (Dukkha-Sacca):

Suffering is not merely restricted to birth, aging, sickness, and death. In brief, the entirety of the five aggregates of grasping (pañcupādānakkhandha) is purely a mass of stress/suffering (kevalaṃ dukkhakkhandha) that must be fully comprehended (pariññeyya).

9. The Middle Way (Majjhimā Paṭipadā) as Dependent Arising (Paṭicca-Samuppāda):

Beyond moral extremes, the true Middle Way declared to Kaccāyana avoids both the extreme of eternal existence ("everything exists," atthitā) and annihilationism ("everything does not exist," nātthitā). It views the world strictly as a process of cause-and-effect arisings and ceasings (samudaya-nirodha / udaya-vaya).

10. Deconstruction of Solidity (Ghana-Saññā) through Mindfulness (Satipaṭṭhāna):

Direct realization of the Dhamma is beyond logic (atakkāvacara) and cannot be measured by physical instruments or mere book-learning. By systematically practicing the Four Establishments of Mindfulness (satipaṭṭhāna)—such as observing the bare filling and emptying of the breath (ānāpānasati)—the illusion of a solid self (ghana-saññā) breaks down, giving rise to disillusionment (nibbidā), equanimity (upekkhā), and the breakthrough knowledge: "whatever is of the nature to arise, all that is of the nature to cease" (yaṃ kiñci samudayadhammaṃ sabbaṃ taṃ nirodhadhammaṃ).
