Sermon 020 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #20 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #20

Primary Theme: Deconstructing the illusion of the physical body (kāya) and the five aggregates (pañcakkhandha) through insight contemplation (vipassanā)—specifically viewing them as empty, substanceless similes like foam and mirages—to cut off the enticeful arrows of defilements (kilesa-māra) and attain the Deathless (Amata) through letting go.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: A certain unnamed monk who had received a individual meditation subject (kammaṭṭhāna) directly from the Buddha.

The Interaction: After obtaining his meditation subject, the monk retreated to a remote forest to practice insight meditation (vipassanā) with great effort.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Despite rigorous effort, the monk failed to attain Arahantship. Discouraged, he decided to travel back to the Buddha to clarify his practice. During his journey:

1. He saw a shimmering mirage in the summer heat and realized: "This body is like a mirage—appearing real from a distance, but substanceless upon closer examination."

2. While resting and bathing at the Achiravatī River, he observed frothy foam rapidly forming and dissolving in the swift current and contemplated: "This form (rūpa) of mine is like a lump of foam."

The Result / Resolution: Sensing the monk’s maturing insight through Buddha-cognition, the Buddha projected His image from the Perfumed Chamber (Gandhakuṭi) and uttered Dhammapada Verse 46 (Pheṇūpamaṃ kāyamimaṃ viditvā...). Validated and energized by this teaching, the monk’s insight knowledges fully matured, and he attained Arahantship along with the four analytical knowledges (paṭisambhidā).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Core Verse (Dhammapada 46):

Pheṇūpamaṃ kāyamimaṃ viditvā (Knowing this body to be like foam)

Marīcidhammaṃ abhisambudhāno (Comprehending its mirage-like nature)

Chetvāna mārassa papupphakāni (Cutting off the flower-tipped arrows of Māra)

Adassanaṃ maccurājassa gacche (Go beyond the sight of the King of Death).

2. The Nature of Form (Rūpa) as Foam (Pheṇūpamaṃ):

Due to the perception of compactness (ghana-saññā), the body appears solid and substantial from afar. In reality, it is formed by consumed food/drink boiling and foaming internally via the primary elements (dhātu: earth, water, fire, air), producing flesh, blood, bone, and excretions. Like foam trapped in a swift river current, it is an empty, rapidly arising, and dissolving mass.

3. The Mirage-Like Nature (Marīcidhammaṃ) and the Three Distortions (Vipallāsa):

Like a thirsty deer chasing a mirage based on a false view (diṭṭhi), worldly beings chase sense objects and body-sustenance due to three levels of cognitive distortion:

Distortion of Perception (saññā-vipallāsa)

Distortion of Mind (citta-vipallāsa)

Distortion of View (diṭṭhi-vipallāsa)

These distortions cause one to mistake the impermanent (anicca) as permanent (nicca), the foul (asubha) as beautiful (subha), suffering (dukkha) as pleasant (sukha), and non-self (anattā) as self (attā).

4. Cutting Off Māra’s Flower-Tipped Arrows (Mārassa Papupphakāni):

Defiled sense objects (kilesa) arriving at the six sense bases act as arrows tipped with attractive flowers. They tempt the mind with signs (nimitta) of beauty, pleasure, and ownership. Insight attention (vipassanā-manasikāra) acts as a blade to cut off this sticky attachment by penetrating sense objects directly as impermanent, painful, and non-self.

5. Penetrative Wisdom (Nibbedhikā Paññā) like a Diamond:

Insight wisdom is compared to a diamond (vajra-māṇikya) because it penetrates and cuts through all other gems. Direct insight doesn’t rely on artificial instruments (like microscopes); it cuts open the "body-cage" built by Māra by observing the two petals of impermanence: arising and passing away (udayabbaya).

6. The Inconceivable Strategy of Liberation: Letting Go vs. Grasping:

The world seeks security by clinging (upādāna), like pulling back in a tug-of-war. However, the five aggregates (pañcakkhandha) are themselves identified as Māra. The Buddha’s ultimate solution to escape Māra is counter-intuitive: when Māra pulls, simply let go. Grasping creates tension, fear, and suffering; letting go leaves the pressure on Māra and frees the mind.

7. Similes for the Five Aggregates (Pañcakkhandha):

Form (rūpa): Lump of foam (pheṇa) — empty and rapidly dissolving.

Feeling (vedanā): Water bubble — bursting instantly upon arising.

Perception (saññā): Mirage (marīci) — deceptive appearance of non-existent reality.

Volitional Formations / Preparations (saṅkhāra): Banana tree trunk — layer after layer peeled away with no inner heartwood found.

Consciousness (viññāṇa): Magician’s trick / Illusion (māyā) — making the non-existent appear existent.

8. Subtlest Suffering: Volitional Formations (Saṅkhāra-Dukkha):

The deepest level of suffering is not merely physical illness or loss, but the strain of continuous volitional preparations (saṅkhārapāramā dukkhā)—the endless mental, verbal, and physical leanings (ara-ændīm) and intentions performed to maintain existence in saṃsāra.

9. Identity View (Sakkāya-Diṭṭhi) in 20 Modes:

Uninstructed worldlings refuse to let go of the aggregates, clinging to them through 20 forms of identity view (e.g., regarding form: "Form is self," "Self possesses form," "Form is in self," or "Self is in form").

10. Mindfulness of Breathing (Ānāpānasati) as Allaying Saṃsāric "Panting":

Inhalation and exhalation (ānāpāna) represent the physical and mental momentum—the "panting"—of endless running in saṃsāra. By anchoring mindfulness (sati) on the breath and tranquilizing these formations, one stops the mind's chaotic running and develops the four Satipaṭṭhānas, leading to the path-knowledges (magga-ñāṇa) of liberation.

11. Crossing the Flood via the Middle Path (Majjhima Paṭipadā):

Referring to the Oghataraṇa Sutta, the Buddha explains that he crossed the flood of defilements "without halting and without straining." Halting (passivity) leads to drowning; straining (over-exertion/grasping) leads to exhaustion and death. The Middle Path is the balanced, wise exertion of insight attention (vipassanā-manasikāra).
