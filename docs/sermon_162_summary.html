Sermon 162 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #162 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #162

Primary Theme: Systematic control and taming of the fickle, defiled mind through wise attention (yoniso manasikāra), self-restraint (niggaha), and progressive methods of thought removal to achieve liberation.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The novice monk Ven. Sānu, his lay mother in Sāvatthi, and his past-life mother who was reborn as a female spirit (yakkhi).

The Interaction: The young novice Ven. Sānu was skilled in Dhamma preaching and regularly dedicated the merit of his sermons to his parents. While his human parents were unaware, his past-life mother—now a female yakkhi—heard his sermons, rejoiced in the merit, and earned great respect and high status among the devas and yakkhas as "the mother of Ven. Sānu."

The Disaster / Turning Point: Upon reaching youth, Ven. Sānu was overcome by the intoxication of youth (yobbana-mada) and defiled impulses, deciding to disrobe and return to household life. His lay mother pleaded with him, highlighting the disadvantages (ādīnava) of lay life, but could not change his mind. Seeing this, the past-life yakkhi mother feared losing her spiritual honor and possessed Ven. Sānu, causing him to collapse to the ground in a trance, and spoke through him to warn against committing unwholesome deeds.

The Result / Resolution: Upon recovering, Ven. Sānu was deeply moved by his lay mother's advice—that returning to lay life after renunciation is like jumping back into a pit of burning embers or being spiritual dead. He resolved to remain ordained, received higher ordination (upasampadā), and was advised by the Buddha with Dhammapada Verse 326. Using this verse as self-instruction, he restrained his mind with wise attention (yoniso manasikāra), attained Arahantship, and preached the Dhamma for 120 years across Ancient India (Jambudīpa).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Self-Restraint and the Simile of the Elephant in Rut (Dhammapada v. 326)

The mind running under the impulse of defilements is compared to an elephant in rut (hatthippabhinna), which can break iron chains and run wild if not controlled. Just as a mahout uses a goad (aṅkusa) to tame such an elephant, a practitioner must use self-directed advice and wise attention (yoniso manasikāra) to subdue a wild mind.

2. The Three Intoxications (Mada)

The sermon highlights three inherent intoxications that corrupt sentient beings:

Intoxication with youth (yobbana-mada)

Intoxication with health (ārogya-mada)

Intoxication with life (jīvita-mada)

Ven. Sānu’s urge to disrobe was driven specifically by the intoxication with youth.

3. Wise Attention (Yoniso Manasikāra) vs. Unwise Attention (Ayoniso Manasikāra)

Unwise attention turns outward, becoming enslaved and captivated by sense objects, chasing them like a mirage. In contrast, wise attention acts as a goad by turning awareness inward to directly observe the mind’s own changing nature, catching defilements like greed (lobha), hatred (dosa), and delusion (moha) at their root.

4. The Five-Step Method for Thought Control (Vitakkasaṇṭhāna Sutta)

The Buddha prescribed a progressive 5-step method to quiet crashing unwholesome thoughts:

Step 1 (Replacing the peg): Replace an unwholesome thought with an opposite wholesome object (e.g., replace lust with asubha-nimitta, or hatred with mettā), like a carpenter driving out a coarse peg with a fine one.

Step 2 (Arousing disgust): Reflect on the danger and consequences (ādīnava) of the unwholesome thought to arouse disgust and shame, like a stylish youth trying to throw off a rotting carcass hung around their neck.

Step 3 (Redirecting attention): Deliberately ignore or look away from the thought by redirecting focus to simple physical tasks (sweeping, chores), like closing one's eyes or looking away from an unwanted sight.

Step 4 (Tracing root causes): Deconstruct and analyze the structure/cause of the thought to step-by-step pacify its momentum, analogous to someone realizing the absurdity of walking fast, then slowing down, standing, sitting, and finally lying down.

Step 5 (Forceful suppression): Subdue, challenge, and crush the unwholesome thought using wholesome determination, like a strong man seizing a weak man by the head or neck and subduing him.

5. The Inclination/Flexibility of the Mind (Namyatā)

Citing the Bodhisatta's reflections (from the Dvedhāvitakka Sutta), the sermon explains that whatever a person thinks about repeatedly creates a mental leaning or flexibility (namyatā) in that direction. Habitually dwelling on sensual thoughts (kāma-vitakka), ill-will (vyāpāda-vitakka), or harm (vihiṃsā-vitakka) bends the mind toward evil, whereas reflecting on renunciation (nekkhamma), non-ill-will (avyāpāda), and harmlessness (avihiṃsā) bends it toward liberation.

6. The Perception of Abandoning (Pahāna-saññā) in the *Girimānanda Sutta*

The sermon details the precise, step-by-step psychological process for eliminating unwholesome mental states:

Nādhivāseti: Does not tolerate the arisen evil thought.

Pajahati: Abandons or discards it.

Vinodeti: Dispels and pushes it away.

Byantīkaroti: Terminates it / puts an end to it.

Anabhāvaṅgameti: Brings it to complete non-existence.

7. Four Right Endeavors (Sammappadhāna) and Four-Factored Energy

Right Effort (sammā-vāyāma) requires a dynamic sequence of mental energy to eliminate obstacles: generating desire/interest (chandaṃ janeti), striving (vāyamati), arousing energy (viriyaṃ ārabhati), upholding determination (cittaṃ paggaṇhāti), and supreme exertion (padahati). When severe obstacles persist, one must apply "four-factored energy" (caturaṅga-samannāgata-viriya), refusing to relax effort even if only skin, sinews, and bones remain.

8. Balancing Restraint (Niggaha) and Exertion (Paggaha)

The practitioner must dynamically regulate the mind: applying exertion/support (paggaha) when the mind is sluggish or depressed, and applying restraint/censure (niggaha) when unwholesome thoughts run out of control. Leaving the mind unguided under the pretext of "just watching" is compared to a driver abandoning the wheel and brakes of a fast-moving vehicle.

9. Deconstructive Awareness via Mindfulness and Clear Comprehension (Sati-Sampajañña)

Mindfulness must be directed inward across the Four Foundations (Satipaṭṭhāna). By observing feelings (vedanā), perceptions (saññā), and thoughts (vitakka) arise, persist briefly, and pass away—like watching vehicle headlights pass by on a dark road—one sees their impermanent (anicca) and non-self (anattā) nature, preventing conceptual proliferation (papañca).

10. Materialistic Science vs. Internal Dhamma Development

Modern scientific and technological development is characterized as a one-sided intellectual advance operating under fundamental ignorance (avijjā). Because it caters to fivefold sensual pleasures and economic expansion without purifying internal defilements (greed, hatred, delusion), it frequently results in destructive weapons and systemic distress. Spiritual development (bhāvanā), by contrast, cultivates both the intellect and the heart.

11. Personal Responsibility for Liberation (Tumhehi Kiccaṃ Ātappaṃ)

Emphasizing the stanza “Tumhehi kiccaṃ ātappaṃ, akkhātāro tathāgatā,” the Thero reminds listeners that the Tathāgatas are merely teachers who point out the path. Neither rituals, Bodhi Pujas, parents, nor external powers can grant Nibbāna; liberation depends entirely on individual self-discipline, absorption (jhāna), and personal exertion to break free from the bonds of Māra (mārabandhanā).
