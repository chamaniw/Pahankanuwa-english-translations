Sermon 196 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #196 Summary

Here is a scholarly, structured analysis and summary of Pahankanuwa Sermon #196 delivered by Ven. Katukurunde Nanananda Thero.

---

Sermon #196 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #196 (Paṭicca Samuppāda Sermon No. 14)

Primary Theme: The realization and total cessation of the six sense spheres (saḷāyatana-nirodha) as the true definition of Vedic mastery (vedagu), universal victory (sabbaji), the extraction of the root of suffering (gaṇḍamūla), and the practical fulfillment of the Supramundane Noble Eightfold Path.

---

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The ascetic Uddaka Rāmaputta (recalled by the Buddha) and the Sangha of monks.

The Interaction: The Buddha recalls to the monks a triumphant, riddle-like declaration frequently made by Uddaka Rāmaputta:

> "Idaṁ jātu vedagu – idaṁ jātu sabbaji / idaṁ jātu apalikhataṁ – gaṇḍamūlaṁ palikhaṇinti"

> ("This is perfect mastery of the Veda; this is victory over all; this is the extraction of the root of the abscess hitherto unextracted.")

The Disaster / Turning Point: Although Uddaka uttered these words with grand claims of spiritual attainment, he lacked true insight into the radical nature of reality. The Buddha makes the striking remark that despite Uddaka’s declaration, only a monk in the Buddha’s dispensation is truly qualified to make such an utterance. Non-Buddhist ascetics remain trapped within views (diṭṭhi) born of feelings (vedanā) generated through contact (phassa) at the six sense doors (chahi phassāyatanehi phussa phussa paṭisaṁvedenti), leaving them helpless within the cycle of Dependent Arising (paṭicca-samuppāda).

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha reinterprets Uddaka’s terms through the framework of the six sense spheres (saḷāyatana):

1. A true Vedagu (Master of Knowledge) is one who knows as they really are the arising (samudaya), passing away (atthagama), gratification (assāda), peril (ādīnava), and escape (nissaraṇa) regarding the six spheres of contact.

2. A true Sabbaji (Victor over All) is one who knows these five aspects and attains complete release through non-clinging (anupādāya vimutto).

3. The Gaṇḍa (abscess) is the physical body born of the four primary elements, and its Gaṇḍamūla (root) is craving (taṇhā). Uprooting craving entirely is the true extraction of the root of the abscess.

---

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Reinterpretation of Traditional Vedic Concepts (*Vedagu*, *Sabbaji*, *Gaṇḍamūla*)

The Buddha deconstructs Brahminical terms. True Vedic mastery (vedagu) is not knowledge of ritual texts or three Vedas, but the tevijjā (threefold higher knowledge) culminating in the destruction of mental influxes (āsavakkhaya-ñāṇa). Universal victory (sabbaji) is total detachment from sensory contact. The physical body is an abscess (gaṇḍa) subject to breaking and scattering, and its hidden root (gaṇḍamūla) is craving (taṇhā).

2. The Definition of "All" (*Sabba Sutta*)

The Buddha explicitly defines "All" (sabba) exclusively as the six internal sense bases (eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind) and their corresponding six external sense objects (forms, sounds, smells, tastes, tangibles, mental phenomena). Anyone who attempts to posit a reality or metaphysical realm outside of these twelve bases engages in meaningless, foolish chatter (aññaṁ sabbaṁ paññāpessāmi... vācāvatthurevassa assa).

3. The Phenomenological Conception of the "World" (*Lokapaññatti*)

In conversation with Ven. Samiddhi, the Buddha explains that the "world" (loko) or the designation of a world (lokapaññatti) exists only where sense faculties, external objects, sense-consciousnesses, and cognizable phenomena converge. Where these cease to exist, neither the world nor its manifestation exists. Thus, saḷāyatana-nirodha (cessation of the six sense spheres) is the transcending of the world.

4. Critique of Speculative Views (*Brahmajāla Sutta* Context)

All 62 speculative views categorized in the Brahmajāla Sutta arise because thinkers experience feelings generated by contact across the sense spheres (phussa phussa paṭisaṁvedenti). Lacking insight into sensory escape (nissaraṇa), they are driven from feeling to craving (vedanā-paccayā taṇhā), to grasping (upādāna), becoming (bhava), and birth (jāti), leaving them endlessly entangled in Saṁsāra.

5. Rejection of a Personal Agent in Experience (*Moḷiya Phagguṇa Sutta*)

Addressing the misguided questions of monk Moḷiya Phagguṇa ("Who touches?", "Who feels?", "Who craves?"), the Buddha rejects the notion of an enduring soul or agent. Experience is entirely process-bound and dependently arisen: saḷāyatana-paccayā phasso (conditioned by the six sense spheres, contact arises), phassa-paccayā vedanā (conditioned by contact, feeling arises), and vedanā-paccayā taṇhā (conditioned by feeling, craving arises).

6. Sensory Cessation and the Transcending of Conceptual Proliferation (*Papañca-Nirodha*)

In a profound dialogue between Ven. Mahā Koṭṭhita and Ven. Sāriputta, Ven. Sāriputta rejects all quadrilemma inquiries regarding whether "anything remains" after the total cessation of the six spheres of contact. Speculating on a state beyond sense cessation is an attempt to conceptualize the unconceptualizable (appapañcaṁ papañceti). Proliferation (papañca) extends only as far as the six sense spheres; thus, saḷāyatana-nirodha is strictly synonymous with papañca-nirodha (Nibbāna).

7. The Nature of Fetters: The Simile of the Two Oxen (*Saṁyojana*)

Ven. Sāriputta explains to Ven. Mahā Koṭṭhita that external objects do not inherently bind the sense organs, nor do sense organs bind external objects. Using the simile of a black ox and a white ox tethered by a single rope, he illustrates that the bond (saṁyojana) is neither ox, but the rope itself—representing desire and lust (chanda-rāga) that arises dependent on both.

8. Sense Restraint as Defense Against Māra (The Simile of the Tortoise)

Just as a tortoise retracts its five limbs and head inside its shell to protect itself from a predatory fox, a monk guards the six sense doors to defeat Māra. True restraint requires avoiding the capture of general signs (nimittaggāhī) or secondary attributes (anubyañjanaggāhī) of sense objects, thereby preventing mental influxes (āsava) from flowing in.

9. The Exhaustion of Grasping Aggregates (*Mahā Saḷāyatanika Sutta*)

The Buddha contrasts the ignorant worldling with the disciplined practitioner. The worldling, lacking true knowledge of the impermanent nature of sense processes, develops lustful passion (sārajjati), accumulating the five aggregates of grasping (upacayaṁ gacchanti) and expanding physical and mental burning (pariḷāha). The mindful monk sees sense processes as impermanent, causing the aggregates of grasping to diminish (apacayaṁ gacchanti) and bringing about physical and mental tranquility (sukha).

10. The Supramundane Eightfold Path and Simultaneous Calm & Insight (*Yuganaddha*)

Contemplating the impermanence of the six sense bases spontaneously fulfills the Noble Eightfold Path. The moral factors (sammā vācā, kammanta, ājīva) are already purified at this stage, while the remaining five factors (diṭṭhi, saṁkappa, vāyāma, sati, samādhi) operate on a supramundane level. In this state, tranquility (samatha) and insight (vipassanā) run yoked together like a pair of oxen (yuganaddha), leading directly to full liberation through wisdom (vijjā-vimutti).

11. The Threefold Characteristic of Impermanence (*Anicca*, *Vipariṇāmī*, *Aññathābhāvī*)

Sensory phenomena possess three intrinsic facets of instability: they are impermanent (anicca), subject to change (vipariṇāmī), and subject to "otherwiseness" (aññathābhāvī). Furthermore, visual consciousness (cakkhu-viññāṇa) is dependently arisen (dvayaṁ paṭicca viññāṇaṁ sambhoti). Because it depends on two impermanent conditions (eye and visual form), consciousness itself can never be permanent; it quivers (calā) and withers (vayā) moment by moment.
