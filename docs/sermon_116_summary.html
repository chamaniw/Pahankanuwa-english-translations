Sermon 116 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #116 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #116 (Pahankaṇuwa Sinhala Audio Dhamma Sermon No. 9)

Primary Theme: True spiritual protection (surakkhitaṃ) is achieved not through external safeguards, omens, or rituals, but through diligence in wholesome states (kusala dhamma) across the stages of life, guarding the three doors of body, speech, and mind, establishing Right Livelihood (sammā-ājīva), and penetrating the Four Noble Truths to realize Nibbāna.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Prince Bodhirāja, a wealthy ruler of the Bhesakaḷā Forest region, his wife, the Supreme Buddha, and Ven. Ānanda.

The Interaction: Prince Bodhirāja constructs a magnificent palace named "Kokanada" and invites the Buddha for its housewarming alms-offering. Expecting a foretoken regarding his childlessness, the prince spreads white cloths starting from the threshold, believing that if the Buddha walks over them, it will signify that he and his wife will be blessed with a son or daughter.

The Disaster / Turning Point: The Buddha refuses to step on the white cloths and signals Ven. Ānanda to have them removed. When questioned by the prince, the Buddha exposes his hidden ulterior expectation and declares that he will remain childless due to past unwholesome karma (akusala kamma). In a past life, after a shipwreck, the prince and his wife survived on an isolated island by roasting bird eggs and slaughtering fledglings continuously through all three stages of their lives, completely negligent of wholesome qualities (kusala dhamma).

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha utters Dhammapada verse 157, explaining that if one holds oneself dear, one must protect oneself by engaging in wholesome deeds during at least one of the three stages of life. Hearing this discourse, Prince Bodhirāja brings forth his past accumulated merits and attains the fruit of Stream-entry (Sotāpatti-phala).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. True Self-Protection (Surakkhitaṃ) vs. External Security:

Mundane security measures (locking doors, employing bodyguards, or financial insurance) cannot protect a person from the heavy retributive force of past karma (kamma). Genuine safety is internal, attained solely by protecting the three doors of action—body, speech, and mind—through active diligence in wholesome states (kusala dhamma).

2. Diligence Across the Three Stages of Life (Tiṇṇaṃ Aññataraṃ Yāmaṃ):

The phrase "three watches of the night" in Dhammapada 157 allegorically refers to the three stages of human life: youth, middle age, and old age. The wise person (paṇḍito) must take care to cultivate giving (dāna), virtue (sīla), concentration (samādhi), and wisdom (paññā) during at least one of these stages—and ideally across all three—before death intervenes.

3. Residual Karmic Retribution (Kamma-Niyāma):

Present-life misfortunes, illnesses, or childlessness are often merely the minor residual remnants (tail-end) of past severe evil deeds whose major retributions were already experienced in woeful states (apāya), ghost realms (peta-loka), or animal realms. However, engaging vigorously in current wholesome deeds can decrease or neutralize (ahosi-kamma) pending evil retributions.

4. Universal Self-Love as the Premise for Non-Harming (Na Hiṃse Paraṃ):

Traversing all directions with the mind reveals that no one is dearer to oneself than oneself (attānañce piyaṃ jaññā). Recognizing that all other living beings similarly cherish their own lives (sabbesaṃ jīvitaṃ piyaṃ), one who truly loves oneself must refrain from harming or killing others (tasmā na hiṃse paraṃ attakāmo).

5. Primacy of Mind and Intention (Cetanā):

The mind acts as the steering wheel of life's journey (Cetanāhaṃ, bhikkhave, kammaṃ vadāmi). A wrongly directed mind (micchā-paṇihitaṃ cittaṃ) inflicts self-destruction greater than any enemy (amitteneva attanā), whereas a rightly directed mind (sammā-paṇihitaṃ cittaṃ) bestows benefits surpassing those given by parents.

6. Right Livelihood (Sammā-Ājīva) and the Four Fears:

Engaging in wrong livelihood (micchā-ājīva)—such as trading in weapons, living beings, flesh, intoxicants, or poisons—subjects a person to four severe fears: self-reproach (attānuvāda-bhaya), censure by others (parānuvāda-bhaya), legal punishment (daṇḍa-bhaya), and rebirth in woeful destinations (duggati-bhaya). Sacrificing income for righteous living yields true peace of mind.

7. False Refuges vs. Supreme Refuge (Saraṇa):

Frightened human beings futilely seek refuge in mountains, forests, groves, and external shrines (pabbatāni vanāni ca). The only true, supreme, and secure refuge (saraṇaṃ khemaṃ) that liberates one from all samsāric suffering (sabbadukkhā pamuccati) is taking refuge in the Triple Gem (Buddha, Dhamma, Saṅgha) and penetrating the Four Noble Truths (cattāri ariya-saccāni).

8. The Opportune Moment (Khaṇa-Sampatti) and the Minimum Yield:

Obtaining a human rebirth during the dispensation of a Buddha is a rare opportune moment (khaṇa-sampatti). The essential baseline yield to extract from this life is Stream-entry (Sotāpatti), which limits remaining samsāric existence to a maximum of seven lives, reducing remaining suffering to a mere fingernail's quantity of soil compared to the vast earth.

9. The Systematic Integration of the Noble Eightfold Path (Ariya Aṭṭhaṅgika Magga):

The path functions as a linked sequence: Right Vision (sammā-diṭṭhi) naturally generates Right Thoughts (sammā-saṅkappa), which refine speech (sammā-vācā), action (sammā-kammanta), and livelihood (sammā-ājīva). This ethical base enables Right Effort (sammā-vāyāma) to purge unwholesome states and establish Right Mindfulness (sammā-sati) and Right Concentration (sammā-samādhi).

10. Igniting Latent Spiritual Faculties (Indriya):

Past wholesome merits often lie dormant in the mind like burning embers covered by ashes (bhasmāchannena). Through wise attention (yoniso manasikāra) and listening to the Dhamma, one blows away the ash to activate the five spiritual faculties: faith (saddhā), energy (viriya), mindfulness (sati), concentration (samādhi), and wisdom (paññā).
