Sermon 065 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #65 Summary

Here is the structured summary of Pahankanuwa Dhamma Sermon #65, based strictly on the provided text.

---

Sermon #65 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #65

Primary Theme: The practical, gradual nature of the Dhamma path (anupubba-paṭipadā) as taught by the Buddha, refuting defeatist concepts such as the lack of perfections (pāramī) or lack of triple-rooted rebirth (trihetuka-paṭisandhi), and emphasizing personal exertion (tumhehi kiccaṃ ātappaṃ) through the Noble Eightfold Path.

---

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Suppabuddha the leper, an impoverished, disfigured beggar in Rājagaha.

The Interaction: Seeking food, Suppabuddha approached a large gathering where the Buddha was preaching. Realizing it was a Dhamma sermon rather than a food distribution, he decided to sit at the edge of the crowd. Scanning the audience with His Buddha-cognition (buddhacakkhu), the Buddha bypassed kings and scholars and identified the poor beggar as the one capable of comprehending the Dhamma.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Suppabuddha suffered from severe poverty, social degradation, hunger, and leprosy. The turning point occurred when he let go of his initial desire for physical food to listen to the Buddha. Recognizing his mind was receptive, the Buddha delivered the gradual discourse (ānupubbī-kathā)—covering generosity (dāna), virtue (sīla), heaven (sagga), the danger/vileness of sensual pleasures (kāmānaṃ ādīnavaṃ/okāraṃ/saṅkilesaṃ), and the benefit of renunciation (nekkhamme ānisaṃsaṃ)—culminating in the Four Noble Truths (sāmukkaṃsikā dhamma-desanā).

The Result / Resolution: Suppabuddha’s mind became prepared, soft, free from hindrances, uplifted, and brightened (kalla-cittaṃ, mudu-cittaṃ, vinīvaraṇa-cittaṃ, udagga-cittaṃ, pasanna-cittaṃ). The stainless Dhamma-eye (virajaṃ vītimalaṃ dhammacakkhuṃ) arose in him, realizing Stream-entry (sotāpatti) with the insight: "Whatever is of a nature to arise, all that is of a nature to cease" (yaṃ kiñci samudayadhammaṃ sabbaṃ taṃ nirodhadhammaṃ). He attained a noble spiritual state superior to the sovereignty of a wheel-turning monarch (cakkavatti).

---

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Personal Responsibility in Exertion (*Tumhehi Kiccaṃ Ātappaṃ*):

Citing Dhammapada stanza 276, the sermon emphasizes that individual effort and meditation (jhāyino) are mandatory to escape Māra's bonds (mārabandhanā). The Tathāgatas and Dhamma teachers are merely shower-of-the-way (maggakkhāyī / akkhātāro); they cannot perform the practice for the disciple.

2. Gradual Training, Action, and Practice (*Anupubba-Sikkhā, Anupubba-Kiriyā, Anupubba-Paṭipadā*):

Citing the Gaṇaka-Moggallāna Sutta and the Pahārāda Sutta, the Dhamma-Vinaya is described as having a gradual slope (anupubba-ninno) like the ocean floor, without an abrupt precipice (na āyatakenēva papāto / na āyatakenēva aññā-paṭivedho). Practice proceeds systematically through:

Virtue (sīla)

Sense-restraint (indriyesu guttadvāratā)

Moderation in eating (bhojane mattaññutā)

Devotion to wakefulness (jāgariyānuyoga)

Mindfulness and clear comprehension (sati-sampajañña)

Seclusion and absorption (jhāna).

3. Refutation of Defeatist Doctrines Regarding *Pāramī* and Triple-Rooted Rebirth:

The Thero strongly rejects contemporary claims that individuals today cannot realize Nibbāna due to a lack of perfections (pāramī) or lack of triple-rooted rebirth (trihetuka-paṭisandhi). He labels the dogma of triple-rooted rebirth a "parasite on the Dispensation" (sāsana-parapusa), noting that the Buddha never turned anyone away based on such qualifications.

4. The Efficacy of the Gradual Discourse (*Ānupubbī-Kathā*):

The Buddha’s method of teaching adapts to the listener's mental state by systematically unfolding:

Generosity (dāna-kathā)

Virtue (sīla-kathā)

Heavenly existence (sagga-kathā)

The danger, vileness, and definement of sensuality (kāmānaṃ ādīnavaṃ, okāraṃ, saṅkilesaṃ)

The benefits of renunciation (nekkhamme ca ānisaṃsaṃ)

The Four Noble Truths (sāmukkaṃsikā dhamma-desanā: dukkha, samudaya, nirodha, magga).

5. The Eight Attainments/Characteristics of a Stream-Enterer (*Sotāpanna*):

Upon gaining the Dhamma-eye, a Stream-enterer possesses eight key qualities:

Diṭṭhadhammo (saw the Dhamma / Nibbāna)

Pattadhammo (reached the Dhamma)

Viditadhammo (understood the Dhamma)

Pariyogāḷhadhammo (dived into the Dhamma)

Tiṇṇavicikiccho (crossed over doubt)

Vigatakathaṅkatho (freed from indecisive questioning)

Vesārajjappatto (attained bold confidence)

Aparappaccayo satthusāsane (became independent of others in the Teacher's Dispensation, guaranteed full liberation within seven births at most).

6. Four Categories of Individuals Regarding Realization:

Ugghaṭitaññū: Realizes the Dhamma instantly upon hearing a brief hint/stanza (e.g., Bāhiya Dārucīriya).

Vipañcitaññū: Realizes the Dhamma when a discourse is explained in detail.

Neyya: Realizes the Dhamma gradually through guidance, association with noble friends (kalyāṇa-mitta), hearing the Dhamma, and systematic effort.

Padaparama: Cannot attain the paths and fruits (magga-phala) in the present life, but learning the Dhamma implants seeds for future realization; no one is discarded by the Buddha.

7. Primacy of the Correct Method (*Yoniso*) over Desire (*Bhūmija Sutta*):

Spiritual fruits depend not on whether one practices with or without desire, but on whether one practices properly (yoniso) via the Noble Eightfold Path (sammā-diṭṭhi to sammā-samādhi).

Improper method (ayoniso): Squeezing sand for oil, pulling a cow's horn for milk, churning water for butter, or rubbing wet wood for fire yields no fruit regardless of desire.

Proper method (yoniso): Squeezing sesame flour, pulling a cow's teat, churning curd, or rubbing dry wood yields results regardless of desire.

8. Lay Practice and the Nature of Renunciation (*Pabbajjā*):

Renunciation is an organic, internal transformation—likened to a caterpillar transforming into a butterfly. Pabbajjā literally means "breaking out of the pen/stall" (vaja). Lay practices (5 precepts, 8 Poya precepts observed in accord with Arahants, and 10 householder precepts) serve as the foundation from which monastic renunciation naturally emerges.

9. Four Factors of Stream-Entry (*Sotāpattiyaṅga*):

Essential elements required to enter the stream of Dhamma:

Association with superior persons / noble friends (sappurisa-saṃsevo / kalyāṇa-mitta)

Listening to the True Dhamma (saddhamma-savanaṃ)

Wise attention (yoniso-manasikāra)

Practice in accordance with the Dhamma (dhammānudhamma-paṭipatti).

10. The Six Rare Occurrences (*Dullabha-Dhamma*):

Visualized as concentric circles on a target board, leading step-by-step toward realization:

Arising of a Buddha (dullabho buddhupādo lokasmiṃ)

Obtaining human birth (dullabho manussatta-paṭilābho)

Obtaining the opportune moment free from unfavorable conditions (dullabhā khaṇa-sampatti)

Obtaining ordination (dullabhā pabbajjā)

Hearing the True Dhamma (dullabhaṃ saddhamma-savanaṃ)

Associating with superior persons/noble friends (dullabho sappurisa-saṃsevo).

11. Superiority of Practice-Offering (*Paṭipatti-Pūjā*):

True commemoration of historical events like Poson Poya (the arrival of Arahant Mahinda) requires offering practice (paṭipatti-pūjā)—developing tranquility (samatha) and insight (vipassanā)—rather than relying solely on material offerings (āmisa-pūjā) or empty nostalgia.
