Sermon 088 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #88 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #88

Primary Theme: Cultivating true renunciation (nekkhamma) by stabilizing a fickle mind (anavaṭṭhita-citta) through wise reflection (yoniso manasikāra), developing the five qualities of spiritual wakefulness (jāgara-dhamma), and transcending karmic preparations (puññapāpapahīna) to attain Nibbāna.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: A poor farmer from Sāvatthī who repeatedly entered and left the Saṅgha, earning the nickname Venerable Cittahattha ("ruled by his mind"); his pregnant wife; and in the parallel narrative, Kuddāla Paṇḍita (the Bodhisatta in a past life).

The Interaction: Searching for a lost cow until midday, the hungry poor man arrived at Jetavana Monastery. The Saṅgha compassionately offered him leftover alms food. Astonished by the delicious food obtained without hard labor, he requested ordination purely as a means of securing a comfortable livelihood.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Without wise reflection (paccavekkhaṇa) or interest in meditation (bhāvanā), he overate, became physically fat, and mentally weak. Overwhelmed by sensual longing for lay life, he disrobed and re-ordained six times. Before his seventh attempt to disrobe, he returned home to fetch his under-robe. He encountered his pregnant wife sleeping flat on her back—saliva drooling from her open mouth, clothes disheveled—resembling a bloated corpse (uddhūmātaka-asubha).

The Result / Resolution: The horrifying sight triggered an intense perception of impermanence (Aniccaṃ) and spiritual urgency (saṃvega). Reciting "Aniccaṃ" while walking back to the monastery, he attained Stream-entry (sotāpatti). Upon re-ordaining for the seventh time, he swiftly attained Arahantship with the four analytical knowledges (paṭisambhidā). The Buddha validated his attainment by uttering Dhammapada verses 38–39 and relating the Kuddāla Jātaka, where the Bodhisatta discarded his spade into a river to conquer internal greed, shouting "Jitaṃ me!" ("I have won!").

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Ultimate Essence of Renunciation (Nekkhamma-saṅkappa):

True renunciation is not merely an external social custom or a escape from poverty; its ultimate goal is Nibbāna. Rooted in Right View (sammā-diṭṭhi), genuine nekkhamma requires relinquishing mental attachments—whether abandoning a vast fortune or a single spade blade (appaṃ vā bhogakkhandhaṃ pahāya, mahantaṃ vā...).

2. The Fickle Mind (Anavaṭṭhita-citta) and Floating Faith (Pariplava-pasāda):

A mind that sways like a post set in a heap of chaff or a dwarf on a galloping horse cannot fulfill supramundane wisdom (lokuttara-paññā). Superficial faith, pulled toward varied external teachers, prevents the mind from anchoring firmly in the True Dhamma (Saddhamma).

3. Wise Reflection on Requisites (Paccavekkhaṇa):

Consuming alms food without wise reflection generates defilements (kilesa). Monastics and lay practitioners must reflect that food is composed of the four great elements (catudhātu) and consume it solely for bodily maintenance (yāpanā) and the holy life (brahmacariya), not for amusement, beautification, or fattening the body.

4. Appropriate Meditation Subjects (Sappāya-kammaṭṭhāna):

Meditation practice must suit individual temperaments. For those strongly attached to the fleshy, well-nourished body, contemplating the bloated corpse (uddhūmātaka-asubha) serves as the appropriate antidote (sappāya) to shatter lust (rāga) and trigger spiritual disillusionment (nibbidā).

5. Qualities of Spiritual Wakefulness (Jāgara-dhamma):

The verse phrase "natthi jāgarato bhayaṃ" ("there is no fear for the awakened") refers to cultivating the five controlling faculties/powers (indriya/bala): Faith (saddhā), Energy (viriya), Mindfulness (sati), Concentration (samādhi), and Wisdom (paññā). These five faculties keep the practitioner spiritually awake and immune to the terrors of Saṃsāra.

6. A Mind Free from Lust and Hatred (Anavassuta-citta & Ananvāhata-ceta):

Liberation demands a mind unmoistened by the wetness of lust (anavassutacitta) and unassailed or unhardened by hatred (ananvāhataceta). Both attachment and aversion stem from the core delusion (moha) of an "I" and "mine."

7. Transcending Both Merit and Demerit (Puññapāpapahīna):

An Arahant has completely abandoned both merit (puñña) and demerit (pāpa). While merit is preferable to demerit within Saṃsāra, both are conditioned preparations (saṅkhāra) that fuel the cycle of existence (bhava). Full liberation requires the complete stilling of all volitional activities (saṅkhāra-upasama).

8. Shattering Identity View (Sakkāya-diṭṭhi):

The wheel of karma (kamma-cakka) is broken only by the wheel of Dhamma (dhamma-cakka). Through insight wisdom (vipassanā-paññā), the rigid belief in a permanent self (sakkāya-diṭṭhi) is severed at Stream-entry (sotāpatti), destroying the karmic potential to be reborn in the four lower realms of misery (apāya).

9. Unwavering Faith as the Primary Seed (Acala-Saddhā / Bījaṃ):

Faith (saddhā) acts as the primary seed (bījaṃ) for all spiritual growth. It implies surrendering the heart to the Triple Gem, removing doubt (vicikicchā) and mental dryness, and inspiring the sustained energy (viriya) required for meditation.

10. The Naturally Luminous Mind (Pabhassara-citta):

The mind is naturally luminous (pabhassaramidaṃ cittaṃ), but becomes tarnished by adventitious defilements (āgantuka-upakkilesa). Mundane book-learning merely paints over the mind, whereas vipassanā meditation acts as a rust-remover, stripping away visiting impurities to reveal the mind's inherent radiance and supramundane wisdom.
