Sermon 010 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #10 Summary

Sermon #10 Summary

---

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #10

Primary Theme: The critical necessity of purifying and guarding the three doors of action (kāya, vācā, mano)—with special emphasis on verbal restraint—to avert catastrophic saṃsāric consequences and cultivate the internal peace of Nibbāna, rather than relying on conventional secular notions of a "new era."

---

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Venerable Mahā Moggallāna, Venerable Lakkhaṇa, and the Sūkara-preta (a pig-faced hungry ghost, who was formerly a Dhamma-preaching monk during the era of Kassapa Buddha).

The Interaction: While descending Vulture’s Peak (Gijjhakūṭa), Ven. Mahā Moggallāna smiles upon witnessing an

unseen vision but defers explaining the reason until he and Ven. Lakkhaṇa are in the presence of the Buddha at Veluvana Monastery.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Ven. Moggallāna reveals he saw a terrifying preta with a human body, a pig’s head, worms oozing from its mouth, and a pig’s tail protruding from its mouth. The Buddha confirms this vision and relates its origin: During Kassapa Buddha's era, two senior monks lived in complete harmony. A visiting preaching monk, driven by corrupt greed (abhijjhā) to seize their monastery, systematically slandered each elder to the other (pisuṇā vācā combined with musāvāda). Believing the lies, the two harmoniously residing elders grew angry (dosa), clashed, and abandoned the monastery in opposite directions, remaining spiritually stalled for a century.

The Result / Resolution: After 100 years, the two alienated elders accidentally met at a distant hermitage, recognized the malicious deception, reconciled, and returned to banish the imposter. Upon death, the deceitful preaching monk was boiled in Avīci Hell for an entire buddhantara (inter-buddha period) and was subsequently reborn as a Sūkara-preta, bearing bodily grotesque deformities directly corresponding to his verbal sins.

---

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Delusion of Calendar "New Dawns" vs. Genuine Dhamma Progress: Expecting a personal transformation or a "new era" purely from changes in the conventional calendar (e.g., a new century or year) without inspecting one's philosophy of life and rectifying moral shortcomings is a great delusion. Real progress requires entering the path traversed by the Noble Ones (Isippaveditaṃ maggaṃ).

2. Purification of the Three Doors of Action (*Kamma-dvāra*): Based on Dhammapada verse 281, spiritual practice centers on guarding speech (vācānurakkhī), thoroughly restraining the mind (manasā susaṃvuto), and avoiding bodily unwholesomeness (kāyena ca akusalaṃ na kayirā).

3. The Three Types of Eyes (*Cakkhu*): The sermon delineates three levels of vision:

The physical eye (maṃsa-cakkhu / masäsa)

The divine eye (dibba-cakkhu / diväsa), which beholds unseen realms (hungry ghosts, devas, hells) through jhāna and abhiññā

The wisdom eye (paññā-cakkhu / pänäsa)

Denying unseen realms simply because they are invisible to the physical eye is likened to a blind person denying the existence of the sun and moon.

4. Karmic Morphological Correspondence (*Kamma-vipāka*): Physical manifestations in lower realms directly mirror past unwholesome actions. The Sūkara-preta's massive body represented his grand ambition for status/monastery ownership; his pig head, oozing worms, and tail in the mouth were the direct fruit of his corrupt, divisive, and deceitful speech.

5. Parallel Visions from the *Lakkhaṇa Saṃyutta*: The sermon cites multiple canonical accounts of pretas seen from Gijjhakūṭa, illustrating the strict karmic fruits of wrong livelihood (micchā-ājīva):

Flying skeletons/meat-lumps pecked by birds (former butchers/hunters)

Sword-haired pretas self-mutilating (pig-hunter)

Headless pretas with eyes/mouth on the chest (Hārika the executioner)

Flaming robes and bowls flying through the air (corrupt monks, nuns, and novices of Kassapa Buddha's era)

6. Analysis of the Ten Unwholesome Courses of Action (*Dasa-Akusala-Kamma-Patha*):

Body (kāya): Killing, stealing (adinnādāna), sexual misconduct.

Speech (vācā): False speech (musāvāda), slander (pisuṇā vācā), harsh speech (pharusa vācā), frivolous chatter (samphappalāpa).

Mind (manas): Covetousness (abhijjhā), ill-will (vyāpāda), wrong view (micchā-diṭṭhi).

7. The Four Types of Fear (*Bhaya*) Endured by the Unvirtuous:

Fear of self-reproach (attānuvāda-bhaya)

Fear of reproach by others/the wise (parānuvāda-bhaya)

Fear of legal punishment (daṇḍa-bhaya)

Fear of evil destinations (duggati-bhaya)

8. The Shadow of Karma (*Kamma-Chāyā*) and Deathbed Remorse: Unwholesome karma festers internally long before bodily death. Like the lengthening shadow of a mountain at sunset, the shadow of past unwholesome deeds (kamma-chāyā) hangs over and oppresses the fool’s mind, culminating in severe agony and panic at the moment of death (e.g., Cunda the pig-butcher).

9. Redefine "Fool" (*Bāla*) vs. "Wise Person" (*Paṇḍita*): Canonical wisdom is independent of academic titles or degrees.

A fool (bāla) is defined by conduct: dubbihita-cintī, dubbhāsita-bhāsī, dukkaṭa-kammakārī (bad thoughts, bad speech, bad actions).

A wise person (paṇḍita) is defined by foresight and virtue: suvihita-cintī, subhāsita-bhāsī, sukaṭa-kammakārī (good thoughts, good speech, good actions).

10. Analytical Breakdown of the Four Lower Realms (*Apāya-Duggati-Vinipāta-Niraya*): Ven. Nanananda provides an illustrative metaphor for the canonical formula of ruin:

Apāya: Skidding off the main road.

Duggati: Sliding down a side ditch.

Vinipāta: Tumbling down a steep precipice.

Niraya: Hitting the bottom and being smashed to pieces (Hell).

11. Nibbāna as Internal Coolness (*Sītibhāva*): Nibbāna is not an external realm floating in space, but the soothing coolness (sītibhāva) established in the mind when the three saṃsāric fires of greed (lobha), hatred (dosa), and delusion (moha) are extinguished through non-greed (alobha), non-hatred (adosa), and non-delusion (amoha).

12. Virtue (*Sīla*) as the Indispensable Foundation for Meditation: Undertaking precepts (such as pañca-sīla or ājīva-aṭṭhamaka-sīla) provides the firm ground necessary to perform mental work. Just as one cannot lift a heavy load while sinking in mud, one cannot develop concentration (samādhi) or insight (vipassanā) while plagued by moral remorse. True practice requires turning inward to conduct the "Noble Quest" (ariya-pariyesana).
