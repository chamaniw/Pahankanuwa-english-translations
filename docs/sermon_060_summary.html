Sermon 060 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #60 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #60 (Sinhala Audio Sermon No. 177)

Primary Theme: The destructive, binding, and renewed-existence-producing nature of craving (taṇhā)—illustrated through classical similes—and its eradication at the root of ignorance (avijjā) through sense-restraint ("in the seen only the seen"), mindfulness (sati), and the perception of impermanence (anicca-saññā).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Venerable Māluṅkyaputta Thera, born to a high-class family in Sāvatthi (named after his mother, Māluṅkyā).

The Interaction: After initially leaving home to join the wanderers (paribbājakas), he met the Buddha, heard the Dhamma, ordained under him, practiced insight (vipassanā), and attained Arahantship. Out of compassion, he later returned to visit his relatives in his former home.

The Disaster / Turning Point: His relatives served him alms food and then attempted to entice him back into the lay life by compiling and displaying their family wealth before him, pleading with him to leave the monkhood, become a layman, and support a wife and children with the accumulated riches.

The Result / Resolution: Demonstrating total liberation from worldly attachment, Venerable Māluṅkyaputta rose into the air and declared the disadvantageous, sticky nature of craving (taṇhā) through verses (recorded in Dhammapada 334–337 and Theragāthā). In a related Sutta context, he had previously realized Arahantship after receiving the Buddha's brief riddle-like admonition on sense-restraint.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Disadvantageous Nature of Craving (Taṇhā / Ponobhavikā)

Craving is designated as ponobhavikā because it is the primary engine driving renewed existence (punarbhava) across saṃsāra.

It is compared to a parasitic māluvā creeper that starts small on a tree and eventually smothers and destroys it, and to a monkey leaping from tree to tree (phalamicchaṃva vanasmiṃ vānaro) seeking fruit.

2. The Seamstress Simile (Sibbanī) and Cessation (Phassa-Nirodha)

In the Pārāyana Vagga verse cited from the Deer Park discussion, craving is termed the "seamstress" (sibbanī).

Like a tailor stitching two pieces of cloth together, taṇhā stitches contact (phassa) directly to the next arising of contact (phassa-samudaya). In doing so, it hides the middle link—the cessation of contact (phassa-nirodha), which is Nibbāna. Beings remain trapped because they fail to see this cessation.

3. The Brief Instruction on Sense Restraint (Diṭṭha, Suta, Muta, Viññāta)

As taught to both Ven. Māluṅkyaputta and Bāhiya Dārucīriya, liberation occurs when processing at the six sense doors is confined strictly to mere experience: in the seen (diṭṭha), let there be only the seen; in the heard (suta), only the heard; in the sensed (muta—smell, taste, touch), only the sensed; in the cognized (viññāta), only the cognized.

4. Deconstruction of Duality and Conceptual Proliferation (Papañca)

Failing to stop at mere sensing causes defilements to establish identity: through taṇhā ("this is mine"), māna ("this am I"), and diṭṭhi ("this is my self").

Getting established "by" and "in" an object constructs a "here," which automatically creates a "there," and consequently an "in-between." Uprooting this mental proliferation eliminates the subjective center and brings the end of suffering (dukkha).

5. Accumulation (Ācinati) vs. Decumulation (Apacinati) of Aggregates

Grasping agreeable signs at sense doors causes bewildered mindfulness, lust, and greed, resulting in the accumulation (ācinati / upacaya) of the five groups of grasping (pañcupādānakkhandhā), taking one far from Nibbāna (ārā nibbānā).

Applying mindfulness prevents lust, wearing down and decumulating (apacinati / apacaya) the aggregates.

6. Craving as Fuel (Upādāna) for Rebirth

Addressing Vacchagotta's query, the Buddha explained that just as wind propels a flame to catch onto another tree, taṇhā itself serves as the grasping fuel (upādāna) carrying the mind to a new bhava at death.

As long as the underlying tendency of craving (taṇhā-anusaya) remains intact, dukkha sprouts repeatedly like a tree whose root is undamaged.

7. The Sticky Trap of Sensuality (Visattikā / Lepa)

Craving is visattikā (sticky/clinging). Ven. Nanananda highlights the Buddha's parable of the monkey trap: a monkey touches sticky pitch (lepa) with one hand, then gets both hands, both feet, and its snout stuck trying to free itself.

This illustrates how worldlings get bound across all five strands of sensual pleasure (kāma-guṇa).

8. Ignorance (Avijjā) as the Poisonous Root of the Dart (Taṇhā-Salla)

The root of the bīraṇa grass of craving is ignorance (avijjā).

In the Licchavi youth discourse, the worldling is pierced by the dart of craving (taṇhā-salla), which is laced with the poison of avijjā. The Tathāgata is the surgeon (sallakatta / bhisakko), Noble Wisdom (ariyā-paññā) is the scalpel, and mindfulness (sati) is the probe (esanī) used to locate and extract the poisoned arrow (abbahe).

9. The Perception of Impermanence (Anicca-saññā) and Dissolution (Bhaṅga-ñāṇa)

Penetrating the impermanent (anicca), unstable (calaṃ), and changing (aññathābhāvī) nature of the six sense bases, sense objects, consciousness (viññāṇa), contact (phassa), and feeling (vedanā) destroys all lust for existence.

As craving fades, the practitioner sees the passing away side clearly, developing the knowledge of dissolution (bhaṅga-ñāṇa).

10. The Hierarchy of Subtlety Across the Sense Bases

Sense objects range from coarse to deeply subtle:

Diṭṭha (seen) is coarse/gross (oḷārika).

Suta (heard) is less gross.

Muta (sensed via tongue, nose, body) is highly personal and subtle.

Viññāta (cognized by mind) is the subtlest—a magician's illusion (māyā) where mental objects (dhammā) created by the mind (mano) are falsely taken as external entities. Realizing Nibbāna is possible at any sense door by recognizing this momentary arising and passing away (udayabbaya).
