Sermon 016 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #16 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #16

Primary Theme: The protective power, daily solace, and mental awakening derived from living according to Right View (sammādiṭṭhi) through six specific recollections and mental attitudes (the recollections of the Buddha, Dhamma, and Saṅgha, mindfulness of the body, delight in non-violence, and delight in meditation).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: A child from a Right View family, a child from a wrong-view family, two yakkhas (one devoted to the Triple Gem and one of wrong view), a carter (the second child's father), and the King of Rājagaha.

The Interaction: The two children regularly played a marble/ball game (guḷa-krīḍā) in Rājagaha. The child of Right View would say "Namo Buddhassa" whenever he threw the ball and consistently won. Observing this, the child of the wrong-view family began practicing the recollection of the Buddha (buddhānussati) by saying "Namo Buddhassa" as well. Later, his father took him into the forest to gather firewood. Upon returning near a cemetery outside the city, the oxen wandered off. By the time the father found the oxen, the city gates had closed for the night, leaving the young boy sleeping alone under the cart near the cemetery.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Two yakkhas saw the sleeping child. The wrong-view yakkha attempted to eat the child and grabbed him by the leg. Upon being pulled, the child instinctively cried out "Namo Buddhassa".

The Result / Resolution: The wrong-view yakkha froze in fear. Realizing they had committed a wrong act, both yakkhas protected the child through the night. The wrong-view yakkha brought food on a golden royal platter from the palace to feed the child. Using psychic power (iddhi), they inscribed a message on the platter for the king and left it in the cart. The next day, palace officials found the platter and arrested the child as a thief. Brought before the king, the child explained that his "parents" had cared for him overnight. The real parents arrived, and together with the king and child, visited the Buddha to ask if protection is obtained solely through buddhānussati or by other means. The Buddha preached Verses 296–301 of the Dhammapada, showing that keeping the mind directed toward these six subjects provides ultimate protection and enables one to awaken well-awakened (suppabuddhā) without external charms or mantras.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Right View (Sammādiṭṭhi) as the Foundation for Daily Solace:

Right View is the first step of the Noble Eightfold Path. Living illuminated by Right View allows one to obtain real protection, peace, and awakening in daily life, replacing the need for external mantras, charms, or superstitions.

2. The Protective Power of Buddhānussati as a Paritta:

Recollection of the Buddha (buddhānussati) functions as a protective paritta because the mass of Buddha-qualities (guṇakkhandha) naturally permeates the universe. Contemplating the Buddha dispels fear, trepidation, and harm, as corroborated in the Dhajagga Sutta and the Ratana Sutta.

3. True Meditation vs. Scholarly Distraction in Buddhānussati:

Practical meditation requires mental concentration (samādhi) and one-pointedness, achieved by plunging directly into a single quality to see the Buddha (as Prince Rāhula felt drawn to the Buddha). Analyzing endless etymologies or reciting elaborate scholarly interpretations causes mental distraction rather than calm concentration.

4. Analytical Breakdown of the Nine Supreme Buddha-Qualities (Navaguṇa):

Arahaṃ: Worthy of receiving both material offerings (āmisa-pūjā) and offerings of practice (paṭipatti-pūjā).

Sammāsambuddho: Fully Self-Awakened who realized the Four Noble Truths via self-sprung knowledge (svayaṃbhū-ñāṇa) without a teacher.

Vijjācaraṇasampanno: Complete in both insight/knowledge (vijjā, representing the eyes) and conduct (caraṇa, representing the feet).

Sugato: Arrived successfully at the end of the spiritual journey (Nibbāna).

Lokavidū: Knower of the world, comprehending both external world-systems (loka-dhātu) and the internal world of the individual.

Anuttaro purisadammasārathi: An unexcelled trainer who skillfully tames tamable beings through wisdom and compassion.

Satthā devamanussānaṃ: Supreme teacher of both gods and humans.

Buddho: Awakened through self-knowledge and endowed with the power to awaken others.

Bhagavā: Fortunate or blessed, possessing supreme saṃsāric merit, majestic fortune, and spiritual quality.

5. Natural Selection of a Single Quality for Concentration:

A meditator can narrow the nine qualities down to one single word that naturally resonates due to past saṃsāric connections (e.g., repeating "Bhagavā, Bhagavā"). Anchoring the mind on that chosen word brings immediate concentration without needing extensive verbalization.

6. The Six Qualities of the Dhamma (Dhammānussati):

Svākkhāto: Well-proclaimed, leading directly to the ultimate goal, good in the beginning, middle, and end.

Sandiṭṭhika: Directly visible and verifiable in this very life by reducing defilements (kilesas).

Akāliko: Yielding immediate results without delay; specifically seen in the fruit (phala) instantly following the supramundane path (lokuttara-magga).

Ehipassiko: Inviting one to "come and see"—demonstrating its practical, action-oriented nature.

Opanayiko: Leading onwards, drawing the practitioner sequentially from one stage of practice to the next.

Paccattaṃ veditabbo viññūhi: To be personally realized by the wise within their own minds.

7. The Qualities of the Saṅgha (Saṅghānussati):

The community of the eight Noble Persons (aṭṭha purisapuggalā / cattāri purisayugāni) is defined by four practice-qualities: practicing correctly (supaṭipanno), straightforwardly (ujupaṭipanno), systematically (ñāyapaṭipanno), and appropriately (sāmīcipaṭipanno). Consequently, they are worthy of brought offerings (āhuneyyo), hospitality (pāhuneyyo), gifts (dakkhiṇeyyo), reverent salutations (añjalikaraṇīyo), and serve as an unexcelled field of merit for the world (anuttaraṃ puññakkhettaṃ lokassa).

8. Mindfulness of the Body (Kāyagatāsati) and Its Three Forms:

The body is compared to a bag open at both ends containing impurities. It is contemplated in three ways:

Repulsiveness (Paṭikkūlamanasikāra): Seeing the 32 anatomical parts as impure.

Foulness (Asubha-bhāvanā): Contemplating the ten stages of decay (dasa-asubha) of a corpse to dismantle lust (rāga) and pride (māna).

Elements (Dhātu-kammaṭṭhāna): Viewing the body as composed of Earth (paṭhavī), Water (āpo), Fire (tejo), and Wind (vāyo), which matches the external elements, thus cultivating equanimity (upekkhā).

9. Deriving Spiritual Value from Bodily Waste:

Rather than ignoring daily bodily functions and waste products (e.g., spittle, feces, urine), a practitioner utilizes them as direct triggers for kāyagatāsati, reducing saṃsāric attachment to the "bodily cage" even during routine activities.

10. Delight in Non-Violence (Ahiṃsāya Rato Mano):

Rather than limiting this phrase strictly to compassion (karuṇā), it refers broadly to maintaining a daily attitude of non-harming, encompassing both loving-kindness (mettā) and compassion (karuṇā), enabling one to awaken peacefully without anxiety or regret.

11. Delight in Meditation (Bhāvanāya Rato Mano) Over Modern Distractions:

Giving priority to mental development (bhāvanā) in daily life counters the societal trend of falling asleep, awakening, and living in constant anxiety generated by media and artificial lifestyles.

12. Seeing Truth as It Is (Yathābhūta-Ñāṇa) Leads to Disillusionment (Nibbidā):

Contemplating the reality of the body and existence leads to yathābhūta-ñāṇa, which naturally triggers disillusionment (nibbidā) and dispassion (virāga), opening the door to Nibbāna.
