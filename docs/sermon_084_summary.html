Sermon 084 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #84 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #84

Primary Theme: The essential role of authentic faith (saddhā) cultivated through the recollection of the Buddha (buddhānussati) and the nine virtues (navaguṇa), liberating the mind from hindrances (nīvaraṇa) to enable direct insight into the Dhamma.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The matted-hair ascetic Keṇiya (jaṭila), the accomplished Vedic Brahmin Sela and his 300 young pupils, and the Supreme Buddha (along with Ven. Sāriputta).

The Interaction: While the Buddha was on a Dhamma tour in Aṅguttarāpa with 1,250 monks, Keṇiya heard the glorious report of the Buddha's nine virtues (ipi so Bhagavā...), visited the Buddha, and invited the Sangha for a meal/sacrifice. Brahmin Sela, a master of the three Vedas and the physical characteristics of a Great Man (mahāpurisa-lakkhaṇa), visited Keṇiya and observed elaborate preparations underway.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Upon learning that these preparations were for the "Buddha," Sela was deeply shaken, noting that even hearing the word Buddha is exceedingly rare in the world. Sela visited the Buddha, verified the 32 physical marks of a Great Man, and praised the Buddha in verse, urging him to rule as a Wheel-turning Monarch (Cakkavatti). The Buddha refuted this worldly identity, declaring himself to be the King of Dhamma (Dhammarājā) who turns the Wheel of Dhamma solely through Dhamma, with Ven. Sāriputta as his chief general, and proclaiming his Buddhahood as an incomparable surgeon (sallakatto anuttaro).

The Result / Resolution: Inspiring immense faith, Sela and his 300 disciples requested and received going forth (pabbajjā) and higher ordination (upasampadā). Putting forth strenuous meditation effort, all 301 monks attained Arahantship within seven days and returned to express their deep gratitude to the Buddha.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Transformative Power of the Nine Virtues (Navaguṇa): In ancient India, news of a Buddha's arising spread primarily through the recital of the nine virtues starting with Arahaṃ. Hearing even a single word among these virtues (such as "Buddha") was potent enough to trigger a massive psychological revolution in a person.

2. Critique of Scholastic Over-Analysis vs. Practical Meditation: Scholastic traditions often dissected terms like Arahaṃ into convoluted etymologies (e.g., "not committing sin in secret" via rahas, or "cutting the spokes of saṃsāra" via ara), which obscures the actual meaning—"worthiness of receiving offerings"—and distracts the meditator's mind. Meditating on the Navaguṇa should be like gazing at an eight- or nine-faceted gem: whichever facet one looks at, one should simply be reminded of the Buddha's pristine quality without running after peripheral derivations.

3. Analytical Breakdown of the Nine Virtues:

Bhagavā: The Blessed/Fortunate One; encapsulates the sum total of all internal and external perfections (pāramī).

Arahaṃ: Worthy of supreme offerings due to the absolute purity of his virtues.

Sammāsambuddho: Fully Awakened through self-attained higher knowledge (svayambhū-ñāṇa), without a teacher's instruction.

Vijjācaraṇasampanno: Perfect in both knowledge/vision (vijjā, like eyes) and behavioral conduct/virtue (caraṇa, like legs).

Sugato: "Well-gone"; one who has successfully traversed to the very end of the path.

Lokavidū: Knower of the worlds, possessing complete comprehension of all things internal and external.

Anuttaro Purisadammasārathi: Incomparable tamer of persons fit to be tamed, discerning the precise moment, place, and disposition of every individual.

Satthā devamanussānaṃ: Teacher of gods and humans alike.

Buddho: The Awakened One who has fully understood what is to be known, developed what is to be developed, and abandoned what is to be abandoned (abhiññeyyaṃ abhiññātaṃ...).

4. The Buddha as the Incomparable Surgeon (Sallakatto Anuttaro): While worldly surgeons perform physical procedures without saving one from Māra (death), the Buddha surgically removes the poison dart of craving (taṇhā) dipped in the poison of ignorance (avijjā-visadoso) from the heart. The nine virtues serve as the supreme surgeon's qualifications.

5. The Necessity of Faith (Saddhā) Over Pure Intellectualism: Modern rationalists erroneously believe everything can be achieved through logic alone. However, faith is essential to cross the torrent of saṃsāra (Saddhāya tarati oghaṃ). Faith generates the perception of capability (haki-saññā), giving one the courage to leap into the flood of practice and put forth the required energy (viriya).

6. Suppression of Hindrances (Nīvaraṇa-Vikkhambhana): When the mind is established on the virtues of the Buddha, unwholesome states like lust (rāga), hatred (dosa), and delusion (moha) cannot enter. The mind is temporarily freed from the five hindrances and draws close to concentration (samādhi), creating the ideal condition for realizing the noble paths and fruits (magga-phala) while listening to the Dhamma.

7. The Sequential Framework of Spiritual Progress (Caṅkī Sutta): Realization occurs through a structured progression: Faith arises (saddhā-jāto) → approaching the Teacher (upasaṅkamati) → attending closely (payirupāsati) → lending an ear (sotaṃ odahati) → hearing the Dhamma (dhammaṃ suṇāti) → retaining the teaching (dhāreti) → examining the meaning (atthaṃ upaparikkhati) → arising of eagerness/desire (chanda) → exerting effort (ussahati) → scrutinizing (tuleti) → striving (padahati) → realizing the ultimate truth (paramattha-saccaṃ sacchikaroti).

8. The Simile of the Water-Clearing Gem (Udaka-Prasādaka-Maṇi): Faith (saddhā) acts like a water-clearing gem dropped into muddy water: it causes the turbid mud of mental defilements to settle to the bottom, producing clear, purified mental clarity (pasāda) through which realization occurs.

9. Practical Methodology of Buddhānussati Practice: Meditators do not need to endlessly recite complex lists. Associating with (āsevati), developing (bhāveti), and practicing frequently (bahulīkaroti) even a single virtue that grasps the mind (e.g., Buddho, Sugato, or Purisadammasārathi) across all four postures is sufficient to anchor concentration and guard foundational practices like mindfulness of breathing (ānāpānasati).

10. Intellect (Buddhiya) vs. True Wisdom (Paññā): Intellectual cleverness is not equivalent to spiritual wisdom (paññā). True wisdom requires the balanced development of the five spiritual faculties (pañcindriya): faith (saddhā), energy (viriya), mindfulness (sati), concentration (samādhi), and wisdom (paññā). Skipping faith and energy to jump directly to intellectualization risks spiritual ruin.

11. The King of Dhamma (Dhammarājā) and Foremost Fruit (Aggappasāda Sutta): The Buddha turned the supreme Wheel of Dhamma rather than seeking temporal kingship. As declared in the Aggappasāda Sutta, the Buddha is the foremost of all beings; placing unwavering faith in the foremost yields the foremost spiritual reward—complete liberation in Nibbāna.
