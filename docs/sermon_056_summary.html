Sermon 056 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #56 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #56 (Sinhala Audio Sermon No. 70)

Primary Theme: The dual dimension of nursing the sick—combining physical compassionate care with timely Dhamma instruction—as a vehicle to contemplate the impermanent (anicca), painful (dukkha), and not-self (anattā) nature of the body, leading to the eradication of saṃsāric existence (bhava).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Venerable Pūtigatta Tissa ("Tissa of the Putrid Body"), a monk residing in Sāvatthī who, in a past life during the era of Kassapa Buddha, was a bird hunter.

The Interaction: Beholding Venerable Tissa through the attainment of Great Compassion (mahā-karuṇā-ñāṇa), the Buddha perceives that Tissa possesses the supportive conditions (upanissaya-sampatti) for Arahantship. The Buddha personally goes to the monastery, heats water in the fire-house, washes the monk’s sodden robes, and bathes his festering body with the assistance of the Sangha, restoring physical comfort.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Venerable Tissa suffers from a horrific, rotting bodily condition—eczema escalating into oozing boils, bursting abscesses, sodden garments, and broken bones—causing him to be abandoned in helplessness by his fellow monks. This severe physical agony is the karmic fruit (kamma-vipāka) of his past life as a bird hunter who broke the wings and legs of live birds to keep their meat fresh for sale. The turning point occurs when the physically cleansed Tissa lies comfortably on his bed, and the Buddha stands near his head to preach Dhammapada verse 41.

The Result / Resolution: Upon hearing the verse, Venerable Pūtigatta Tissa simultaneously penetrates the Dhamma, attains Arahantship with the four analytical knowledges (paṭisambhidā), and undergoes final extinguishment (parinibbāna). The Buddha subsequently cremated his body, erected a stupa enshrined with his relics, and explained the dual karmic causes of Tissa's physical suffering and ultimate spiritual liberation.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Dual Nursing: Physical Relief and Spiritual Healing (Dhammenāupaṭṭhāna)

The Buddha demonstrates that true nursing requires both physical care and Dhamma instruction. Physical care provides immediate mental composure (citta-pasāda), creating the optimal foundation for Dhamma advice (dhammānusāsani) to cure the underlying disease of saṃsāra (saṃsāra-roga).

2. The Inexorable Workings of Karma (Kamma-Vipāka)

Venerable Tissa’s putrid body and broken bones resulted directly from his past livelihood (ājīva) as a bird hunter who tortured animals. Conversely, his capacity to attain Arahantship in his final life stemmed from a past wholesome act: offering alms-food (piṇḍapāta) to an Arahant and making a firm spiritual aspiration (paṇidhāna).

3. Proper Exegesis of *Yo Gilānaṃ Upaṭṭheyya So Maṃ Upaṭṭheyya*

Ven. Nanananda Thero clarifies the famous Vinaya passage ("He who would nurse the sick, should nurse me"). Rather than implying that physical nursing is strictly identical to nursing the Buddha in fruitfulness, it denotes that fulfilling the Buddha’s injunction for mutual care within the monastic community (sabrahmacārī) constitutes a direct act of service and obedience to the Teacher.

4. Five Qualifications of a True Sick-Nurse (Gilānupaṭṭhāka)

A proper attendant must: (1) be competent in preparing medicine; (2) distinguish between suitable (sappāya) and unsuitable (asappāya) conditions; (3) nurse out of a mind of loving-kindness (mettā) free from expectations of gain; (4) handle bodily excretions (pus, phlegm, urine, feces) without disgust; and (5) be capable of delivering timely Dhamma talks to encourage the patient.

5. Five Characteristics of a Good Patient (Gilāna)

An easily nursed patient must: (1) adopt what is beneficial (sappāya); (2) know the correct moderation in remedies and food; (3) take prescribed medicine systematically; (4) report the actual progress or aggravation of the illness truthfully; and (5) bear severe physical pain (vedanā) patiently without lamentation.

6. The Body as a Useless Log (Kaliṅgara) and a "Cage of Disease"

Reflecting on Dhammapada verse 41 (Aciraṃ vatayaṃ kāyo...), the sermon highlights that once devoid of consciousness (apeta-viññāṇa), the physical body is cast aside like a worthless piece of timber. The Thero notes that "health" is merely hidden disease; affliction is the body's natural state, manifested continuously through baseline afflictions like hunger, thirst, heat, cold, defecation, and urination (sītaṃ uṇhaṃ jighacchā pipāsā uccāro passāvo).

7. Elevenfold Contemplation of Form (Rūpa-Manasikāra)

To produce disenchantment (nibbidā) towards the physical aggregate, one is instructed to contemplate bodily form in eleven distinct ways: as impermanent (aniccato), suffering (dukkhato), a disease (rogato), a boil (gaṇḍato), a dart (sallato), a calamity (aghato), an affliction (ābādhato), alien (parato), disintegrating (palokato), empty (suññato), and not-self (anattato).

8. Unafflicted Mind Amidst an Afflicted Body (Anātura-Citta)

Referencing the Buddha's advice to Nakulapitā (Ātura-kāyassa me sato, cittaṃ anāturaṃ bhavissati), the sermon emphasizes that while physical sickness (ātura-kāya) is inevitable due to bodily structure, the trained mind can remain completely unafflicted (anātura-citta) through wise contemplation (yoniso manasikāra).

9. Analytical Breakdown of the Threefold Suffering (Tividha Dukkhatā)

Raw Suffering (Dukkha-dukkhatā): The direct experience of bodily pain, illness, and physical discomfort.

Suffering Due to Change (Vipariṇāma-dukkhatā): The instability and pain inherent in pleasant feelings (sukha-vedanā) when they alter or reverse.

Suffering Inherent in Formations (Saṅkhāra-dukkhatā): The subtle, pervasive tension and burden involved in all conditioned mental and physical fabrications (saṅkhāra).

10. Transcendence of Conditioned Formations (Sabba-Saṅkhāra-Samatha)

Both meritorious (puñña) and unmeritorious (pāpa) fabrications propel existence (bhava) in saṃsāra. While wholesome deeds serve as essential rafts on the gradual training path (anupubba-paṭipadā), final Nibbāna is defined as the complete stilling of all preparations (sabba-saṅkhāra-samatha) and the total eradication of greed (lobha), hatred (dosa), and delusion (moha).
