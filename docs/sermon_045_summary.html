Sermon 045 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #45 Summary

Sermon #45 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #45

Primary Theme: The re-definition of conventional spiritual vocabulary through the Buddha’s Four Analytical Knowledges (catu-paṭisambhidā), the transcendence of views through non-grasping (anupādā-vimutti), and the realization of Nibbāna beyond duality (dvayatatā).

---

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Sabhiya the Wanderer (Sabhiya Paribrājaka). In a past life during the time of Kassapa Buddha, he was one of seven monks who climbed a cliff to meditate with life-sacrificing effort. In his present life, he was born to a female wanderer, became well-versed in the Vedas, and established his own hermitage.

The Interaction: A Suddhāvāsa Brahma (his former monastic companion who had attained Non-returning) appeared to Sabhiya at night and taught him twenty deep questions concerning spiritual terminology. He advised Sabhiya to seek out any recluse or brahmin who could answer them and to take ordination under that teacher. Sabhiya initially questioned the famous contemporary leaders (Pūraṇa Kassapa, Ajita Kesakambalī, Pakudha Kaccāyana, Nigaṇṭha Nātaputta).

The Disaster / Turning Point: Upon being asked these questions, the sectarian teachers became angry, agitated, and defensive, even asking Sabhiya himself for the answers. Deeply disillusioned, Sabhiya contemplated abandoning the ascetic life for sensual pleasures. He then remembered the young ascetic Gotama, traveled to Veḷuvanārāma in Rājagaha, and presented his sequence of questions to the Buddha.

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha answered all twenty questions smoothly and systematically. Overwhelmed with faith (saddhā), Sabhiya praised the Buddha in seven verses and requested ordination. Waiving the standard four-month probationary period (parivāsa) for sectarians due to his spiritual maturity, the Buddha ordained him immediately. Practicing diligently in seclusion, Venerable Sabhiya soon attained Arahantship.

---

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Four Analytical Knowledges (*Catu-Paṭisambhidā*):

The sermon showcases how the Buddha illuminates truth using the four analytical knowledges:

Attha-paṭisambhidā: Understanding the meaning/effect (atthanānatta).

Dhamma-paṭisambhidā: Penetrating the underlying cause or principle (dhamma-pabheda).

Nirutti-paṭisambhidā: Mastery over linguistic expression and etymology.

Paṭibhāna-paṭisambhidā: Ready wit, contextual illumination, and the spontaneous presentation of appropriate similes.

2. Spiritual Redefinition of Social Terminology:

The Buddha took pre-existing socio-religious terms used by wanderers and brahmins and filled them with ultimate Dhamma meaning:

Bhikkhu: One who has reached Parinibbāna via the self-cultivated path, crossed over doubt, abandoned both eternalist (bhava) and annihilationist (vibhava) views, and ended renewed existence (khīṇapunabbhavo).

Sorata (Gentle): One who is mindful and equanimous everywhere, non-harming, unperturbed, and free from internal "swellings" or prominences (ussadā) such as craving, conceit, and views.

Danta (Tamed): One whose sense faculties (indriya) are fully developed internally and externally, who has penetrated this world and the next, and who awaits the end of his lifespan with a tranquil mind.

Buddha: One who has scrutinized all mental fabrications/constructs (kappa), saṁsāra, and the process of passing away and arising (cutūpapāta); who is free from defilements (vigataraja), blemishless (anaṅgaṇa), pure (visuddha), and has attained the destruction of birth (jātikkhaya).

3. Etymological and Doctrinal Transformations of Key Terms:

Brāhmaṇa: Re-defined as sabbapāpaṁ bāhita—one who has warded off all evil.

Samaṇa: Re-defined as samitāvī—one who has quieted both merit and demerit, accomplishing the quieting of all fabrications (sabba-saṅkhāra-samatha).

Nahātaka (Cleansed): Not one who bathes externally in water, but one who has washed away all evil states (ninhāya sabbapāpakāni) internally and externally.

Nāga: Derived via etymology as āguṁ na karoti kiñci—one who commits no wrong/sin whatsoever and is liberated from all fetters (saṁyojana).

Khettajina (Conqueror of Fields): One who understands divine, human, and brahma fields, and is liberated from the root-bond of karma (kammaṁ khettaṁ).

Kusala (Skilful): One who has dismantled the root-bond that leads into "sheaths" (kosa) of heavenly or brahma existences.

Vedagū (Knowledge-attained): Re-defined not as mastery over the three Vedas, but as being passionless towards all feelings (sabbavedanāsu vītarāgo) and transcending sensation (sabbaṁ vedaṁ aticca).

Anuvidita (Fully Understood): One who has understood how Name-and-Form (nāmarūpa) is generated through prolific conceptualization (papañca) as the root of all disease (rogamūla).

Paribbājaka (Wanderer): Re-defined as total avoidance (parivajjana) of evil, faring with full understanding (pariññācārī), and seeing the ultimate limit/end of Name-and-Form (pariyantaṁ akāsi nāmarūpaṁ).

4. Critique of the Sixty-Three Assertions of Recluses (*Samaṇappavāda*):

Addressing Sabhiya's verse regarding sixty-three assertions (yāni ca tīṇi yāni ca saṭṭhi), Venerable Nanananda critiques the commentarial interpretation which merely adds self-illusion (sakkāya-diṭṭhi) to the sixty-two views of the Brahmajāla Sutta. Citing the Saḷāyatana Saṁyutta (Citta the Householder's dialogue with Ven. Isidatta), he highlights that the sixty-two views arise because of sakkāya-diṭṭhi. Thus, sakkāya-diṭṭhi cannot be logically appended as a distinct sixty-third view; rather, the sixty-three assertions represent all conceptual standpoints grounded in perception and letters (saññā-nisitāni).

5. Anāthapiṇḍika’s Analysis of Views (Aṅguttara Nikāya, Dasaka Nipāta):

Anāthapiṇḍika the Householder demonstrated Stream-entry wisdom when refuting sectarians regarding the ten undeclared (avyākata) issues. He explained that every speculative view arises through unwise attention (ayoniso manasikāra), is conditioned/prepared (saṅkhata), volitionally thought out (cetayita), dependently arisen (paṭiccasamuppanna), and impermanent (anicca).

6. The Concept of *Osaraṇa* vs. *Nissaraṇa*:

Osaraṇa (Resorts/Inflows): Views and assertions act as internal pull-factors (osaraṇāni) that drag the mind back into saṁsāra and reinforce attachment to letters and concepts.

Nissaraṇa (Escape): True liberation consists in seeing the escape beyond all constructed views. Anāthapiṇḍika saw the impermanence of views and realized the escape from them, refusing to cling even to the theoretical statement of impermanence itself.

7. The Simile of the Raft (*Kulla-ūpama*) and Non-Grasping of Right View:

Right View (sammā-diṭṭhi) functions purely as a instrument for crossing the flood (oghataraṇa), not as a dogma to be carried on one's shoulders. Grasping even Right View transforms it into another conceptual baggage. Liberation is realized through non-grasping (anupādā vimutto).

8. Transcendence of Duality (*Dvayatatā*):

The ultimate goal requires abandoning both sides of worldly duality—abandoning both merit and demerit (puññapāpa-pahīna), and being unattached to both the near shore and the far shore (pārāpāra). Just as a lotus is unsmeared by water (toyena na upalippati), the Arahant remains unsmeared by wholesome and unwholesome constructs.

9. The Danger of Grasping the Snake by the Tail (*Alagaddūpama Sutta*):

Venerable Nanananda warns against prematurely rejecting moral practices or Right View under the pretense of "abandoning merit and demerit." Doing so is like grasping a water-snake by its tail—it leads to wrong view and self-destruction. The raft is only abandoned after reaching the other shore, using the creeper of insight (vipassanā) to leap across the stream of existence before letting go completely.

10. The Function of *Yāvadeva* and Direct Insight (*Dassana*):

Using the simile of the relay chariots (Rathavinīta Sutta), the term yāvadeva ("solely for the purpose of") illustrates that every stage of purification is merely a means to an end. True practice (paṭipatti) leads to penetration (paṭivedha), transforming book knowledge into direct vision (dassana) via the Dhamma-eye (dhamma-cakkhu), realizing the cessation of the world within this fathom-long body.
