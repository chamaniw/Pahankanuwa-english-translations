Sermon 115 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #115 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #115 (Pahankaṇuwa Sinhala Audio Dhamma Sermon No. 173)

Primary Theme: The distinction between personal friendship (mittatā) and universal loving-kindness (mettā), the identification of true versus counterfeit friends in lay life, and the absolute necessity of spiritual friendship (kalyāṇamittatā) as the complete holy life (brahmacariya) leading to Nibbāna.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Prince Temiya (the Bodhisatta born as the son of the King of Kāsi in Benares) and Sunanda the royal charioteer.

The Interaction: After sixteen years of Prince Temiya feigning to be a mute, deaf cripple, his despondent father commands the royal charioteer, Sunanda, to take the prince in a chariot to a cemetery deep in the forest and bury him alive in a grave.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Prince Temiya’s feigned disability was driven by his recollection of past lives (jātissara-ñāṇa), through which he remembered having ruled Benares for twenty years in a past life and consequently suffering in hell (niraya) for 80,000 years. Fearing the karmic peril of kingship, he endured sixteen years of self-imposed torment—even wallowing in his own excrement. When Sunanda stops the chariot in the forest and begins digging the grave, Prince Temiya secretly tests his strength, steps out of the chariot, walks over to Sunanda, and reveals that he is neither deaf, mute, nor crippled. Seeing Sunanda's hesitation, Temiya warns him that killing a person whose protective shadow he enjoyed is an act of betraying a friend (mittadūbhī).

The Result / Resolution: Prince Temiya recites the ten verses of the Mittānisaṁsa Sutta expounding the supreme blessings of non-betrayal of friends. He renounces royal wealth and kingship to live as an ascetic in the forest, transforming Sunanda's perspective and establishing the foundational doctrine on the ethical and karmic power of true friendship.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Analytical Distinction Between Friendship (Mittatā) and Loving-Kindness (Mettā):

Ven. Nanananda Thero delineates six fundamental differences to resolve common confusions between the two concepts:

Friendship is personal; loving-kindness (mettā) is universal.

Friendship is mutual; mettā is universally impartial.

Friendship is narrow; mettā is broad.

Friendship is a bond (bandhana); mettā is a deliverance of mind (cetovimutti).

Friendship requires at least two individuals; mettā can be fully developed in solitude.

Friendship is limited; mettā is limitless, immeasurable, and infinite.

2. Critique of the Visuddhimagga’s Method of Mettā Meditation:

The Sermon critiques Ven. Buddhaghosa’s method in the Visuddhimagga, which bases mettā-bhāvanā on categorizing individuals (oneself, neutral person, dear friend, hostile person). Ven. Nanananda points out that according to the Buddha's original discourses, the true foundation of mettā is the universal, shared aspiration for happiness inherent in all sentient beings. Thus, authentic mettā practice should be cultivated from the beginning as a directional radiation (disā-pharaṇa) rather than through narrow personal classifications.

3. Solitary Spiritual Practice vs. Friendship (Khaggavisāṇa Sutta):

Referencing the Khaggavisāṇa Sutta ("Rhinoceros Horn Discourse"), the sermon highlights the ancient ideal of solitary forest sages wandering alone (eko care khaggavisāṇakappo). Even in total isolation, such sages cultivate the four divine abodes (brahmavihāra—mettā, karuṇā, muditā, upekkhā) as deliverances of mind (cetovimutti), proving that mettā does not depend on social proximity or external companionship.

4. Analysis of Counterfeit Friends (Mitta-Paṭirūpaka):

Drawing from the Sigālovāda Sutta, the Buddha identifies four types of enemies disguised as friends ("puppet friends"):

Aññadatthuhara: The exploitative/selfish friend who gives little only to take much.

Vacīparama: The friend in words only, who offers lip service and past/future promises but no practical help.

Anuppiyabhāṇī: The flatterer who approves of wrong deeds, praises to one's face, and backbites behind one's back.

Apāyasahāya: The companion in ruin who leads one into alcoholism, drug addiction, and ruinous vices.

5. Analysis of Warm-Hearted Friends (Suhada-Mitta):

The Sigālovāda Sutta outlines four genuine, warm-hearted friends essential for worldly and spiritual stability:

Upakāraka: The helpful friend who protects one and one's property during negligence and serves as a refuge in fear.

Samānasukhadukkha: The friend in joy and sorrow, who keeps secrets, does not abandon one in adversity, and is willing to sacrifice life itself.

Atthakkhāyī: The mentor who restrains one from evil, directs one to the wholesome, teaches the Dhamma, and points the way to a happy rebirth (sugati).

Anukampaka: The compassionate friend who sympathizes with one's misfortunes, rejoices in one's success, and defends one's reputation.

6. The Child-on-Chest Simile for Utmost Trust (Hiri Sutta):

The sermon cites a poignant simile from the Hiri Sutta: a true friend is one upon whom a person can rest with total trust, like a young child falling asleep peacefully on its parent's chest (yasmiñca seti urasīva putto). This stands in sharp contrast to pseudo-friends who constantly scan for faults and seek opportunities to cause division (bheda-saṅkī randham evānupassī).

7. The Four Bases of Sympathy (Cattu Saṅgahavatthu) and Non-Deceitfulness (Avisaṅvādanatā):

To maintain healthy social and familial bonds (symbolized as the Northern direction in the Sigālovāda Sutta), one must practice generosity (dāna), pleasant speech (peyyavajja), useful conduct (atthacariyā), and impartiality/equality (samānattatā), anchored by strict non-deceitfulness (avisaṅvādanatā).

8. Spiritual Friendship (Kalyāṇamittatā) as the Entire Holy Life:

In response to Ven. Ānanda’s statement that spiritual friendship is half of the holy life, the Buddha explicitly corrected him, declaring that kalyāṇamittatā is the entire holy life (sakalam ev'idaṁ brahmacariyaṁ). Relying on a spiritual friend is the single external factor responsible for bringing the Noble Eightfold Path (ariya-aṭṭhaṅgika-magga) to full unfoldment.

9. The Dawn Simile (Aruṇugga Sutta Context):

Just as the red dawn light (aruṇa) in the sky is the indispensable forerunner and harbinger of the rising sun, so too is spiritual friendship (kalyāṇamittatā) the ultimate forerunner for the arising of the Noble Eightfold Path within a practitioner's mind.

10. Seven Qualities of a Genuine Spiritual Teacher / Friend:

The sermon highlights seven canonical criteria required of a monastic or teacher before one should follow them (even if initially turned away, paṇujjamāno'pi):

Piyo: Endearing and pleasant.

Garu: Respectable and venerable.

Bhāvanīyo: Adorable/worthy of emulation.

Vattā: Capable of giving effective counsel and admonition.

Vacanakkhamo: Patient under criticism; willing to accept correction gracefully.

Gambhīrañca kathaṁ kattā: Skilled in expounding deep Dhamma discussions.

No caṭṭhāne niyojaye: Refraining from directing disciples to inappropriate or harmful paths.

11. Human Self-Interest vs. Karmic Reality (Attatthapaññā):

Ven. Nanananda points to the tragic reality of unpurified human nature expressed in the suttas: "humans are wise only for their own self-interest, hence they are impure" (attatthapaññā asucī manussā). While counterfeit friends may seem to gain temporary worldly victories through exploitation, the unyielding law of kamma (kamma-vipāka) ensures their ultimate spiritual downfall.

12. The Buddha as the Ultimate Kalyāṇamitta:

The sermon concludes by emphasizing that the Supreme Buddha is the ultimate Kalyāṇamitta for all beings. By relying upon his Dhamma, beings subject to birth (jāti-dhammā), decay (jarā-dhammā), disease, and death (marana-dhammā) gain complete emancipation from the cycle of saṁsāra, realizing the supreme, deathless Nibbāna through the four stages of awakening (Sotāpanna, Sakadāgāmi, Anāgāmi, Arahant).
