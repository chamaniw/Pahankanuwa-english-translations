Sermon 024 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #24 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #24

Primary Theme: The preservation of the Buddha Sāsana through pure monastic conduct, proper lay-monk relationships based on the Fourfold Purification of Virtue (Catu-pārisiddhi-sīla), and the practical application of the Ten Reflections (Dasa Dhamma Sutta) to stem the tide of modern decline.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Venerable Tissa, a monk possessing immaculate virtue (sīla) preserved without blemish from the day of his higher ordination (upasampadā).

The Interaction: Seeking a congenial (sappāya) place for meditation, Ven. Tissa enters a mountain cave. The resident cave deity, recognizing the monk's superior virtue, cannot reside above him and vacates the cave, expecting the monk to leave soon. However, Ven. Tissa accepts a three-month rainy season retreat (vassāna) invited by a pious lay woman (upāsikā).

The Disaster / Turning Point: Wanting to evict the monk without valid grounds, the deity possesses the lay woman's eldest son, causing violent fits. Speaking through the child, the deity demands that the woman obtain medical treatment (wild ginger vine, beehive oil, asafoetida) from Ven. Tissa. Knowing that practicing medicine is a wrong livelihood (micchā-ājīva) for monks, the lay woman refuses even at the risk of her son's life. She agrees only to sprinkle water used to wash the monk's feet on the child's head. The child is cured, but when Ven. Tissa returns to the cave, the deity blocks the entrance and falsely accuses him: "Great physician, do not enter here!"

The Result / Resolution: Ven. Tissa realizes the accusation is entirely baseless and stems from the deity's inability to find any true defect in his conduct. Reflecting on his immaculate virtue (sīlānussati), immense joy and rapture (pīti, pāmojja) arise within him. Subduing this rapture, he establishes deep concentration (samādhi) and attains Arahatship on the spot before taking another step. He banishes the deity, completes his retreat, and later confirms to the Buddha that he felt no anger (dosa) toward his accuser. The Buddha endorses his realization, uttering Dhammapada verse 404.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Understanding Allowable (Kappa) vs. Unallowable (Akappa) Conduct

The ancient lay woman demonstrates the vital role laypeople play in safeguarding the Sangha's virtue. By refusing to ask a monk for medical help—even to save her child—she respected the boundary separating allowable practice from wrong livelihood (micchā-ājīva) and family corruption (kula-dūsana).

2. The Fourfold Purification of Virtue (Catu-pārisiddhi-sīla)

The structural integrity of a monk's life rests on four pillars:

Pātimokkha-saṁvara-sīla: Restraint according to the monastic disciplinary code.

Indriya-saṁvara-sīla: Restraint of the six sense faculties against incoming defilements (kilesa).

Ājīva-pārisiddhi-sīla: Purification of livelihood by avoiding improper intimacy with lay devotees to obtain requisites.

Paccaya-sannissita-sīla: Wise reflection on the four requisites (robes, almsfood, lodging, medicine) as mere elements (dhātu-manasikāra) or repulsive (asubha-manasikāra) to eradicate greed and advance toward Nibbāna.

3. Monastic Decline and the "Cock’s Comb" Simile

When laypeople treat monks as tools for social, mundane, or economic convenience, monks deviate from ājīva-pārisiddhi-sīla. Over-intimacy leads laypeople to lose reverence for the robe, illustrated by the adage: "The comb of the cock seen morning and night looks white." Monks are degraded to the status of ordinary laypersons in function, destroying their core spiritual purpose.

4. Reflection on Virtue (Sīlānussati) as an Instantaneous Vehicle for Awakening

When falsely accused, Ven. Tissa did not react with ill-will (dosa), but used his virtue as a mirror (paccavekkhaṇa). The intense rapture (pāmojja) born of spotless virtue, when calmed and concentrated, yields direct meditative absorption (samādhi) and liberation.

5. Strategic Purpose of Ven. Mahinda’s Mission Group

The mission sent to Sri Lanka comprised five higher-ordained monks (the minimum required to confer upasampadā), one novice (sāmaṇera Sumana), and one non-returner lay disciple (anāgāmī Bhanduka). Bhanduka’s role was specifically to educate the local population on the proper lay-monk dynamic and the gradual progression from lay life to ordination.

6. Altered Appearance (Vevaṇṇiya) and Dependent Livelihood (Parapaṭibaddhā)

The Dasa Dhamma Sutta mandates daily monastic reflections:

Vevaṇṇiyaṁ upagato: Acknowledging an altered physical appearance (kāya-vevaṇṇiya) via a shaved head and discolored, patched robes (parikkhāra-vevaṇṇiya), renouncing worldly fashions and luxury.

Parapaṭibaddhā me jīvikā: Remembering that one's survival depends entirely on alms offered in faith, keeping the monk humble and focused on acting as an unblemished field of merit (puññakkhetta).

7. Deportment of Postures (Ākappasampatti) Like a Stream of Oil

A monk must maintain a distinct, restrained behavior (añño me ākallo karaṇīyo) that inspires clarity in the unclarified (appasannānaṁ vā prasādāya). Monastic posture should flow softly and smoothly, summarized by the traditional simile: "Siniddhā tela dhārāva ahosi iriyāpatho"—moving calmly like a smooth stream of poured oil.

8. Internal Moral Scrutiny and Karma Ownership

Monks must constantly examine whether their own mind blames them regarding virtue (attā sīlato na upavadati) and whether wise fellow-practitioners (viññū sabrahmacārī) could find fault. Furthermore, grounding oneself daily in the law of karma (kammassako'mhi) provides fearlessness and equanimity amidst worldly hardships.

9. The Crowning Reflection: Superhuman Attainment (Uttari-manussa-dhamma)

The tenth reflection of the Dasa Dhamma Sutta challenges the monk to consider whether, at the moment of death, they can face fellow monastics without shame or regret regarding their realization of noble path-attainments (jhāna, paths, and fruits). Realizing the supramundane is the sole reason the monastic robe-belt is tied.

10. The Threefold Sāsana and Modern Misconceptions

The Sāsana consists of three interdependent stages: Pariyatti (learning/scripture), Paṭipatti (practice), and Paṭivedha (penetration/realization). Modern trends mistake high volumes of academic study or digital access to texts (carrying the Tipiṭaka in one's pocket) for progress. Without paṭipatti and paṭivedha, external knowledge remains sterile.

11. Timelessness of the Path to Liberation

Declaring that Nibbāna or Arahatship is impossible in the modern era is a dangerous misconception. As declared by the Buddha, as long as the 37 Aids to Enlightenment (bodhipakkhiyā dhammā) are cultivated, the world will not be empty of Arahats (suññā loka arPerformance/Arahattehi). Realization requires internal practice rather than external scientific research.
