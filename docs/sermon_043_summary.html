Sermon 043 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #43 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #43 (Sinhala Audio No. 206)

Primary Theme: Correcting commentarial misinterpretations of the Virtues of the Saṅgha (Saṅghaguṇa) by distinguishing between instantaneous path-moments (magga) and extended practical training (paṭipannā/paṭipadā); elucidating the status of entry-level Noble disciples (dhammānusārī and saddhānusārī), the nature of gotrabhū, and the purification of offerings (dakkhiṇā-visuddhi) made to the supreme field of merit (anuttaraṃ puññakkhettaṃ lokassa).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Mahāpajāpatī Gotamī (the Buddha’s foster-mother) and Ven. Ānanda.

The Interaction: At Nigrodhārāma in Kapilavatthu, Mahāpajāpatī Gotamī approaches the Buddha with a pair of new, high-quality cloths that she had personally spun and woven with her own hands, requesting that the Blessed One accept them out of compassion.

The Disaster / Turning Point: The Buddha declines to accept the cloths as a personal, individual gift (pāṭipuggalikā dakkhiṇā), instructing her instead: "Saṅghe, gotami, dehi; saṅghe dinne ahañceva pūjito bhavissāmi..." ("Gotamī, give it to the Saṅgha. When it is offered to the Saṅgha, I too will be honored"). Even after Ven. Ānanda intercedes, reminding the Buddha of how she lovingly nursed and raised him after his birth mother died, the Buddha maintains that personal offerings are inferior to communal offerings given to the Saṅgha.

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha expounds the Dakkhiṇāvibhaṅga Sutta, outlining the 14 types of individual offerings and the 7 types of communal offerings to the Saṅgha. By redirecting her gift to the Saṅgha, the Buddha elevates her merit from a finite personal act to an incalculable, immeasurable reward, thereby honoring his foster-mother at the highest possible level ("paying the milk bill") while establishing the absolute spiritual preeminence of the Saṅgha-treasure (Saṅgha-ratana).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Distinction Between Path (Magga) and Practice (Paṭipadā / Paṭipannā):

The supramundane path (magga) is an instantaneous concentration (ānantarika-samādhi) that leads immediately into its corresponding fruit (phala). Therefore, the traditional commentarial division of "four paths and four fruits" (satara-maṅga satara-phala) as eight distinct static individuals (maggaṭṭha and phalaṭṭha) creates a logical impossibility when trying to give alms separately to someone "on the path." The Buddha deliberately used the term paṭipannā ("one who has entered upon the practice") rather than maggaṭṭha, referring to an ongoing, temporal process of spiritual training (paṭipadā).

2. Correction of the Commentarial Misreading Aṭṭhasataṃ (108 Persons):

Ven. Nanananda points out a textual distortion from the Commentarial period regarding the verse "ye puggalā aṭṭha sataṃ pasatthā" (Ratana Sutta). Grammatically, aṭṭha sataṃ means "eight individuals praised by the virtuous (sataṃ = santo)." Incompetent reciters misread this as aṭṭhasatā ("one hundred and eight"), and Commentators like Ven. Buddhaghosa compounded the error by constructing elaborate lists of 108 individuals. The original sutta text refers strictly to eight noble individuals, not 108.

3. The Entry-Level Noble Disciples (Dhammānusārī and Saddhānusārī):

Those "practicing for the realization of the fruit of Stream-entry" (sotāpattiphala-sacchikiriyāya paṭipanno) are specifically identified in suttas like the Okkantika Saṃyutta and Cūḷagopālaka Sutta as the Dhamma-follower (dhammānusārī, guided by wisdom) and the Faith-follower (saddhānusārī, guided by faith). Though not yet Stream-enterers (Sotāpanna), they possess the five spiritual faculties (indriya) in an established state and are guaranteed not to pass away without realizing Stream-entry.

4. Entry into the Law of Rightness (Sammatta-Niyāma):

Both the dhammānusārī and saddhānusārī have stepped onto the plane of the virtuous (sappurisa-bhūmi) and transcended the status of an ordinary worldling (puthujjana). They are declared incapable (abhabba) of committing any grave evil that would lead to rebirth in hell, the animal realm, or the ghost realm.

5. Critique of the Commentarial Ṭhitakappī Concept:

The Puggalapaññatti treatise claims that a dhammānusārī or saddhānusārī is ṭhitakappī—meaning they possess the power to "halt the ending of an eon (kappa)" until they attain Stream-entry. Ven. Nanananda rejects this literalist exaggeration as "putting the cart before the horse." The Buddha’s term abhabba simply signifies that if such a practitioner has not realized the fruit during their lifetime, they will inevitably realize Stream-entry at the moment of death.

6. Simile of the Wise Herdsman (Cūḷagopālaka Sutta):

The Buddha compares himself to a wise herdsman crossing cattle across the Ganges at a safe ford. The lead bulls represent the Arahants; the strong bulls represent Non-returners and Once-returners; the heifers represent Stream-enterers; and the newborn calf—crossing safely merely by hearing its mother’s lowing (mātugāviyā rāvena)—represents the dhammānusārī and saddhānusārī. All are bound for enlightenment (sambodhiparāyaṇā).

7. Twofold Meaning of Gotrabhū (Change-of-Lineage):

Gotrabhū in Insight Meditation (vipassanā-ñāṇa-vīthi): The momentary, subtle cognitive moment of letting go of all preparations (saṅkhāra), illustrated by a man swinging across a stream on a hanging creeper who releases his grip right before landing on the opposite bank.

Gotrabhū in Social/Monastic Context (kāsāvakaṇṭha gotrabhū): In the Dakkhiṇāvibhaṅga Sutta, it refers to future degenerate monks who wear merely a fragment of a yellow robe around their necks. They represent the "Noble lineage" (ariya-gotra) in name and appearance only.

8. Four Modes of Purification of an Offering (Dakkhiṇā-Visuddhi):

An offering can be: (1) purified by the giver, not the recipient; (2) purified by the recipient, not the giver; (3) purified by neither; or (4) purified by both. An offering given to an unvirtuous kāsāvakaṇṭha monk is purified on the part of the giver (dāyakato visujjhati) if offered with a pure mind focused on the Saṅgha-treasure (Saṅgha-saññā), yielding incalculable merit for the giver even though the unvirtuous recipient incurs grave evil.

9. Symbolic Power of the Monk’s Robe and the Eight Requisites (Aṭṭha-Parikkhāra):

The yellow robe functions as a powerful mental signifier (saññā) of the Noble Saṅgha, capable of inspiring wholesome faith across generations (similar to a stupa's visual impact, cetiya-saññā). The eight requisites are the minimal structural support for monastic ordination (pabbajjā); modern expansions into "sixty requisites" (häṭa-pirikara) represent commercialized distortions that risk eroding the core symbol of renunciation.

10. The Pure Mindset of the Lay Giver (Example of Ugga Gahapati):

The lay disciple Ugga Gahapati of Hatthigāma (an Anāgāmī) was privately informed by deities about the varying spiritual attainments or ethical failings of individual monks. Nevertheless, when serving alms, he maintained complete equanimity and made no mental distinction whatsoever, offering to all with equal devotion to the Saṅgha. Lay disciples should cultivate this pure, non-judgmental stance rather than trying to audit monastic purity.

11. Diligence (Appamāda) vs. Spiritual Apathy over Dispensation Decline:

Although many cite the impermanence (aniccatā) of the Buddha’s dispensation (sāsana) to justify apathy or accept decline passively, Ven. Nanananda urges practitioners to exert the same energetic effort to preserve the Dhamma as one would spend on medical treatments to prolong bodily life. Diligent effort in practical training (paṭipadā) ensures individual liberation "before the lamp of the dispensation is extinguished."
