Sermon 184 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #184 Summary

Here is a scholar's analysis and structured summary of Pahankanuwa Sermon #184 by Ven. Katukurunde Nanananda Thero.

---

Sermon #184 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #184 (Paticca Samuppada Sermon 2: The Law of Dependent Arising)

Primary Theme: The mutual conditionality (aññamañña paccayatā) between Consciousness (viññāṇa) and Name-and-Form (nāmarūpa); the release from the saṁsāric vortex (vaṭṭa) through Radical Attention (yoniso manasikāra); and the realization of Non-Manifestative Consciousness (anidassana viññāṇa).

---

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Bodhisatta Vipassi (recounted in the Mahāpadāna Sutta), whose contemplative journey serves as the core canonical narrative framework of the sermon, complemented by the inquiring monk in the Kevaḍḍha Sutta and Bodhisatta Mahosadha in the Ummagga Jātaka.

The Interaction: Prior to his awakening, Bodhisatta Vipassi engaged in radical attention (yoniso manasikāra) to trace the causal sequence of saṁsāric suffering backward link by link, starting from decay and death (jarāmaraṇa), through birth (jāti), becoming (bhava), grasping (upādāna), craving (taṇhā), feeling (vedanā), contact (phassa), and the six sense-spheres (saḷāyatana), down to name-and-form (nāmarūpa) and consciousness (viññāṇa).

The Disaster / Turning Point: Upon reaching consciousness, Bodhisatta Vipassi observed that consciousness does not go beyond name-and-form but turns back (paccudāvattati kho idaṁ viññāṇaṁ nāmarūpamhā, nāparaṁ gacchati). He realized that existence is trapped in a closed, reciprocal whirlpool (vaṭṭa) where viññāṇa and nāmarūpa continually sustain one another, creating the perverted illusion of a localized "self" or "existence" (bhava).

The Result / Resolution: By applying radical attention to the cessation order (nirodha-krama), Bodhisatta Vipassi saw how the cessation of name-and-form leads to the cessation of consciousness's vortical interplay. Contemplating the arising and passing away (udayabbaya) of the five aggregates of grasping (pañcupādānakkhandhā), he broke the cycle, exposed the magical illusion of consciousness, and attained full Enlightenment (Buddhahood).

---

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Vortex Metaphor of Saṁsāra (*Saṁsāra Vaṭṭa*):

Saṁsāric existence operates like a river whirlpool. Driven by ignorance (avijjā) and preparations (saṅkhārā), a runaway current flows against the mainstream, clashes, turns round, and digs an abyss. This funnel creates a vacuum—symbolizing craving (taṇhā)—which develops a force of attraction, symbolizing grasping (upādāna). The resulting vortical interplay between consciousness and name-and-form creates a localized, perverted notion of a persistent "here" and "there" (an illusory self).

2. Mutual Conditionality (*Aññamañña Paccayatā*) of *Viññāṇa* and *Nāmarūpa*:

As revealed in the Sara Sutta (SN 1.67) and Mahāpadāna Sutta (DN 14), consciousness (viññāṇa) and name-and-form (nāmarūpa) are mutually dependent. Consciousness cannot proceed independently of name-and-form; it turns back (paccudāvattati). This inter-locking relationship is the pivot of the entire twelve-link chain of Dependent Arising (paṭicca samuppāda).

3. Correction of the Canonical Definition of 'Name' (*Nāma*):

Ven. Nanananda reiterates the authentic Sutta definition of nāma: feeling (vedanā), perception (saññā), intention (cetanā), contact (phassa), and attention (manasikāra). "Name" is only formal name, and "form" (rūpa) is only nominal form—recognized mentally via those five factors operating on the four great primaries (mahābhūta). Standard commentary mistakes that include consciousness inside nāma distort the middle position that consciousness holds relative to name and form.

4. The Five-Finger Simile and Legal Strategy for Attention (*Manasikāra*):

Using a hand illustration, the five factors of nāma are mapped to the hand: Little Finger = Feeling, Ring Finger = Perception, Middle Finger = Intention, Index Finger = Contact, and the Thumb = Attention. Attention (manasikāra) is the ringleader that originally leads one astray via non-radical attention (ayonisomanasikāra). Like a judge granting pardon to a key accomplice to turn state's evidence, the Buddha transforms non-radical attention into radical attention (yonisomanasikāra), exposing the entire illusion of consciousness.

5. Non-Manifestative Consciousness (*Anidassana Viññāṇa*):

Analyzing the Kevaḍḍha Sutta (DN 11), the sermon clarifies viññāṇaṁ anidassanaṁ anantaṁ sabbatopabhaṁ ("Consciousness that is non-manifestative, endless, lustrous on all sides"). In this state, the four elements find no footing (na gādhati), and dualities like long/short, subtle/gross, comely/ugly, and name-and-form are held in check with no trace left (asesaṁ uparujjhati). This occurs with the cessation of specifically prepared/deluded consciousness (abhisaṅkhata-viññāṇa).

6. Reframing Element Cessation: 'Not Finding a Footing' (*Na Gādhati*):

The Buddha corrected the monk in the Kevaḍḍha Sutta who sought the physical destruction of the four elements. The true question is not where physical elements are destroyed, but where their mental footing/perception (rūpasaññā) ceases to establish itself. Enlightenment does not destroy the physical universe; it dissolves the conceptual grasping and perception of form.

7. Illuminating the Illusory Projection (The Cinema and Mirror Similes):

Consciousness functions like a magic show, a mirage, or a reflection (as in the Ummagga Jātaka's gem-in-the-pond narrative and the updated Buddhist story of Narcissus). Deluded by ayonisomanasikāra, one treats the projected reflection as an actual object. When the "lustre of wisdom" (paññāpabhā) illuminates the mind—like turning on the lights in a cinema hall—the projected manifestative images vanish, leaving only the unestablished screen.

8. Craving as the Seamstress (*Sibbanī*):

Reflecting on the Tissametteyya Sutta (Sutta Nipāta), craving is identified as the "seamstress" (sibbanī). Craving stitches together two ends—such as Name (nāma) and Form (rūpa), or Internal Sense-Spheres and External Sense-Spheres—while obscuring the mediating role of Consciousness (viññāṇa) in the middle. Wisdom unties this knot by recognizing consciousness in the middle.

9. All Phenomena Originate from Attention and Rooted in Interest:

Citing foundational Sutta passages:

Manasikārasambhavā sabbe dhammā: All phenomena/mind-objects originate from attention. A "thing" becomes a "thing" for an individual only when attention isolates it.

Chandamūlakā sabbe dhammā: All phenomena are rooted in interest/desire (chanda). Chanda represents the subtle, initial shade of craving (taṇhā) that brings objects into subjective existence (bhava).

10. The Distinction Between Consciousness (*Viññāṇa*) and Wisdom (*Paññā*):

The Suttas state: paññā bhāvetabbā, viññāṇaṁ pariññeyyaṁ ("Wisdom is to be developed, consciousness is to be fully comprehended"). Developing wisdom serves the purpose of exposing the deceptive nature of consciousness. As wisdom increases, the spell of consciousness breaks (viññāṇassa nirodhena), bringing an end to the round of saṁsāric suffering (saṁsāra vaṭṭa).
