Sermon 061 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #61 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #61 (Sinhala Audio Sermon No. 88)

Primary Theme: The essential role of balanced energy (vīriya-samatā) and the harmonization of the spiritual faculties (indriya-samatā) in the realization of Nibbāna, countering both extreme over-exertion, physical/mental indolence, and the dangerous heresy of the doctrine of non-action (akiriya-vāda).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Soṇa Kolivisa, a delicate millionaire’s son from the city of Campā born with a rare, miraculous physical feature—hair growing on the soles of his feet.

The Interaction: King Bimbisāra summons Soṇa to inspect his feet and subsequently directs eighty thousand village chiefs to visit the Buddha at Vulture’s Peak (Gijjhakaṭa). After Venerable Sāgata displays impressive psychic powers (iddhi) through the fire-kasina (tejo-kasiṇa), the Buddha delivers a gradual discourse (anupubbikathā). The entire crowd of eighty thousand attains the fruit of Stream-entry (Sotāpatti-phala). Deeply inspired, Soṇa requests and receives full monastic ordination (upasampadā).

The Disaster / Turning Point: Retiring to the forest dwelling Sītavana (Cool Grove), Venerable Soṇa exerts extreme, unrestrained energy (accāraddha-vīriya). He engages in continuous walking meditation (caṅkamana) to the extent that the soles of his delicate feet crack and bleed profusely, turning his walking path red like a cattle slaughterhouse. Despite this painful torture, he fails to reach Arahantship. Discouraged, he contemplates disrobing to live as a wealthy layman and perform acts of merit.

The Result / Resolution: Perceiving Soṇa's inner crisis, the Buddha teleports to Sītavana and instructs him using the famous Simile of the Lute (Vīṇūpamā): just as a lute's strings produce harmony only when tuned neither too tight nor too loose, energy must be balanced to yield spiritual attainment. Applying this lesson, Venerable Soṇa harmonizes his spiritual faculties (indriya-samatā), swiftly attains Arahantship, modestly declares his realization through Dhamma principles, refuses exclusive sandals out of contentment (appiccha), and is designated by the Buddha as the foremost disciple among those who arouse energy (āraddha-vīriya).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Superiority of Aroused Energy (Āraddha-vīriya)

Living even a single day with aroused energy is declared by the Buddha to be far more noble than living a hundred years in laziness (kusīta). The Dhamma and the path to the Deathless (Amata-Nibbāna) are explicitly reserved for those who exert active spiritual effort.

2. The Simile of the Lute (Vīṇūpamā) and Balancing Energy (Vīriya-samatā)

Over-exerted energy (accāraddha-vīriya) leads to mental restlessness (uddhacca) and physical exhaustion, whereas insufficient energy leads to sluggishness (līnatā) and laxity. Spiritual progress requires discovering a balanced, middle state (majjhimā paṭipadā) tailored to one's mental continuum.

3. Harmonization of the Five Spiritual Faculties (Indriya-samatā)

The internal faculties (indriya) required for liberation are:

Conviction (saddhā)

Energy (vīriya)

Mindfulness (sati)

Concentration (samādhi)

Wisdom (paññā)

These are dynamic mental powers that must be systematically cultivated and balanced rather than left to operate unevenly.

4. Pairings and Counter-Balancing of Faculties

Conviction (saddhā) vs. Wisdom (paññā): Conviction represents the emotional/affective aspect (the heart), while wisdom represents the cognitive/intellectual aspect. High conviction without wisdom breeds blind, foolish faith; high intellect without conviction breeds cunning and deceitfulness.

Energy (vīriya) vs. Concentration (samādhi): Energy tends toward restlessness (uddhacca), while concentration tends toward stillness/sluggishness (līnatā). They must balance each other to produce stable insight (vipassanā).

5. Mindfulness (Sati) as the Universal Balancer

Mindfulness sits precisely at the center of the spiritual faculties like the needle of a weighing balance. Grounded in the foundations of mindfulness (satipaṭṭhāna) and mindfulness directed to the body (kāyagatā-sati), sati monitors and adjusts the interplay between conviction, wisdom, energy, and concentration.

6. Refinement of Energy in Postural Concentration (Iriyāpatha-samādhi)

Analysing Venerable Ānanda's unique attainment of Arahantship outside the four standard postures (sitting, standing, walking, lying down), the Thero explains that Ānanda's eagerness to attend the First Buddhist Council had introduced slight restlessness (uddhacca). At the precise transitional point between sitting and lying down, his continuous kāyagatā-sati brought his energy to perfect balance, releasing his mind from all taints (āsava).

7. The Buddha's Triad vs. The Doctrine of Non-Action (Akiriya-vāda)

The Buddha defines His fundamental doctrine through three terms:

Kamma-vāda: Teaching the efficacy and moral reality of action.

Kiriya-vāda: Teaching that beings possess the internal power/potential to act.

Vīriya-vāda: Teaching that applying energy brings forth that potential.

The sermon strongly refutes Makkhali Gosāla’s non-action view (akiriya-vāda)—which claims effort is useless and denies karma—comparing it to a wretched human-hair blanket (kesa-kambala) and a destructive fishnet cast at a river mouth. Modern trends that dismiss spiritual exertion as "reinforcing ego (sakkāya-diṭṭhi)" are identified as dangerous resurgences of this heresy.

8. Three Tripartite Elements of Inner Energy

The Buddha outlines three internal modes of energy used to overcome sloth and torpor:

Ārambha-dhātu: The element of instigating effort (the power to begin an action).

Nikkhama-dhātu: The element of launching out (the power to sustain momentum).

Parakkama-dhātu: The element of exerting progress (the power to overcome obstacles and advance).

9. Psychological Contrast: Grounds for Laziness (Kusīta-vatthu) vs. Arousing Energy (Ārambha-vatthu)

The sermon details the 8 paired conditions (undertaking work, finishing work, embarking on a journey, returning from a journey, receiving little food, receiving ample food, slight illness, recovery from illness).

The indolent person (kusīta) uses every circumstance as a justification to sleep and indulge saṃsāra.

The energetic practitioner (āraddha-vīriya) uses those exact same conditions via wise attention (yoniso manasikāra) as an urgent incentive to practice meditation and escape saṃsāra.

10. The Supreme Fourfold Energy (Caturanga-samannāgata-vīriya)

Once energy is balanced within the Middle Path, the practitioner applies ultimate determination: "Let only skin, sinews, and bones remain; let flesh and blood dry up; whatever can be achieved by manly strength and effort will not be abandoned until reached." It is this relentless, balanced effort that culminates in the realization of the Deathless (Nibbāna).
