Sermon 107 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #107 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #107

Primary Theme: Achieving victory in life's battles through the path of Dhamma—conquering hatred, evil, stinginess, and untruth through loving-kindness (mettā), virtue (sīla), wise attention (yoniso manasikāra), and truthfulness (sacca) rather than retaliating with unrighteousness.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Uttarā (a stream-enterer / Sotāpanna lay-disciple and daughter of the financier Puṇṇa), Puṇṇa (her father), Sirimā (a courtesan of Rājagaha), Uttarā's husband (son of the non-Buddhist financier Sumana), and the Buddha.

The Interaction: Due to social pressure, Puṇṇa married Uttarā to the son of financier Sumana, a household of wrong views. Unable to perform alms-giving (dāna), hear the Dhamma, or see monks for two and a half months, Uttarā expressed deep sorrow to her father. Puṇṇa sent 15,000 kahāpaṇas so Uttarā could hire the courtesan Sirimā to attend to her husband for a fortnight, freeing Uttarā to offer daily alms to the Buddha and the Saṅgha.

The Disaster / Turning Point: On the final day of the fortnight, Uttarā's husband looked out the kitchen window, saw Uttarā covered in soot and sweat organizing alms, and smiled mockingly. Watching this, Sirimā became intensely jealous and angry. She scooped boiling hot ghee from a hearth ladle and poured it over Uttarā’s head.

The Result / Resolution: Uttarā suffused Sirimā with loving-kindness (mettā), reflecting on Sirimā’s favor in enabling her alms-offering, and made an assertion of truth (saccakiriyā) based on mettā-jhāna. The boiling ghee felt like cold water and left her unhurt. When her maidservants attacked Sirimā, Uttarā stopped them, washed Sirimā, applied oil, and comforted her. Moved by spiritual urgency (saṃvega), Sirimā asked for forgiveness. Guided by Uttarā, Sirimā offered alms to the Buddha the following day, repented before Him, listened to the Buddha utter Dhammapada verse 223, and attained stream-entry (Sotāpanna) along with her retinue.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Four Principles of Dhamma Victory (Dhammapada 223):

True victory is achieved not by unrighteousness ("destroying poison with poison"), but through four specific practices:

Conquer anger by non-anger (akkodhena jine kodhaṃ)

Conquer the bad/unvirtuous person through goodness/virtue (asādhuṃ sādhunā jine)

Conquer the stingy person through giving (jine kadariyaṃ dānena)

Conquer the liar through truth (saccena alikavādinaṃ)

2. Liberation of Mind Through Loving-Kindness (Mettā-cetovimutti):

Mettā-cetovimutti requires breaking down the limiting "fences" or barriers of anger, dear persons, neutral persons, and hostile persons, allowing unconditional well-wishing to radiate universally to all living beings without exception (niravasesa).

3. Validation of the Benefits of Loving-Kindness (Mettānisasa Sutta):

Among the eleven benefits of loving-kindness, the Sutta states: "Nāssa aggi vā visaṃ vā satthaṃ vā kamati" (fire, poison, or weapons do not harm the body of one who practices mettā-cetovimutti). Uttarā's immunity to boiling ghee directly demonstrated this principle.

4. Systematic Cultivation of Mettā:

To yield its full power, loving-kindness must be cultivated through eight continuous stages: associated with (āsevitāya), developed (bhāvitāya), practiced abundantly (bahulīkatāya), made into a vehicle (yānīkatāya), made a foundation/plot of land (vatthukatāya), established (anuṭṭhitāya), habituated (paricitāya), and well-undertaken (susamāraddhāya).

5. Universal Inclusiveness of Mettā (Karaṇīyametta Sutta):

Mettā must gather all living beings without exception (anavasesā):

Those who tremble/frail (tasā) and the firm/strong (thāvarā) [illustrated by the frog and the cobra, where both deserve goodwill].

Physical variations: long (dīghā), large (mahantā), medium (majjhimā), short (rassaka), minute (aṇuka), and gross (thūla).

Spatial/temporal aspects: seen (diṭṭhā) and unseen (addiṭṭhā), living far (dūre) or near (avidūre), born (bhūtā) or seeking birth (sambhavesī).

6. Wise Attention (Yoniso Manasikāra) & Right Intention (Sammā-saṅkappa):

Uttarā used wise attention to overcome hatred by focusing entirely on a single "hair" of virtue in Sirimā (her enabling of the alms-giving) to cover a "mountain" of fault. This aligns with Ven. Sāriputta's simile of lapping up water from a cow's footprint (gopade udakaṃ) in a desert to eliminate anger by searching out even the smallest virtue in an enemy.

7. Five Methods for Uprooting Resentment (Āghātapaṭivinaya Sutta):

Deep-seated resentment (āghāta) can be systematically removed using five sequential methods:

Developing loving-kindness (mettā)

Developing compassion (karuṇā)

Developing equanimity (upekkhā)

Non-mindfulness and non-attention (asati amanasikāro)—turning the mind away from the disturbing object

Reflecting on the ownership of kamma (kammassakatā)—realizing that everyone inherits the results of their own deeds.

8. The Power and Ancient Law of Truth (Sacca-pāramitā):

Truth is declared as "deathless speech" (Saccaṃ ve amatā vācā, esa dhammo sanantano). Truthfulness holds miraculous power for assertions of truth (saccakiriyā). It formed an unbroken perfection during the Bodhisatta's path and allowed the Buddha to immediately dispel the doubts of the group of five monks (pañcavaggiyā).

9. The Priority of Cultivating Virtue over Material Cultivations:

External wealth, crops, and estates can be destroyed instantly by a single matchstick struck with a mind of hatred. Material efforts become barren without the cultivation of virtue (sādhu-caryā). Uprooting inner defilements (kilesa) is essential for lasting peace.

10. The Role of Precepts (Sīla) and Meditation (Samādhi):

Precepts (5, 8, or 10) discipline physical and verbal actions to prevent catastrophic downfalls. Observing Uposatha precepts is described as having great luster and resonance (mahajjutiko mahāvipphāro). Because the mind is the forerunner (Manopubbaṅgamā dhammā), meditation (samādhi) anchors the restless mind onto wholesome objects to quell subtle mental defilements.

11. Transcending Worldly Conventions and Māra’s Illusions:

Society falsely encourages fighting evil with evil ("destroying poison with poison"), but the Buddha reveals that worldlings are spiritually blind (Andhabhūto ayaṃ loko) and mentally unstable (Sabbe puthujjanā ummattakā). Defilements present evil as good through the deception of Māra. Disciples must evaluate life through the law of kamma rather than worldly conventions.

12. Nibbāna as the Ultimate Beacon via the Noble Eightfold Path:

All mundane and supramundane practices must be oriented toward Nibbāna. By starting with Right View (sammā-diṭṭhi) and completing the Noble Eightfold Path, virtue, concentration, and wisdom (sīla, samādhi, paññā) mature to penetrate the Three Characteristics—Impermanence (anicca), Suffering (dukkha), and Not-self (anattā)—culminating in the realization of the Noble Paths, Fruits, and Nibbāna.
