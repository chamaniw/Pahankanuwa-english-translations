Sermon 075 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #75 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #75

Primary Theme: Cultivating strong renunciation (nekkhamma) and spiritual urgency (saṁvega) by contemplating the terrifying, insatiable, and endless nature of saṁsāra and the repulsiveness of the body; penetrating the deceptive illusion of consciousness (viññāṇa-māyā), Name-and-Form (nāmarūpa), and the six sense bases (saḷāyatana) to realize the Deathless (amata)—Nibbāna—in this very life.

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Princess Sumedhā (daughter of regional King Koñca of Mantāvatī), who later became Arahant Therī Sumedhā; her parents; and King Anīkadatta of Vāraṇāvatī.

The Interaction: Since childhood, Princess Sumedhā visited bhikkhunī monasteries with her attendants, heard the Dhamma, and developed deep faith and urgency regarding saṁsāra. Upon reaching youth, her parents arranged her marriage to King Anīkadatta of Vāraṇāvatī.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Refusing the proposal, Sumedhā declared her desire to go forth (pabbajjā) into monastic life, emphasizing that sensual pleasures are bitter, body is a bag of excrement, and saṁsāra is impermanent. When her parents insisted, she threatened to starve herself, cut off her hair, locked herself in her chambers, and entered the first absorption (jhāna) while contemplating her cut hair. When King Anīkadatta arrived to persuade her with royal luxury, Sumedhā delivered a profound Dhamma discourse detailing the ten similes for sensual pleasures (kāma) and the terrifying, endless cycles of suffering in saṁsāra (Anamatagga Saṁyutta). She capped her discourse with the pivotal verse (Verse 515 of Therīgāthā), asserting that the Deathless (amata) can be attained even today by those who strive with wise attention (yoniso).

The Result / Resolution: Deeply moved and transformed by her wisdom, King Anīkadatta himself implored her parents to grant her permission to ordain. Sumedhā went forth, practiced insight meditation (vipassanā), attained full Arahantship, and ultimately left behind the longest single verse group in the Therīgāthā as a testament to liberation.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. The Power of Wise Effort and the Attainability of the Deathless (Amata)

Drawing from Sumedhātherīgāthā (Verse 515), the sermon stresses that Nibbāna—the Deathless—is not an unattainable relic of the past nor a mythical realm. It is directly realizable today (ajja pi ca labhanīyamidaṃ) by anyone who applies wise attention (yoniso payuñjati) and energetic effort (ghaṭamāna).

2. The Deceptive and Unsatisfiable Nature of Sensual Pleasures (Kāma-ādīnava)

Sumedhā highlights the ten classic canonical similes for sensual desire: a fleshless bone (aṭṭhikaṅkala), a pit of glowing embers (aṅgārakāsu), a burning grass torch (tiṇukkā), a piece of meat pursued by birds of prey (maṁsapesi), borrowed goods (yācitaka), a transient dream (supinaka), a tree fruit (rukkha-phala) that brings down the climber, and a serpent’s head (sappasira). Even universal monarchs like King Mandhātā die completely unsatisfied; sensual desire is an insatiable thirst (taṇhā) akin to chasing a mirage.

3. Reframing the Truth of Suffering (Dukkha-Sacca) Beyond Superficial Views

The sermon critiques modern, simplistic definitions of suffering that reduce dukkha to daily anxieties or minor stress. True dukkha-sacca is far deeper: it is the terrifying, endless repetition of birth (dukkhā jāti punappunaṁ), aging (jarā), sickness (vyādhi), and death (maraṇa) across countless realms of becoming (bhava).

4. The Incalculable Duration and Horrors of Saṁsāra (Anamatagga Saṁyutta)

By citing the Anamatagga Saṁyutta, the sermon details how each being has shed tears, drunk mother's milk, and shed blood (when slaughtered as animals or executed) in quantities greater than the waters of the four great oceans. The soil of the earth formatted into pellets or the vegetation of Jambudīpa cut into pieces would be exhausted before one could finish counting one's past mothers and fathers.

5. Utilizing the Rare Wealth of the Opportune Moment (Khaṇa-Sampatti)

Human birth during the survival of a Buddha's Dispensation is an extremely rare occurrence (khaṇa-sampatti). Failing to practice during this brief window risks falling into the eight inopportune conditions (aṭṭha akkhaṇā), such as the hells or animal realms, where monastic ordination and Dhamma practice are impossible.

6. The Four Distortions (Vipallāsa) as the Engine of Illusion

Deluded worldlings (puthujjana) are spiritually "blind," caught in four cognitive inversions:

Taking the impermanent to be permanent (anicce nicca-saññā)

Taking the repulsive/unattractive to be attractive (asubhe subha-saññā)

Taking suffering to be pleasure (dukkhe sukha-saññā)

Taking non-self to be a self (anattani atta-saññā)

Insight meditation (vipassanā) systematically dismantles these distortions.

7. The Deceptive Illusion of Consciousness (Viññāṇa-Māyā) and Name-and-Form (Nāmarūpa)

Consciousness (viññāṇa) operates like a magician (māyākāra), generating an illusion of substantial reality by projecting mental factors (nāma: feeling, perception, volition, contact, attention) and physical form (rūpa: the four great elements—paṭhavī, āpo, tejo, vāyo). Worldly continuity arises because we "glue" these rapidly flickering mental images together with craving (taṇhā), much like watching a moving film in a dark cinema.

8. The Trap of the Six Sense Bases (Saḷāyatana)

Citing the canonical verse (Chassu loko samuppanno...), the sermon explains that the entire "world" (loko) arises, seeks companionship, gets bound, and becomes weary within the six sense bases (eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind). True liberation is the cessation of sense-grafting/concept-building (saḷāyatana-nirodha), wherein the world is directly seen as empty (loko suñño).

9. Uprooting the Conceit "I Am" (Asmi-Māna) and the House-Builder

The primary peg of existence is the subtle conceit "I am" (asmi-māna). In the inspired utterance (Udāna) "Aneka jāti saṁsāraṁ...", the Buddha identifies consciousness driven by craving as the "house-builder" (gahakāraka). When the light of wisdom (paññā) arises, the ridgepole of ignorance is shattered, preparations (saṅkhāra) cease, and the mind attains the unconditioned/unprepared (visaṅkhāra).

10. Nibbāna as Extinguishment Without Grasping (Anupādā-Parinibbāna) in This Very Life

Nibbāna is not a remote geographic location, a novel realm, or a mystical beverage. It is the destruction of craving (taṇhakkhaya), dispassion (virāga), and the immediate peace of letting go of all fuel/grasping (anupādā-parinibbāna). It is a direct experience of "dying without dying"—eradicating the fear of death by eliminating the false sense of self ("mine") that is subject to death.
