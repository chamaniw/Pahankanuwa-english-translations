Sermon 143 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #143 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #143

Primary Theme: Transcending saṃsāric burning (pariḷāha) and existence (bhava) through spiritual urgency (saṃvega), insight into the three characteristics (tilakkaṇa), and the supramundane path of virtue, concentration, and wisdom (sīla, samādhi, paññā).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: The Lord Buddha, Elder Devadatta, Physician Jīvaka, Venerable Ānanda, and Prince Ajātasattu.

The Interaction: After gaining supernormal powers (abhiññā), Devadatta grew jealous of the Buddha’s honor, won over Prince Ajātasattu with miracles, and demanded control of the Sangha. When the Buddha refused, Devadatta developed intense hatred and conspired to assassinate Him.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Devadatta rolled a boulder down Mount Gijjhakūṭa (Vulture Peak) to kill the Buddha. Intercepted by rock peaks, a splinter nevertheless struck the Buddha's foot, causing severe bleeding and intense pain (vedanā). Escorted to Jīvaka’s Mango Grove, physician Jīvaka applied a powerful medicine and bandage, but was subsequently locked inside the city gates overnight. Jīvaka panicked, worrying that the potent medicine would cause the Buddha unbearable burning sensations (pariḷāha).

The Result / Resolution: Perceiving Jīvaka's anxiety, the Buddha instructed Ven. Ānanda to remove the bandage, which instantly healed the wound. When Jīvaka returned at dawn and inquired about burning sensations (pariḷāha), the Buddha uttered Dhammapada Verse 90, declaring that while physical sensations occur, no mental burning (pariḷāha), distress, or sorrow exists for an Arahant who has finished the saṃsāric journey (gataddhi).

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Distinction Between Physical Feelings and Mental Burning (Pariḷāha)

Physical pain (vedanā) affects even an Arahant or a Buddha due to the nature of the physical body. However, mental burning (pariḷāha)—distress, sorrow, and agitation—is entirely absent in an Arahant who abides in the fruit of Arahantship concentration (arahattaphala-samādhi), remaining equanimous and free from attraction and aversion.

2. The Thematic Unity of Vesak

The three events commemorated on Vesak—the Buddha’s final birth, Enlightenment, and final passing away (Parinibbāna)—are three stages of a single overarching event: the complete realization and termination of the extremely long journey through Saṃsāra (gataddhi).

3. The Danger and Perpetuation of Hatred (Dosa)

Devadatta’s animosity began eons prior as the merchant Serivānija against the Bodhisatta (Kacchapuṭa) over a golden bowl. This saṃsāric habit of hatred continued to burn and scorch him across lifetimes, proving that hatred is never appeased by hatred, but only by equanimity and neutrality (upekkhā).

4. The Three Fires Driven by Craving (Taṇhā)

Saṃsāric existence is a state of continuous mental burning caused by the thirst of craving (taṇhā ponobhavikā). Craving manifests as three distinct internal fires: lust (rāga), hatred (dosa), and delusion (moha). Attempting to quench these fires through sensual gratification is like throwing straw onto a fire or running after a mirage.

5. Cultivating Spiritual Urgency (Saṃvega) via the *Anamatagga Suttas*

To break attachment to existence (bhava-taṇhā), one must reflect on the unimaginable length of Saṃsāra using the Buddha's similes:

Endless Mothers: Twigs cut into 4-inch pieces across Jambudīpa run out before the count of one's past mothers ends.

Ocean of Tears & Milk: The tears shed over loss and the mother's milk consumed in past births exceed the water in the four great oceans.

Duration of an Eon (Kalpa): A solid rock mountain strok’d once a century with a fine Kāsī cloth would wear away before a single eon ends.

Mountain of Bones: The skeletons left by a single person in one eon would rival Mount Vepulla.

River of Blood: The blood shed by being decapitated as animals or executed as criminals far exceeds the four great oceans.

6. Overcoming the Role of 'Cemetery-Extenders' (Kaṭasivaḍḍhitā)

Without Dhamma practice, beings merely act as "graveyard-extenders" (kaṭasivaḍḍhitā), continually accumulating deaths and expanding cemeteries. The Buddha-Sāsana does not exist to expand or find satisfaction in the world (loka-vaḍḍhana), but to guide practitioners toward supramundane liberation (lokuttara-vimutti).

7. Abandonment of the Four Knots (Gantha)

Complete spiritual freedom (sabbaganthappahīna) requires severing the four mental knots: covetousness (abhijjhā), ill-will (vyāpāda), clinging to rules and vows (sīlabbata-parāmāsa), and dogmatic insistence that one's own view alone is true (idaṃsaccābhinivesa).

8. Refutation of Modernist Dilutions of Kamma and Rebirth

The Buddha rejects secular arguments that reduce hells, heaven realms, and hungry ghosts (petas) to mere psychological states or economic metaphors. Invisible to ordinary eyes, these realms are directly witnessed through the divine eye (dibba-cakkhu). Denying their existence simply because they are unseen is compared to a blind man claiming the sun and moon do not exist.

9. The Gradual Path of Sīla, Samādhi, and Paññā

Virtue (Sīla): Rooted in volition (cetanā) and mental factors (cetasika), pure virtue provides a protective framework that prevents past remorse from intruding into practice.

Concentration (Samādhi): Gathers the scattered mind onto a single wholesome meditation object (kammaṭṭhāna), providing a refined bliss (samādhi-sukha) far superior to the five cords of sensual pleasure (pañca-kāma-sampatti).

Wisdom (Paññā): Develops the eye of wisdom (paññā-cakkhu) through insight (vipassanā).

10. Piercing the Perception of Compactness (Ghana-Saññā)

Delusion (moha) persists because ignorance (avijjā) veils reality behind the continuous perception of compactness (ghana-saññā). Through wise attention (yoniso manasikāra), one contemplates the three characteristics (tilakkaṇa)—impermanence (anicca), suffering (dukkha), and non-self (anattā)—to see things as they truly are (yathābhūta-ñāṇa), evoking natural disillusionment (nibbidā) and leading to Nibbāna.
