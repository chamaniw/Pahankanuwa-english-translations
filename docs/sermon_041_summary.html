Sermon 041 Summary

Pahankanuwa Sermon #41 Summary

1. Overview

Sermon Title / Number: Pahankanuwa Sermon #41 (Audio No. 155)

Primary Theme: The definition of the "world" (loka) in the Noble Discipline (ariyassa vinaye) as the six sense bases (saḷāyatana), and the direct realization of Nibbāna through the cessation of the six sense bases (saḷāyatana-nirodha) by breaking the "eggshell of ignorance" via the cultivation of the Three Characteristics (tilakkhaṇa).

2. Background Story (Nidāna-Kathā)

The Protagonist: Two Yakkha generals, Sātāgira (residing on Mt. Sāta in the Middle Country of India) and Hemavata (residing on Mt. Hemavata in the Himalayas). In a past life during the era of Kassapa Buddha, both were ordained bhikkhus.

The Interaction: As monks, they committed a disciplinary (vinaya) error due to partiality when settling a dispute. Owing to the resulting remorse, they were reborn as long-lived Yakkha generals instead of celestial beings. Remembering their past friendship, they agreed to meet annually and notify one another if any miraculous event occurred in their respective regions.

The Disaster / Turning Point: Sātāgira attended the preaching of the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta by Gautama Buddha but was unable to concentrate his mind. Seeking his friend to share the news, he flew into the sky just as Hemavata—having witnessed cosmic miracles in the Himalayas—was flying toward the Middle Country. The two met in the sky above Rājagaha. After Sātāgira praised the Buddha’s virtues (such as being endowed with tādī, free from unwholesome deeds, and taintless), they approached the Blessed One together. Hemavata posed the pivotal metaphysical question: "Where is the world born? Where does it maintain its intimacy? Grasping what does it get vexed, and where is it vexed?"

The Result / Resolution: The Buddha answered that the world is born, finds association, grasps, and suffers vexation within the six sense bases (saḷāyatana), and that release comes from eliminating desire (chanda) and craving (taṇhā) for them. Delighted and spiritually transformed by this teaching, the two Yakkha generals expressed profound joy and pledged to travel from mountain to mountain and village to village proclaiming the virtues of the Buddha and the Dhamma.

3. Core Dhamma Points Emphasized

1. Definition of the World in the Noble Discipline (Ariyassa Vinaye Loka)

In contrast to conventional or scientific worldviews, the Buddha defines the "world" (loka) strictly in terms of the six internal and external sense bases (saḷāyatana): the eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind, along with their corresponding objects and associated consciousnesses (viññāṇa).

2. The Etymology of the World as "Subject to Crumbling" (Paloka-Dhammaṃ)

Citing the discourse to Ven. Ānanda, the sermon highlights that the word loka is derived from lujjati / palujjati (to crumble or disintegrate). Whatever is subject to crumbling—the sense organs, sense objects, sense-contact (phassa), and feelings (vedanā)—is designated as "the world" in the Noble discipline.

3. Breaking the Eggshell of Ignorance (Avijjā-Aṇḍakosa)

Drawing from the Verañja Sutta, the Buddha presents Himself as the eldest chick who broke through the eggshell of ignorance at the moment of Supreme Enlightenment (sambodhi). Trapped inside this sixfold sense sphere, worldly beings remain inside the shell; transcending the six sense bases constitutes lokuttara (transcending the world).

4. The Fathom-Long Carcass and Point of View (Rohitassa Sutta & Asmi-Māna)

The Rohitassa Sutta establishes that within this very fathom-long living body, endowed with perception and mind, lies the world, its arising, its cessation, and the path to its cessation. The world arises relative to a point of view—specifically anchored in the conceit "I am" (asmi-māna).

5. Limitations of Modern Empirical Science

Scientific instruments (telescopes, microscopes) are merely technological extensions of the six sense bases (saḷāyatana). Because scientific inquiry, such as modern quantum physics, continuously seeks an objective world independent of a point of view, it remains trapped inside the eggshell of ignorance, unable to complete its search.

6. The Impossibility of Reaching the End of the World by Traveling

The Buddha declared a apparent riddle: the end of the world cannot be reached by physical travel, yet without reaching the end of the world, there is no end to Dukkha. Ven. Ānanda clarified that "that by which one has a perception of the world (loka-saññā) and a conceit of the world (loka-māna)"—the six sense bases—is the world itself. Reaching its cessation (nirodha) is reaching the end of the world.

7. The Three Characteristics (Tilakkhaṇa) and the Three Liberations (Vimokkha)

Insight (vipassanā) directed at the six sense bases yields three specific gateways to liberation:

Contemplating impermanence (anicca) dismantles signs/images, leading to Signless Liberation (animitta-vimokkha).

Contemplating suffering (dukkha) removes expectations/aspirations, leading to Desireless Liberation (appaṇihita-vimokkha).

Contemplating non-self (anattā) removes the illusion of ownership, leading to Void Liberation (suññatā-vimokkha).

8. The Deceptive Nature of Consciousness (Viññāṇa) Between Name-and-Form (Nāmarūpa)

Consciousness (viññāṇa) stands between nāma (feeling, perception, volition, contact, attention) and rūpa (the four great elements). Ven. Nanananda uses the similes of the dog on the plank bridge (reacting to its own reflection in the water) and Narcissus to demonstrate how consciousness deludes beings into projecting a solid "self" or external "substance" onto mere mental representations and sensory contact.

9. Simile of the Dog Bound to a Post (Sakkāya-Diṭṭhi)

An uninstructed worldly person bound by the dogma of self-illusion (sakkāya-diṭṭhi) is like a dog chained to a post: whether walking, standing, sitting, or lying down, it cannot escape the immediate vicinity of the post. Similarly, one bound to self-illusion carries the five aggregates of grasping (pañc'upādānakkhandha) everywhere.

10. Radical Reflection (Yoniso Manasikāra) and Scrutinizing (Sammasana) Fabrications

Fabrications (saṅkhāra) are compared to a banana trunk that lacks heartwood. Instead of grasping them with unwise attention (ayoniso manasikāra), the insight meditator engages in gentle touch/scrutiny (sammasana) and noting (salakkhaṇa). Peeling back the layers of fabrications reveals no core, producing knowledge of things as they truly are (yathābhūta-ñāṇa) and disenchantment (nibbidā).

11. Cessation of the Six Sense Bases (Saḷāyatana-Nirodha) as Realization in This Life (Diṭṭheva Dhamme)

Nibbāna is not a post-mortem relocation to a distant realm, but the direct experience of the cessation of the six sense bases in this very life (diṭṭheva dhamme). During the attainment of the fruit of Arahantship (arahatta-phala-samāpatti), the Buddha and Arahants temporarily step out of the sphere of suffering, dwelling in Nibbāna as an island, refuge, and shelter.

12. Equating Becoming-Cessation with Suffering-Cessation

Worldly beings often desire release from suffering (dukkha-nirodha) while attempting to retain becoming (bhava). Ven. Nanananda emphasizes the Buddha's precise equation: the cessation of becoming (bhava-nirodha) is identically the cessation of suffering (dukkha-nirodha), which requires the total destruction of craving (taṇhā) and ignorance (avijjā).
